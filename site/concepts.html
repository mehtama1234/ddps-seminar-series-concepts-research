Physics-Informed Learning

A computer learning purely from data can accidentally find a guess that looks right on your measurements but breaks the laws of physics. This is especially bad when you only have a few examples, or when you need the guess to work for new situations the program has never seen before.

data-driven physical simulation

Reduced-Order Modeling

Real simulations that predict physical behavior often need to solve thousands of equations at millions of points. Running these over and over—to test different designs, to find the best parameters, or to make decisions in real time—takes hours or days per run. In design, manufacturing, medicine, or robotics, waiting that long is not practical.

data-driven physical simulation

Differentiable Simulation

In science and engineering, you often don't just need to predict what happens given some input — you need to find what input would produce a desired result. For example: what shape of wing produces the most lift, or what sequence of moves makes a robot grasp an object most reliably.

data-driven physical simulation

Operator Learning

In the real world, engineers don't solve one physics problem and move on. They solve families of related problems: the same differential equation (a rule that says how something changes from moment to moment or place to place) but with different starting conditions, different shapes, different material properties, or different boundary conditions (the fixed facts at the edges and at the start). Brute-force simulators have to run all the way through each time.

data-driven physical simulation

Fluid Mechanics Simulation

Real fluids move in ways that are impossible to predict by hand — they change shape constantly, interact with themselves in intricate ways, create instabilities that spiral into chaos, and take enormous compute time to simulate step by step.

data-driven physical simulation

Hybrid Twins

A pure simulator built from equations may miss forces or details that show up in the real world but were never written into the math. A pure data model—a learning program trained only on past examples—may give completely wrong answers once conditions change outside the range it saw during training.

data-driven physical simulation

Scientific Machine Learning

Real-world science and engineering problems are stubborn: you have only a handful of expensive experimental measurements or runs (not millions of examples), you know some laws of physics that must hold, your simulators are slow and cost money to run, and making a bad prediction can lead to a broken airplane wing or a failed drug candidate — so guessing is not an option.

data-driven physical simulation

Uncertainty And Robustness

A surrogate model (a program trained to mimic a simulator) might get the average answer right but collapse exactly where decisions matter most—where scientists or engineers stake their conclusions.

data-driven physical simulation

Inverse Problems And Control

Every day, engineers and scientists face two recurring headaches. First: you measure something (a temperature reading, a video of water flowing, sensor data from a bridge) but you don't know what actually caused it—you have to work backward to guess. Second: you want a system to behave a certain way (a drone to hover steady, a chemical process to make the right product) and you have to figure out what to do to make it happen. Both need you to reverse-engineer the system or pick the right lever to pull.

data-driven physical simulation
