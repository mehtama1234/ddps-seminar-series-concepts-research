Physics-Informed Learning

This whole series is about teaching computers to predict how physical things behave — how air pushes over a wing, how heat spreads through metal, how structures flex — by learning from examples instead of only writing down step-by-step math by hand. Physics-informed learning adds a new ingredient: as the computer learns, you also tell it the laws of physics you already know (conservation of energy, momentum, mass). The computer then finds a guess that both matches the examples AND obeys those laws.

Ordinary Problem

A computer learning purely from data can accidentally find a guess that looks right on your measurements but breaks the laws of physics. This is especially bad when you only have a few examples, or when you need the guess to work for new situations the program has never seen before.

Naive Picture

The simple hope is that if you gather enough examples of how a physical system behaves, a learning program can just memorize those patterns and act like a simulator — a program that steps a physical situation forward to predict what happens next — without needing to know the actual physics rules at all.

Why It Fails

Real physics problems tangle together the governing equations (rules that say how things change moment to moment or place to place), the data you measured, how finely you divided space or time, whether small wobbles grow or shrink, and whether the answer makes sense scientifically. A learning program trained only on examples can look correct on your test data but fail when you change the setup slightly, run it for much longer, or ask it to obey a conservation law that was never written down in your examples.

First-Principles Move

If you already know a physical law — the rule for how heat spreads, or how air pressure changes, or how energy must stay constant — then the computer should search only among guesses that respect that law. Instead of treating every data point as a separate instruction with no constraint, you add a penalty: if a guess breaks the physics rule even slightly, the learning program punishes it and tries something else. This way, the computer respects both the examples and the known laws at the same time.

What Breaks Without It

Without this idea, you can build a learning program that scores well on examples you already have but falls apart when you ask it to predict something new — a different setup, a longer time, a pushed parameter. The program learned to copy patterns in the data without actually learning the deeper rule, so it has no way to generalize safely.

Summit Role

This topic ties together many of our seminars: it asks when and how you can combine learning from data with the math and laws you already know. It helps us ask the central question of this whole series — can learning programs predict physics faster and cheaper than hand-written rules, and when do they fail — more clearly.
