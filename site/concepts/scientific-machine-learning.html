data-driven physical simulation

Scientific Machine Learning

This series is about getting computers to predict how physical systems behave — how heat spreads, how structures bend, how air flows — by training a learning program on real measurements and simulation data, instead of (or alongside) hand-written equations. The program tunes millions of little dials by trial and error until its predictions match reality, then we use those predictions to run what-if scenarios, control the system, or fill in missing information.

Ordinary Problem

Real-world science and engineering problems are stubborn: you have only a handful of expensive experimental measurements or runs (not millions of examples), you know some laws of physics that must hold, your simulators are slow and cost money to run, and making a bad prediction can lead to a broken airplane wing or a failed drug candidate — so guessing is not an option.

Naive Picture

A naive hope is that once you collect enough data, a program trained on examples can just replace your entire simulator. Feed in the current conditions, read off the next state; done.

Why It Fails

Physical systems are governed by equations that describe how things change moment by moment and place by place. A generic learning program knows nothing about these rules, symmetries, or the fundamental conservation laws (energy, momentum) that the real system must obey. It can memorize patterns in your data perfectly but still fail wildly on new starting conditions, longer time steps, or regimes you never trained on. It might predict that energy appears or disappears, that momentum is not conserved, or that stability breaks in ways the real physics forbids. A score showing 'accurate on test data' hides these failures until you run it on a real system and something breaks.

First-Principles Move

Instead of letting the learning program invent its own rules, you design the learning problem around what you know: encode the physics equations and symmetries directly into how the program learns, inject uncertainty quantification so you know when to trust or distrust predictions, add the measurements you have as constraints, and structure the problem so the program learns to respect conservation laws and physical stability, not just match example data.

What Breaks Without It

If you ignore this structure and just hand a generic learning program your data, you build a model that looks good on the examples you have but fails when: the system operates at a different scale or with different parameters; time horizons are longer than your training data; measurements are sparse or noisy; or real-world conditions differ slightly from your lab setup. The model breaks because it never learned to respect the underlying physics, only to copy patterns it saw.

Summit Role

This theme ties the whole seminar series together: every talk you will hear is exploring how and when learning from data can predict physical behavior when the exact rules are unknown or too expensive to compute. This one defines the core principle: marry what you know (the physics structure) with what you learn (the data patterns).

Worked Example

Predicting protein folding energy given an amino-acid sequence and 3D coordinates: the energy is a sum of physics terms (electrostatic repulsion, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic effect) plus unknown non-bonded interactions. Traditional all-atom molecular dynamics costs 100 hours per fold. Scientists collect 10,000 experimentally validated (sequence, structure, energy) triples. A pure neural network trained on these inputs predicts energy with 95% validation R², but when tested on a novel protein, it predicts an energy 100 kJ/mol below the true value, violating known bounds and suggesting the model learned non-physical artifacts. A scientific ML model embeds energy conservation: it outputs energy_nn + E_electrostatic(structure, charges) + E_vdw(structure, radii), where the second and third terms are hand-computed from known physics. The hybrid output respects bounds and reduces training error by 5× because the network only learns the remaining ~5% residual, not the 95% that physics already explains.

One Level Deeper

Scientific machine learning adds physics structure to the loss function. Instead of L = ||y_true - f_nn(x)||², the loss becomes L = ||y_true - f_nn(x) - f_physics(x)||² or L = ||residual_data||² + λ_physics·||PDE_residual||² where PDE_residual = ∂u/∂t - N[u]. The loss is a multi-task objective: minimize data misfit AND minimize the residual of known conservation laws. For inference, uncertainty quantification via Bayesian approaches (e.g., Laplace approximation, ensemble methods) estimates confidence: q_uncertain = Var[f_nn(x) | data], allowing the practitioner to know when predictions are guesses. A critical trick is to use known symmetries as inductive biases: if energy should be translation-invariant, the network architecture is built to respect that (e.g., permutation-equivariant layers for molecular systems), so the symmetry is implicit rather than enforced by loss terms, which saves training data and improves generalization.

Failure Boundary

Scientific ML fails when the practitioner encodes the wrong physics. If conservation of energy is imposed as a hard constraint but the true system dissipates (e.g., friction), the learned model will compensate by learning unphysical dissipation channels, and it diverges when dissipation changes. Sparse data—only 100 examples instead of 10,000—magnifies overfitting: the network learns noise correlations that masquerade as physics. When the true phenomenon involves rare events (e.g., a 1-in-1000-scenario phase transition), even 10,000 examples may miss it; the model confidently predicts the wrong behavior on that rare event because it has no signal to learn it. If the discretization (mesh size, timestep) affects the true dynamics but is not captured in the physics terms, the model fits one mesh scale perfectly but fails on another. Extrapolation beyond the training parameter regime is unreliable: a model trained on Reynolds numbers 100–1000 may hallucinate turbulence structure at Re=10,000. Domain adaptation is also fragile—a model trained on synthetic data (simulations) and applied to real data encounters distribution shift: unmodeled friction, sensor noise, or material heterogeneity that the synthetic data never saw.
