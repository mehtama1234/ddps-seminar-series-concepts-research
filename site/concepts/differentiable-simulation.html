data-driven physical simulation

Differentiable Simulation

A way to run a simulator (a program that steps a physical situation forward in tiny increments to predict what happens next) and trace backwards through every choice it made, so you can see exactly how to tweak each input or design choice to get a better result. Like rewinding a video and nudging each decision along the way.

Ordinary Problem

In science and engineering, you often don't just need to predict what happens given some input — you need to find what input would produce a desired result. For example: what shape of wing produces the most lift, or what sequence of moves makes a robot grasp an object most reliably.

Naive Picture

A simpler idea: use examples of what a simulator does, feed them to a learning program (a network with millions of little dials it adjusts by trial and error until its answers match the examples), and let the network replace the simulator. Once you have enough examples, the thinking goes, the network should work just as well but much faster.

Why It Fails

Real physical systems are tangled: equations describe behavior, but you also have measurement data, choices about how finely to divide space and time, uncertainties, and rules about what counts as physically sensible. A learning program trained only to match past examples can hide serious mistakes — it might look right on your training data but completely fail when you push it beyond what it has seen before, or when physical constraints get tested.

First-Principles Move

A simulator is a chain of steps: inputs go in, the laws of physics get applied piece by piece, and outputs come out. If you keep track of how each step depends on the last, you can work backwards from the final result to see exactly how much each input influenced it. That backward trace tells you which direction to nudge each input to improve the result.

What Breaks Without It

Without this tracing ability, you end up building models that perform well on test examples but warp and fail in ways that don't make physical sense when you try them on new conditions, over longer time horizons, or when you push the system beyond its training range. You can't steer the simulator toward better designs because you have no signal saying which direction to move.

Summit Role

This talk sits in the series about getting computers to learn physics from data instead of hand-written rules. Differentiable simulation is one of the main routes for doing that learning in a way that respects physical laws and scales to real engineering problems. Knowing this concept helps you understand why some learning approaches work and others hit walls.

Worked Example

A wing design problem: given an airfoil shape parameterized by control points, you want to maximize lift-to-drag ratio at Mach 0.7. A differentiable simulator builds a forward model: shape → mesh → run CFD solver → extract forces. Each step (mesh generation, flux computation, time integration) is differentiable, so ∂(drag)/∂(shape) can be computed via reverse-mode AD, yielding gradients for all 50 design parameters in one backward pass. Classical optimization would require 50+ separate simulator runs (each costing 4 hours on HPC). Differentiable simulation computes gradients in 2× the forward cost. A gradient descent loop finds the improved geometry in ~10 iterations (40 hours versus 200+ hours). The key: the simulator is 'unrolled' as a computational graph, and backprop traces through all physics decisions.

One Level Deeper

Differentiable simulation treats a time-stepping solver as a composition of differentiable operations: x_{n+1} = x_n + dt · f(x_n, θ), where θ are design parameters (shape, material properties). The loss L = Σ c_i(x_i) sums costs at each timestep or the final state. Backpropagation computes ∂L/∂θ by the chain rule: ∂L/∂θ = (∂L/∂x_final) · (∂x_N/∂x_{N-1}) · ... · (∂x_1/∂x_0) · (∂x_0/∂θ). Each Jacobian ∂x_i/∂x_{i-1} encodes the sensitivity of one step to the prior state, and all steps are 'unrolled' into a single computational graph. During backprop, every intermediate variable's gradient is stored (memory cost ~O(T) for T timesteps), or recomputed on-the-fly (checkpointing, memory O(√T)). The adjoint method gives the same result more efficiently for a single loss, avoiding explicit Jacobian storage.

Failure Boundary

Differentiable simulation fails for ill-posed or chaotic systems: turbulent flows (Lyapunov exponent > 0) are sensitive to infinitesimal perturbations, so gradients computed through 100+ timesteps grow unboundedly or vanish, making descent directions unreliable. Discretization effects (time step size, flux limiter choice) introduce non-smoothness; a limiter that switches between schemes at a threshold discontinuously breaks differentiability. Shocks and sharp gradients demand careful treatment—standard finite-difference AD gives large errors when step size is too large or too small (condition number explosion). Long rollouts (> 10 Lyapunov times) make the gradient signal vanish exponentially in network-based surrogate models due to spectral bias. Also, the adjoint state must be integrated backward in time through all storage from the forward pass, demanding huge GPU memory; numerical drift in the backward integration can corrupt gradient accuracy even if the forward solve was accurate.
