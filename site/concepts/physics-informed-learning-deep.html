Physics-informed learning — solving an equation with no answers to learn from

Machine learning usually needs a mountain of examples: show a program thousands of right answers and it learns to copy them. But what if you have no answers at all — only the law the answer must obey? Physics-informed learning does exactly that. It trains a program to satisfy a physical equation directly, using the equation itself as the only teacher. Here is the idea, a real run where a small program learns a curve it was never shown, why it works, where it stumbles, and how the trick spread across scientific computing.

1 · The problem — no examples to copy

Most machine learning is imitation. You gather a pile of solved cases — pictures already labelled, sentences already translated — and a network (a program with millions of small dials it tunes by trial and error until its answers match) learns to reproduce them. But in physics you often have the opposite situation: you know the rule the answer must follow — how heat spreads, how a beam bends, how a fluid pushes — yet you have no worked-out answers to hand the program. Generating them means running a slow, expensive simulation, which is the very cost you were trying to avoid.

So the question is sharp: can a program learn the answer to a physical problem when nobody can show it a single correct answer to copy?

2 · The reframe — grade the program against the law, not against answers

The move is this. A physical law is usually a differential equation — a rule that ties a quantity to how fast it changes from point to point (how steeply the temperature rises, how sharply the beam curves). The rule holds at every point. So instead of comparing the program's output to known answers, feed the program's own guess back into the equation and measure how badly it breaks the rule. That mismatch — call it the physics error — becomes the score to drive down. Add one more demand: the answer must match the fixed values at the edges (a bar clamped at both ends, a temperature held at the boundary). Now the program has a teacher after all — the law itself, checked at a scatter of points — and needs no solved examples. A program trained this way is a physics-informed neural network, or PINN.

The hidden quantity here is the residual — how far the network's own guess is from satisfying the equation when you put it back in. It is zero wherever the guess obeys the law and grows wherever it breaks it, and driving it toward zero everywhere is the whole training goal.

3 · Seen on a real run — a curve learned from the rule alone

We set a small program loose on a differential equation whose true answer is a smooth arch — the curve that rises and falls like a single hill across the span from 0 to 1. Crucially, the program was never shown that curve. Its only feedback was: plug yourself into the equation, and match the two pinned ends. We measured the largest gap between the program's answer and the true arch, before and after training.

The discovered fact: the largest error fell from 0.911 to 0.0023 — the program reconstructed the entire curve without ever seeing a single point of it. All it had was the equation, sampled at random points, plus the two fixed ends. The law carried all the information the examples would have.

4 · Why the equation is enough

A single solved example tells the program one thing: the answer here is this. A differential equation tells it something far stronger — a relationship that must hold at every point at once, plus the fixed values at the edges pin down which of the many curves obeying the rule is the right one. Sampling the equation at a few hundred scattered points hands the program a dense web of constraints that together squeeze its guess toward the one curve satisfying all of them. That is why no answer key is needed: the rule plus the boundary is already a complete description of the answer.

5 · The one real weakness — the training can quietly fail

The catch is that this training is genuinely hard to balance. The program is chasing two goals at once — obey the equation everywhere and hit the edge values — and the two can pull against each other, so the process can stall in a bad compromise that looks converged but is wrong. Sharp features (a sudden jump, a thin fast-moving front, a stiff fast-and-slow mix) make it worse: the physics error can look small while the real answer is badly off. Getting PINNs to train reliably on hard equations is an open, actively-studied problem — one of this seminar series' own deep dives is titled, plainly, why PINNs fail to train.

6 · The surprise — the trick became a whole toolkit

Turning a governing law into a training score turned out to be a master key. The same idea powers inverse problems — if the equation grades the fit, you can also hunt for an unknown ingredient in the equation (a hidden material property, a missing source) by making it another dial to tune. It blends with data: where you happen to have a few real measurements, you add them as extra terms and the physics fills in everywhere else. And it connects to operator learning and hybrid twins, where physics is baked into the model rather than learned from scratch. The lesson that spread across scientific machine learning: you do not always need data — sometimes you already know the law, and the law is enough to learn from.

7 · Where it is used in practice — Learning from the law, not data

Physics-informed learning appears wherever you have the governing equation but few or no solved examples: seismic wave inversion in geophysics, medical imaging reconstruction (CT, MRI), aerodynamic shape optimization in aerospace, and thermal design in semiconductor manufacturing. In each domain, the forward physics is known (how waves propagate through rock, how X-rays attenuate through tissue, how air flows over a wing, how heat distributes in silicon) but generating training data by solving the equation thousands of times is prohibitively expensive.

A practitioner writes down the differential equation and boundary conditions, then trains a neural network by feeding it the equation itself as the loss function. The network's predictions are inserted back into the equation at random interior points and at measurement locations, and the mismatch — how badly the network's guess violates the law — becomes the training signal. This bypasses the need to pre-compute a library of solved cases. For a medical imager, this means reconstructing an image from sparse, noisy radiation measurements; for a design engineer, it means finding the aerodynamic shape that minimizes drag without running a full solver a thousand times.

The real deployment challenge is knowing when training has converged to the right answer versus stalled in a plausible-looking local minimum, especially for equations with sharp features or multiple scales.
