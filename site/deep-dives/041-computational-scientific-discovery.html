Talk 41 · Institute for the Study of Learning and Expertise / Stanford Center for Design Research

Pat Langley: Computational Scientific Discovery

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of relying only on hand-written math. One approach is to have a learning program (a system with millions of little dials the computer tunes by trial and error until its answers look right) search through many possible scientific laws and equations, guided by what we observe and what scientists already know about how things work. This talk is about how that search works, what makes a discovered law real enough for science to use, and why just finding an equation that fits the data is not enough.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Most computer learning chases one goal: make predictions as correct as possible. But science wants something different. Scientists need a law or model they can write down, read, understand, and use in new situations—not just a machine that predicts well. A true scientific discovery has to explore an organized space of possible laws or models, explained in language scientists already trust and use. Finding a curve that matches your measurements is easy; finding something that tells you WHY things happen is hard and much rarer.

First-Principles Model

A discovery system needs three parts: observations you are trying to explain, a space of possible laws or models you can search through (not infinite guesses, but a structured set built from known mathematical forms and rules), and decision rules (called heuristics) that guide which candidate to check next. The core requirement is that the final result must be something a scientist can grasp and use: an equation, a description of how something works step-by-step, or a model stated in forms science recognizes. Knowledge about the domain—the specific area you are studying—narrows down the space and makes sure candidate models make sense in that field.

Key Claims

  • Scientific discovery can be reframed as a guided search through candidate laws or models—a systematic exploration aimed at the most promising candidates, not random guessing.
  • What separates a discovery system from a simple data-matching algorithm is that it produces something close to a scientific theory: a result stated in a form scientists recognize and can work with.
  • When searching for equations, the goal is mathematical relationships; when searching for models of how things work, you must also capture the qualitative pieces—what causes what and how processes unfold step-by-step.
  • Qualitative models (descriptions of cause-and-effect or structure) often matter more than exact numbers because they show you how and why something happens, not just that it does.
  • One powerful variant, called process modeling, learns equations that describe how a system changes over time by studying sequences of measurements and using existing knowledge to infer the governing rules.
  • Domain knowledge is not something to get around or ignore; it is what makes the search manageable, sensible, and ensures the result will actually be useful to practicing scientists.

Important Terms

Heuristic Search

A method of exploring a vast set of possibilities by using simple rules or scoring to favor candidates that seem most promising, instead of checking every single option.

Communicable Law

A discovered relationship written in compact scientific form—an equation, a process description, or another format scientists accept—rather than a hidden prediction machine that gives answers but no insight.

Equation Discovery

The process of searching for mathematical equations that match observed data and capture an underlying law or relationship in nature.

Qualitative Model

A model that explains mechanism and structure—how or why something happens—even when exact numeric values matter less than understanding the cause-and-effect chain.

Inductive Process Modeling

Learning equations that describe how systems change over time by examining sequences of measurements and using existing scientific knowledge to infer the governing rules.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture connects to SINDy (a method for finding sparse equations from data), symbolic regression (automated equation search), Koopman analysis (finding hidden patterns in dynamic systems), and researchers like Miles Cranmer who argue science needs better automated tools for discovery.
  • It bridges the gap between fitting numbers to data and the way science has always explained nature through laws and mechanisms.
  • It explains why surrogate models (simplified stand-in models humans can read) matter: a tool that predicts well is not the same thing as a scientific discovery.

Practical Caution

A short equation that fits your data does not automatically count as a real discovered law. If you choose the space of possible equations poorly, use weak background knowledge, or set bad search rules, the system will churn out compact equations that look good on paper but have no meaning in actual science. The danger is mistaking a lucky coincidence for a discovery.

Takeaway

The main lesson: scientific machine learning should search and speak in science's own language. Observations put limits on what's possible, but the mathematical forms you allow and the domain knowledge you feed in are what turn a fitting equation into something a scientist can actually trust and use.
