Pat Langley: Computational Scientific Discovery

This lecture treats scientific discovery as a guided search through possible laws and models, directed by observations, mathematical forms scientists use, and what we already know.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Most machine learning chases accuracy in prediction but ignores whether the result is a real scientific theory—something researchers can state, examine, and use elsewhere. True scientific discovery needs more than fitting numbers to curves: it needs to explore a structured space of possible laws or models that explain what we observe in a language scientists recognize and trust.

First-Principles Model

A discovery system rests on three things: observations to explain, a space of possible laws or models you can search through, and heuristics (decision rules) that guide the search. The core requirement is that the result must be communicable—a law, equation, a description of how something works, or a model stated in forms that science already accepts. Background knowledge about the domain narrows the search and ensures the candidate models make sense.

Key Claims

  • Scientific discovery can be framed as guided search across candidate laws, hypotheses, or models—not random exploration.
  • What sets discovery systems apart from simple data-mining algorithms is that they output something like a scientific theory: a communicable object in an established form.
  • Equation discovery searches for mathematical relationships, but model discovery must also capture how things work qualitatively—the mechanisms and structure.
  • Qualitative explanatory models often matter more than exact numbers because they show cause-and-effect or how a process unfolds.
  • Inductive process modeling learns dynamic models (ones that change over time) from sequences of measurements, using existing knowledge to infer differential equations that describe the process.
  • Domain knowledge is not a problem to ignore; it is what makes the search manageable and ensures the result is actually useful to science.

Important Terms

Heuristic Search

A way of exploring a huge set of possibilities by using rules or scores to prefer candidates that seem most promising, rather than checking every option.

Communicable Law

A discovered relationship written in a compact scientific form—an equation, a process description, or another form scientists accept—rather than a hidden prediction machine.

Equation Discovery

The process of finding mathematical equations that match observed data and capture an underlying law or pattern.

Qualitative Model

A model that explains how or why something happens—the mechanisms and structure—even when the precise numeric values are less important.

Inductive Process Modeling

Learning models of systems that change over time by analyzing sequences of measurements and using existing knowledge to build differential equations that describe the dynamics.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture relates directly to SINDy (Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics), symbolic regression, Koopman analysis methods, and Miles Cranmer's work on why we still owe science better tools for discovery.
  • It bridges the gap between data-driven modeling and how science has traditionally explained nature.
  • It also explains why surrogate models that humans can read matter: a good prediction tool is not automatically a scientific discovery.

Practical Caution

A symbolic equation does not automatically count as a discovered law. If the space of possibilities, the background knowledge, or the search rules are chosen poorly, the system can produce short equations that happen to fit the data but are meaningless in science.

Takeaway

The main lesson is that scientific machine learning should search in science's own language. Observations constrain what's possible, but mathematical forms and domain knowledge make the result something a scientist can use.
