Talk 56 · Siemens Technology

Dirk Hartmann: Machine Learning And Physics-Based Simulations For Industrial Digital Twins

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from data instead of only hand-written math. One of the biggest uses is building working copies of real industrial equipment and processes — digital twins — that companies use to plan, test, and run their operations. This talk is about why the twins that actually work in real factories must combine two ideas: rules from physics (which tell you what *should* happen) and learning from examples (which spots patterns in messy real-world data). Neither approach alone gets the job done.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Real factories, products, and infrastructure need computer copies of themselves so people can try out ideas, predict what will happen, and make decisions before building or breaking something real. A full physics simulation — writing down all the rules from first principles — can be extremely accurate but runs slowly and requires a scarce expert to set up by hand. A learning program (one that tunes its millions of dials by trial and error until its guesses match examples) can be fast but often gives nonsense answers when asked about situations outside what it saw in the training data. Adding more twins to more assets hits a hard limit: manual expert effort is expensive and slow.

First-Principles Model

A digital twin is simply a running computer model connected to a real thing — a pump, a power plant, a production line, a bridge. In real industry, this model must answer real business questions while staying within hard constraints: it must run fast enough to guide moment-to-moment decisions, predict accurately enough that people trust it with real consequences, stay simple enough that technicians can maintain and update it over years, and cost little enough that rolling it out across many assets makes sense. Physics-based models give you structure: they capture the real laws of how things work, so they keep working even when you ask about a situation far from any training data. Learning programs give you speed and automatic adjustment to new data. The winning approach in practice is always a hybrid — using physics to guide the learning, and learning to fill in gaps where physics alone is too slow or the rules are too complicated.

Key Claims

  • Real industry cannot wait for full physics simulations to finish and cannot trust learning programs that wander outside their training data.
  • The only way to reach both speed and accuracy at scale is to blend physics and learning tightly, not simply pick one.
  • These hybrid digital twins must work across the full lifetime of equipment — from design testing through years of production.
  • The practical wins come from choosing the right balance between accuracy and speed to solve the specific decision at hand, not from chasing perfect accuracy.
  • The biggest bottleneck today is expert people manually building, tuning, and maintaining models — not the underlying math or algorithms.
  • Research that does not work in a real factory with real constraints, real maintenance, and real responsibility is incomplete.

Important Terms

Digital Twin

A working copy of a real thing (equipment, a building, a process) running inside a computer, used to predict what will happen or guide real decisions.

Hybrid Digital Twin

A digital twin built by combining two ideas: rules from physics (what the laws of nature say *should* happen) and patterns learned from real-world data (what actually *does* happen when rules collide with messy reality).

Industrial Deployment

Taking a method out of research, building it into real equipment or factory systems, running it for months or years, maintaining it as things change, and being honest about who is responsible when it guides a decision that matters.

Accuracy-Speed Tradeoff

In real use, you often cannot have the slowest method (pure physics) or the least trustworthy one (pure learning). You choose a mix: fast enough to guide decisions in time, accurate enough that you bet business money on it.

Expert-Effort Bottleneck

The hard limit you hit because building, tuning, and fixing good models requires scarce people with deep knowledge — and that scarcity stops you from deploying digital twins across all the equipment and processes where they could help.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk roots the whole series' central question — mixing physics rules with learning from data — in the most important real-world context: decisions that affect real equipment, real operations, and real money.
  • It connects to surrogate modeling (using a fast cheap model to replace slow expensive simulation), reduced-order models (simplifying physics without losing the parts that matter), active learning (asking what new data would teach the model the most), and uncertainty quantification (knowing how much you can trust a prediction).
  • It shows concretely why two big research areas matter: interpretability (can you explain why the model predicted what it did, so you can trust it?) and automation (can you build the model without expert hand-tuning, so you can scale to many assets?).

Practical Caution

A beautiful demo twin shown at a conference is not the same as a digital twin that works in a real factory making real decisions. True industrial use means the whole model must run reliably for years, with ways to check it against actual equipment, ways to update it when the real equipment changes or degrades, and someone clearly responsible when the model's prediction guides a choice that matters. Many research projects skip these hard parts.

Takeaway

When you apply learning and physics to real engineering problems under real limits, you are doing constrained engineering, not pure research. The model that actually wins is the one that solves the business problem with enough physics (to be trustworthy when extrapolating), enough data (to adapt to real variation), and enough automation (so you can build and maintain it without scarce experts). Hybrids are not a compromise between two pure approaches — they are often the only thing that works.
