Talk 116 · Caltech

Akhil Nekkanti: Data-Driven Techniques for Analysis of Turbulent Flows

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. Water and air in motion are chaotic and messy, but they hide organized patterns. This talk is about using a learning program to spot those patterns, clean up noisy measurements, and fill in missing data.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

When fluids like water or air move in turbulent ways (chaotic, swirling motion with many speeds and directions at once), they are almost impossible to track. You have to measure or compute thousands of different values to describe what is happening. The motion is also unstable: a tiny bump becomes a big change very fast. Yet underneath the mess, there are organized structures that control where energy goes and how the flow moves. Measurements and computer simulations come with problems too—noise (static, like on a radio), gaps (missing pieces), or errors. So the challenge is: how do you find the real organized patterns and fix the broken data?

First-Principles Model

Spectral proper orthogonal decomposition is a method that takes flow data and breaks it into organized pieces, sorting them by how fast they oscillate (like sorting waves by their frequency). It is related to PCA, which is a method that finds the main directions of change in any data—imagine taking thousands of measurements and asking 'what are the top few patterns that explain most of the variation?' SPOD does this for motion data, organizing by both space and how fast things are changing. You can extend this method to do three things: rebuild data that has missing pieces by borrowing patterns from nearby space and time; clean noise out by keeping only the strongest patterns and throwing away the weak jitter; and track brief-lived structures that appear and disappear quickly.

Key Claims

  • Turbulent motion is hard to predict because you have to track many things at once, small bumps grow fast, and output is not simply proportional to input.
  • Data-driven methods reduce the mountain of numbers by finding a handful of major patterns that explain most of the motion.
  • SPOD is a method that organizes flow patterns by oscillation frequency, which works well for turbulent flows.
  • You can rebuild data that has holes or damage by reusing patterns you find elsewhere in the data.
  • Looking at frequency and time together helps you see when organized structures appear, strengthen, and fade away.
  • Even though turbulence looks totally chaotic, organized structures underneath carry the energy and steer the motion.

Important Terms

Turbulence

Chaotic, swirling motion in a fluid (liquid or gas) with many different speeds and directions at once, where tiny pushes grow into big effects fast.

SPOD

Spectral Proper Orthogonal Decomposition: a method that takes flow data and breaks it into organized pieces sorted by how fast they oscillate (their frequency).

Coherent Structure

An organized clump or pattern of flow (like a vortex or jet) that carries meaningful energy and controls motion, even though it sits inside chaotic turbulence.

Gappy Reconstruction

Filling in missing or damaged parts of data by using the patterns you can see in the rest of it—like completing a photo with a torn-out piece by guessing from the edges.

Low-Rank Reconstruction

Rebuilding data using just a handful of the strongest patterns instead of every tiny detail, like sketching the main shapes of a busy scene instead of every object.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk connects modal analysis (breaking data into organized pieces), reduced-order models (simplified versions that keep the main behavior), turbulent-flow diagnosis (finding out what is actually happening), and data repair (filling holes).
  • It extends prior talks on POD, DMD, and Koopman methods by emphasizing frequency and brief-lived structures.
  • It also relates to sensor placement and experiment design because if you understand the patterns in your data, you can figure out where to put sensors to catch the important events, and fill in gaps using what you already know.

Practical Caution

When you rebuild turbulent flow data by keeping only the strongest patterns, you can remove noise but you may also wipe out rare events that matter physically. You should always check whether your cleaned-up data still matches what you actually see happening, not just whether the average error got smaller.

Takeaway

The core lesson is that turbulent data becomes workable by pulling out the biggest organized patterns—like finding the main currents in a noisy river—while also watching for brief-lived anomalies that the strongest patterns might miss.
