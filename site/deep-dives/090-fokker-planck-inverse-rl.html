Talk 90 · University of Michigan

Krishna Garikipati: Fokker-Planck Inverse Reinforcement Learning

This whole series is about getting computers to predict physical behavior by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. In biology and physics, we often see an agent or system move through a sequence of states—a cancer cell growing, an animal foraging—and want to figure out what it 'wants' (its goal, or reward) and what rules it obeys. This talk tackles the harder problem: when neither the goal nor the rules are known, can we infer both at once from watching the system move, using the math of random motion and probability spread?

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Most methods that try to infer why an agent behaves a certain way (what goal it is trying to reach) assume someone already told them the rules—how states change, how actions lead to new states. But in real science and biology, those rules are often hidden. Cancer cells, bacteria, animals: we see what they do but not the instruction manual. This talk asks: what if we have to figure out both the hidden goal and the hidden rules at the same time from just watching?

First-Principles Model

Think of a system as a jar of colored sand grains. Each grain is in some state (position, age, whatever matters). At each step, each grain can take an action or get pushed by randomness, and most grains spread out into new states. We can track the odds: what fraction of grains are in each state at each time? A Fokker-Planck equation (a rule that says how the spreading happens moment by moment) describes those odds. The lecture shows that this spreading math and the decision-making model (called an MDP: a way to describe choices with states, actions, odds of where you land, and what reward you get) can be made mathematically the same. That match is the key: it lets us run a fitting procedure (adjust the invisible goal and invisible rules over and over until they predict the observed trajectories well) that finds both the goal and the rules at once.

Key Claims

  • You can work backward from observed motion to find the goal (reward) an agent is trying to reach.
  • Most existing methods assume the transition rules are already known or given.
  • When both the goal and the rules are unknown, the problem gets much harder.
  • Random motion in a system (like probability spreading) can be described by the Fokker-Planck equation.
  • This equation and decision-making models (MDPs) have the same mathematical structure underneath.
  • Using that match, you can infer both the transition rules and the reward from just watching what happens.

Important Terms

Inverse Reinforcement Learning

Looking at what an agent actually does and asking: what goal or reward is it trying to reach? Then guessing the goal from the actions.

Markov Decision Process

A model that describes choice-making: a system has states (situations), actions (choices), odds of moving to the next state, rewards for being in states or taking actions, and decision rules for picking actions.

Fokker-Planck Equation

A rule that says how the spread of likely locations (the probability of being in each state) changes moment by moment in a system with random motion.

Transition Function

The rule that says: if you are in this state and take this action, what are the odds of ending up in each possible next state?

Variational System Identification

Finding the hidden rules of a system by adjusting guesses repeatedly to minimize how wrong they are, using calculus-based weak forms of the equations.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture brings together stochastic dynamics (random systems that change over time), inverse problems (working backward from what you observe to find hidden causes), and reinforcement learning (learning to choose actions) in a setting driven by real physics and biology.
  • It connects with other talks in this series on Fokker-Planck equations, SDEs (rules with randomness), and stochastic model reduction.
  • It shows a recurring theme: when a problem seems impossible because too many answers fit the data, adding physics structure can cut down the ambiguity and make learning work.

Practical Caution

Here is the trap: many different goals and many different rule-sets can all lead to the same behavior. A person might share because they like others or because they fear punishment—same action, different motives. When the lab adds physics rules that narrow down which answers make sense, that helps. But we still have to check: does the inferred goal match what we know about the real system from other experiments? Do not assume the math gave us the true answer just because it fits the trajectories.

Takeaway

To understand why something moves the way it does, you need two things: the goal (what reward it gets) and the rules (how states change). The Fokker-Planck equation is the math for how random motion spreads. By connecting that spreading math to decision-making, the method lets us infer the hidden goal and hidden rules together from watching alone.
