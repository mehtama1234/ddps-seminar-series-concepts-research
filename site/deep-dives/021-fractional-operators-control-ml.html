Talk 21 · George Mason University

Harbir Antil: Applications Of Fractional Operators From Optimal Control To Machine Learning

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of hand-written math. One way this series explores is using nonlocal operators (tools that measure how far apart two things can still influence each other, or how the past echoes into the present) to build better learning programs for images, geological data, systems you can control, and deep neural networks (programs with millions of dials tuned by trial and error).

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Real physical systems often show nonlocal behavior: one spot influences another far away, the past matters to what happens now, or something spreads in weird ways that don't match the normal rules. Standard calculus uses only the local slope at one point, but that misses the long-distance reach. If you ignore how a material's properties at one place affect its neighbor miles away, or how layers of rock store memory, or how fluids flowing and reacting jump between scales, you get the wrong prediction.

First-Principles Model

A fractional operator is a way to measure change that doesn't have to be integer (like the 1st or 2nd derivative you learn in school). Instead of just looking at one spot, it can reach back through time or across distance to see how things influence each other. One example is the fractional Laplacian: instead of diffusion (things spreading out evenly in all directions), it models diffusion that has long-range reach—information or particles scatter farther or linger longer than the simple model predicts. You can also treat the fractional exponent (the number that says how far back or how far the reach goes) as a tunable dial you fit from data, or even as a random variable to capture what you don't know.

Key Claims

  • Fractional calculus handles spreading that deviates from normal (anomalous diffusion) and systems where effects reach into the past or across distance.
  • In some cases, fractional operators can replace complicated nonlinear ways of smoothing images with simpler linear fractional differential equations (rules about change that use fractional derivatives).
  • Controlling systems with fractional differential equations raises new mathematical and computational questions that engineers didn't face before.
  • Unknown fractional exponents can be tuned like dials from data or treated as random variables when you need to capture uncertainty.
  • Fractional operators can be built into deep neural network layers (the computational parts that learn).
  • Chemical processes that react and flow show sudden jumps in how fast things happen at different scales, where fractional neural models may catch these shifts.

Important Terms

Fractional Operator

A mathematical operation with a non-integer exponent (like 0.7 instead of 1 or 2), designed to measure how influence spreads across distance or how the past echoes into the present.

Fractional Laplacian

A version of the Laplacian (a tool measuring how things spread and change) that works across distance rather than just at one spot; used to model spreading or influence that reaches far.

Anomalous Diffusion

Spreading of particles or information that breaks the normal rules; particles wander farther or slower than standard diffusion predicts.

Hereditary Property

The system remembers and depends on what happened before; the current state carries marks from the past.

Fractional DNN

A neural network layer built using ideas from fractional operators to capture patterns that depend on distant neighbors or past steps (nonlocal and time-dependent patterns).

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk widens the conversation about operator learning by focusing on operators that capture how things influence each other across distance (nonlocal physics).
  • It connects to optimal control (steering systems toward a goal) and uncertainty quantification (capturing what you don't know) because fractional exponents and long-distance effects can become uncertain parameters that change how you control things.
  • It also relates to graph networks: both are ways to represent how things in a system influence each other beyond just their immediate neighbors on a grid.

Practical Caution

Fractional models fit numbers well, but you have to make choices: how fractional should it be (what exponent), how do you handle the edges (boundary conditions), and how do you actually compute it. These choices change the answer. Use fractional operators only when your physics actually shows this long-distance or memory behavior—not as a generic knob to twist just to fit harder problems.

Takeaway

Not everything that happens depends only on what is at one spot right now. Fractional operators give both machine learning and science a way to describe things that reach across distance, remember the past, and spread in anomalous ways.
