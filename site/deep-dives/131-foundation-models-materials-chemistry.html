Talk 131 · University of Cambridge

Gabor Csanyi: Foundational Models for Materials Chemistry

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from data instead of using only hand-written math. This talk focuses on one specific challenge: when scientists need to simulate what large collections of atoms will do over long times, they usually run quantum calculations (the slow, exact way to compute atomic physics), but those are too slow. Instead, this lecture shows how to train a learning program to act like a fast approximate simulator of atomic physics by learning from quantum results.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Materials chemistry needs to simulate thousands or millions of atoms over long times to understand how materials change, break, or react. Quantum calculations (the rules that describe electrons and atoms precisely) can tell you exactly what happens, but they are so slow that running them on big systems takes weeks or months. The real challenge is building a fast trained simulator that handles chemical reactions, depends on how many atoms push on each other at once, stays accurate even on cases the simulator has never seen, and works whether the material is a solid crystal, a liquid, or something in between.

First-Principles Model

The core idea is this: quantum calculations can tell you the energy stored in any arrangement of atoms. You collect thousands of these atom arrangements and their energies from quantum calculations. Then you train a learning program (a bank of dials the computer tunes by trial and error) to predict that energy for any new arrangement of atoms, much faster than quantum can. The learning program uses two key tricks: it respects symmetries (if you rotate the atoms, the energy should stay the same; if you shift them in space, the energy stays the same), and it has a special wiring that lets atoms pass information to their neighbors in a network, which helps it learn how groups of atoms influence each other. Researchers also use an active strategy: they run the trained simulator on cases it has not seen, find the cases where the simulator is most confused or wrong, run quantum calculations on those cases, add them to the training data, and retrain. This cycle can push accuracy to within a few millielectronvolts per atom (a millielectronvolt is one thousandth of the energy you'd need to pull an electron away from an atom).

Key Claims

  • A mature new capability exists: fast trained simulators that predict energy and forces for large collections of atoms.
  • These simulators learn by fitting to quantum calculations, which give exact atomic physics but are slow.
  • The simulators must handle chemical reactions and depend on how multiple atoms push on each other together.
  • When trained well, these simulators run fast enough to simulate real molecules and materials.
  • Careful training on selected examples can reach accuracy within a few millielectronvolts per atom.
  • The designs that work best use wiring that respects physical symmetries and lets atoms exchange information as a network.

Worked Example

MACE (Multi-ACE) foundation model trained on Materials Project database: 1.6 million crystal structures computed with DFT (PBE functional), 89 elements. Model contains ~2.8M learnable parameters in message-passing graph neural network. Tested on new crystal structure (out-of-training): predicts total energy within 1 meV/atom of DFT, forces accurate to 0.01 eV/Å. Molecular dynamics simulation at 500K for 100,000 atoms (perovskite CsPbI₃) for 2 nanoseconds is completely impossible with DFT (would require ~1 million GPU-years); MACE completes in 1 GPU-day. Organic molecule (polyalanine peptide): MACE trained only on molecules up to dipeptide size extrapolates to larger peptides; solvation free energy predictions match experiment within 0.5 kcal/mol for 20 test molecules not in training set.

One Level Deeper

ACE (Atomic Cluster Expansion) basis: local environment descriptor ρ_i = Σⱼ φ(r_ij) Y_lm(r̂_ij) expanded in spherical harmonics Y_lm and radial basis functions. Power spectrum P_N'L = Σₘ |C_N'Lm|² is rotational invariant (C are expansion coefficients). Full correlation B_N = (C ⊗ C ⊗ ... ⊗ C) via Clebsch-Gordan tensor products generates permutation + rotation invariant basis. MACE adds message passing: atomic features H^(t) updated via H^(t+1) = H^(t) + W · (B^(t) ⊗ H^(t)), repeated for 2-3 layers. Energy E = Σᵢ e_i(B_i, H_i) is sum of local atomic energies, forces -∇E computed via autodiff. Two-body ZBL cutoff added for close-contact repulsion (r<1Å) where training data absent.

Failure Boundary

Training data limited to near-equilibrium structures (~1.6M or 12M depending on dataset); extreme compression (pressures >100 GPa) shows unphysical energy curves with wrong slopes. Extrapolation to unseen chemistry: model trained only on bulk inorganic crystals trained on inorganic salts, when applied to hydrogen combustion reaction network (not in training), initially works but long-time MD simulations of new systems eventually drift into spurious local minima. Fine-tuning catastrophic forgetting: if naively fine-tune on 100 new configurations of lithium compound, model completely forgets materials project knowledge; multi-head readout (training separate output layer) required to avoid this. Charge-dependent properties (electrostatics, polarizability) not in current MACE; organic acids, ionic solutions, or systems requiring explicit electron density fail. Universal potential works "roughly correctly" only within chemical space similar to training; truly out-of-distribution (novel elements or bonding motifs) predictions are unreliable.

Important Terms

Born-Oppenheimer Potential Energy Surface

A map showing the total energy stored in any possible arrangement of atoms, calculated as if electrons have settled into their lowest-energy positions for those atoms.

Force Field

A trained program that takes in any arrangement of atoms and predicts both the total energy and the push (force) acting on each atom.

Equivariant Message Passing

A neural network (learning program) where nearby atoms exchange information through connections in a network, while keeping the property that rotating or shifting all atoms together does not change the answer.

Active Learning

A training strategy where you run your trained simulator on new cases, find the ones where it is most confused or wrong, run exact quantum calculations on those cases, add them to your training examples, and retrain the simulator.

Many-Body Interaction

When the energy depends on how three or more atoms sit relative to each other, not just on pairs of atoms.

  • It demonstrates why hidden physical symmetries (like rotation and translation) are essential for building learning programs that work on real science and materials.

Practical Caution

A trained simulator can look perfect on the cases you trained it on, but completely wrong once you try something new. Imagine training a simulator on normal crystal structures, then pointing it at a material with defects, or a reaction the simulator never saw, or atoms crammed into unusual arrangements—it may give completely false predictions. Materials simulators must be tested hard: on chemical reactions, on cracked or damaged regions, on different crystal structures, on rare atomic arrangements, on systems far from anything in the training data.

Takeaway

Materials simulators are not automatic; they are trained maps of atomic physics that work only as well as the physics and symmetries they learned. Their power comes from respecting the hidden rules of symmetry, keeping energy conserved (if energy can appear or disappear, something is wrong), and seeing enough examples during training so they can make good guesses for new situations.
