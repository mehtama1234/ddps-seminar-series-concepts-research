Talk 119 · Google DeepMind

Yasaman Bahri: A First-Principles Approach to Understanding Deep Learning

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of only hand-written rules. This talk asks: how does deep learning actually work? Instead of just measuring whether a program with millions of little tunable dials gives good predictions, this lecture uses methods from physics—the study of how many tiny parts interact to create big patterns (like how jiggling molecules create heat)—to solve simple cases exactly, then use those solutions to understand real networks.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Deep networks make very good predictions in science and AI, but most people treat them as unknowable machines: you twiddle the dials, it works, nobody really understands why. This means we can't predict what they'll do in new situations, control them when they behave badly, or trust them when the stakes are high. The field needs basic principles that explain: why do certain network shapes, certain ways of tuning them, and certain learning behaviors actually work? What can go wrong?

First-Principles Model

The lecture uses a physics method: find cases simple enough to solve exactly with pencil and paper, solve them completely, then use those solutions to understand networks in the real world. When neural networks have so many dials that they become infinitely wide, they connect to a mathematical object called a Gaussian process—a way to think about probabilities assigned to functions (not just to single numbers), which tells you what functions the network will learn. Related ideas like kernels—measurements of how similar two things are—let you do learning in hidden spaces where the math is simpler. Tools from physics (studying how many parts combine to produce overall behavior, and tracking how systems change over time in ways that aren't just multiplication) describe what happens inside the network as it learns and builds its internal feature detector.

Key Claims

  • Understanding why deep learning works is a major open problem for AI and science.
  • Physics gives you real methods to derive true principles, not just measure how well something works on a test.
  • When neural networks become very wide, they connect to precise mathematical objects like Gaussian processes and kernel methods.
  • Models simple enough to solve exactly help isolate what actually causes the behavior we observe.
  • Averaging techniques can describe what millions of neurons do combined, without tracking every single one.
  • Studying how systems change over time in nonlinear ways helps track how training unfolds and how networks build features.

Important Terms

Statistical Physics

How large-scale patterns and properties emerge from many tiny parts interacting—for example, how individual molecules jiggling randomly create the property we call temperature.

Gaussian Process

A mathematical tool that assigns probabilities to entire functions (not just single numbers), connected to what very wide neural networks can learn.

Kernel

A function that measures how similar two inputs are to each other, allowing learning to happen in a hidden space of features instead of the original input space.

Mean-Field Theory

A method that studies the overall combined behavior of systems where many units interact, by computing averages instead of tracking every individual piece.

Nonlinear Dynamics

The study of how systems evolve over time when the next state depends on the current state in a way that isn't just scaling or multiplication.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk directly addresses the core focus: understanding why computers can learn to predict physical behavior by working from first principles, not just empirical measurement.
  • It extends earlier discussions of algorithm improvement and data quality by asking what deep learning mechanisms are actually understood and provable.
  • It connects scientific machine learning to fundamental learning theory by showing how physics tools (Gaussian processes, kernels, training dynamics) explain what networks can learn.

Practical Caution

Exactly solvable cases reveal the mechanisms that matter, but they may miss what happens when networks are realistically sized (not infinitely wide), when data is limited, when you use specific tuning strategies like Adam, and when engineering choices (like how layers are connected) often dominate what happens in practice.

Takeaway

Deep learning theory advances by finding simple cases where mechanisms can be solved exactly on paper, not just watched and measured.
