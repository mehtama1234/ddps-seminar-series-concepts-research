Talk 125 · Seoul National University

Youngjoon Hong: Operator Networks Based on Numerical Analysis

This whole series is about teaching computers to predict physical behavior (how heat spreads, how air flows, how water moves) by learning from examples instead of only using hand-written math rules. This talk shows how to do that for PDEs—rules that say how something changes from place to place and moment to moment—when the numbers in the rule can shift. The key move: don't start from scratch. Instead, take the proven ideas from 50 years of classical solvers (methods that break the space into small pieces and solve step by step), connect a learning program to that structure, and let the computer tune itself.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Engineers need to solve PDEs quickly and correctly, even when the rule itself changes (the material gets thicker, the flow speed shifts, the heat source strength varies). Right now, learning-based solutions need hundreds or thousands of pre-computed answers from expensive simulations to train on. But here's the puzzle: classical numerical methods (finite elements, spectral methods) already solved this problem decades ago by using structure—they don't treat PDEs as a blind guessing game. So why throw away what works and rebuild from nothing?

First-Principles Model

The approach joins learning with numerical analysis. A neural network (a bank of dials the computer tunes by trial and error until its answers look right) is not just trained as a generic curve-fitter. Instead, it's designed to respect how numerical solvers work: they slice the domain into pieces, approximate the answer inside each piece, and connect them. This design means the network already 'knows' the physics structure. It can handle tough PDEs where flow and smoothing both happen, or where fluid splashes around, without needing hundreds of pre-solved examples.

Key Claims

  • A learning program can solve PDEs with shifting parameters by learning how to map input properties (like coefficients) to the full solution.
  • Anchoring a learning program to numerical analysis structure improves how well it learns.
  • Methods that split the domain into pieces (finite elements) or use special polynomials (spectral methods) give natural foundations that learning programs can build from.
  • This approach handles tough families of PDEs across different settings.
  • You need far fewer pre-solved examples to train the network.
  • Tests show the approach stays accurate, works on cases it never trained on, and runs fast.

Worked Example

A spectral-coefficient-learning neural operator for convection-diffusion with Dirichlet boundary conditions predicts coefficients α_k in the Legendre expansion u = Σ α_k P_k(x) and achieves 10^-5 relative error unsupervised (no training data). For 2D problems on complex domains (circles, polygons), finite-element-based neural operators (replacing Legendre with P1, P2 elements) give 0.1–1% errors versus 10–50% for physics-informed neural networks and DeepONet with 30 training pairs. For variable-coefficient Poisson on a circle domain, the method handles discontinuous coefficients across subdomains.

For singular perturbation (ε ∂²u/∂x² + ∂u/∂x = f, ε = 10^-5), enriching the basis with a boundary-layer corrector function e^{-x/ε} yields 10^-5 error even for sharp transition profiles, whereas standard methods without enrichment fail.

One Level Deeper

The core idea: replace the unknown solution u with a known basis expansion u = Σ_k α_k φ_k(x) where φ_k are Legendre polynomials or finite-element basis functions. The neural network predicts only coefficients α_k, not the full field. A weak-form residual R = ∫ (∇u · ∇v + u·v - f·v) dx is formed by choosing test functions v = φ_j. This yields a residual vector r_j = R(α, φ_j). The loss is L = Σ_j r_j².

For boundary layers, asymptotic analysis predicts the corrector: u_corr = e^{-x/ε}. This is added as an enrichment basis element. The neural network now predicts coefficients for the enriched basis {φ_1, ..., φ_n, u_corr}. Gauss-Legendre quadrature evaluates integrals exactly (no numerical differentiation needed), reducing error sources.

Failure Boundary

The method requires knowing the PDE ahead of time and constructing an appropriate basis. For PDEs with unknown structure or exotic singularities not captured by the enrichment ansatz, the method fails. Basis functions must be smooth; discontinuous solutions (shocks) break the Galerkin formulation. The method doesn't generalize to time-dependent problems as easily: each time-slice requires a separate inference or a more complex temporal basis.

The convergence rate depends critically on basis choice and mesh refinement (for FEM). In a comparison table, errors sometimes increase then decrease ("hiccups") due to condition-number growth in the Galerkin matrix; preconditioning is needed but adds complexity. For nonlinear PDEs (Burgers), the nonlinearity couples basis coefficients, requiring an iterative solver in latent space. Extrapolation to out-of-distribution parameters (e.g., PDE coefficient larger than training range) is unreliable.

Important Terms

Operator Network

A learning program that captures the relationship between inputs (the numbers in the PDE that can change, like how thick or how fast) and outputs (the full solution), rather than computing the answer one point at a time.

Finite Element Method

A technique to solve PDEs by chopping the space into many tiny pieces and finding the approximate answer inside each piece, then gluing the pieces together.

Spectral Element Method

A high-accuracy version of finite elements that uses special wavey polynomials (spectral polynomials) to get very exact answers without needing to chop the space into finer pieces.

Parametric PDE

A rule for how something changes where certain numbers (the parameters—like speed, thickness, or strength) can shift, and each choice changes the answer.

Convection-Diffusion Equation

A rule where something (heat, a pollutant, a dye) both rides along a flow and spreads out sideways at the same time.

Connections Across The Series

  • This work joins neural operators with classical numerical methods, showing how to take proven ideas from the past and weave them into new learning systems.
  • It extends earlier work on learnable rules for how things change: instead of tacking physics rules onto learning as an afterthought, it makes the numerical solver structure itself the foundation of the network design.
  • It also connects to the goal of training on less data, since putting numerical structure into the network means fewer pre-solved examples are needed to teach the computer.

Practical Caution

How you split up the space and how fine you make the mesh choice still affects the result. The approach works well in controlled research settings, but we still need to test it across many different PDE types and real applications before claiming it will always work.

Takeaway

When you teach a computer to solve a PDE using a learning program, build that program on top of the same structure that made classical solvers succeed, not on a blank slate.
