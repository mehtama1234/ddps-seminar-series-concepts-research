Talk 52 · New York University

Benjamin Peherstorfer: Neural Galerkin Schemes With Active Learning

This whole series is about getting computers to predict physical behavior by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. This talk focuses on a technique that uses a program with millions of little dials (called a neural network) to guess solutions to complex rules that describe how something changes across both time and space (high-dimensional PDEs). The key trick: instead of training on random points everywhere, the method notices where its current guess is most wrong and deliberately collects more examples from those spots.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

When a physical rule describes how something changes across both time and space, you need a huge amount of data to train a learning program well. Picking training points blindly from a uniform grid wastes data and misses important stuff that moves or concentrates in just a few places — like a traveling wave or a sharp front. A fixed set of training points, chosen at the start, often fails to track these moving features as time goes on.

First-Principles Model

A classical numerical trick called Galerkin starts with a guess for the solution, measures how badly it breaks the physical rule, and then tweaks it to reduce that violation. In this talk's approach, instead of the guess being a standard mathematical formula, it's a program with dials (a neural network) that evolves step by step through time. The new piece: rather than collecting all training points ahead of time, the method looks at where the current guess breaks the rule most badly and automatically samples new training points from those spots.

Key Claims

  • Predicting how well a trained program will work on new data is hard in engineering when you cannot afford to run expensive experiments or simulations.
  • You can train a neural network to match a physical rule by measuring the rule's violation at sampled points and adjusting the network's dials to minimize that violation, step by step in time.
  • The size of the violation at each point tells you where to collect the next training samples — focus on spots where the guess is most broken.
  • For high-dimensional problems, picking training points adaptively (following the current guess's mistakes) is essential to make learning programs actually useful.
  • This approach shines when the solution has moving concentrated features, like a shock wave or a localized pulse.
  • Treating training data as something the solver itself decides when and where to collect (rather than gathered beforehand) is a different way of thinking about solving these equations numerically.

Worked Example

For high-dimensional (5D, 8D) advection with localized peaks traveling through space, naive uniform sampling of 1000 collocation points in the physical domain failed entirely—the network never saw the peak. With adaptive sampling proportional to squared solution magnitude at the previous timestep, the same 1000 samples per step successfully tracked all 5 dimensions. For the KdV equation with periodic boundary conditions, Neural Galerkin achieved higher accuracy than linear Galerkin despite using the same (small) number of basis functions, capturing shock merging dynamics the linear model smeared away.

One Level Deeper

Neural Galerkin replaces the discretized basis by a neural network u(x,t;theta(t)) whose weights theta evolve via ODEs. The loss function J integrates squared residuals ||∂u/∂t - N[u]||^2 against a time-dependent measure nu_t. Setting ∇_theta J = 0 yields a system of ODEs for theta-dot. Explicit schemes require only linear regression per timestep (cheap); implicit schemes require solving nonlinear equations per step (expensive but allows larger timesteps). Active learning samples from nu_t = sum_i beta_i * Gaussian(center_i, bandwidth_i), where centers and bandwidths move with solution peaks, focusing effort where solution has variation.

Failure Boundary

For waves interacting (collisions, merges), the fixed number of Gaussian kernels may not track all features simultaneously. Neural Galerkin with small networks exhibits mode collapse where the network learns only mean behavior; expressiveness grows with network size but training cost explodes. The method requires smooth PDEs; for systems with genuine discontinuities (shocks that don't smooth), network nonlinearity cannot emulate the exact shock speed and instead smears. Extrapolation to initial conditions or parameters far from training fails; generalization improves with more offline training but cannot break the fundamental data-requirement curse.

Important Terms

Galerkin Method

A numerical approach that starts with a guess for the answer, checks how badly it violates the physical rule at chosen points, then tweaks the guess to reduce that violation.

Neural Galerkin Scheme

The same Galerkin strategy, but the guess is a program with millions of dials (a neural network) instead of a fixed formula.

PDE Residual

A number that measures how badly a guess breaks the physical rule at a particular point — zero means the rule is satisfied perfectly there.

Active Learning

A strategy where the program looks at its own mistakes and asks for more training examples from the spots where it is most confused or wrong.

Evolution Equation

A rule that describes how something changes step by step over time (e.g. how heat spreads, how a wave moves, how a fluid flows).

Connections Across The Series

  • This connects to physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) through the idea of measuring how much a guess breaks the physical rules, but adds the refinement of stepping through time piece-by-piece and smartly picking training points as you go.
  • It also connects to reduced-order models — another way to shrink a huge problem into something smaller — because the neural network acts as an adaptive, learned-on-the-fly simplified representation.
  • Across the whole series, this reinforces one recurring idea: where you collect your training examples is just as important as what kind of program you use.

Practical Caution

The physical rule might look satisfied overall while the actual wrong answer grows silently (because you're only checking points you already sampled). Active learning — the automatic sampling strategy — helps catch this but is not a guarantee. You still need to validate the final answer against other methods or real measurements to be sure.

Takeaway

Learning programs for physical rules should not use a fixed set of training points chosen at the start. Instead, the training points should be chosen as you go, following where the current guess is struggling the most. Data collection is not just a preparation step; it is woven into the solver itself.
