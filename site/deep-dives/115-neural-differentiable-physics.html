Talk 115 · University of Notre Dame

Jianxun Wang: Neural Differentiable Physics

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. One approach is to build a program that is part known-physics (the part we already understand) and part learnable-neural (a set of dials the computer tunes by trial and error). This talk explains how to wire these two halves together so that fixing one automatically fixes the other, and why that matters when we have incomplete physics, high costs, or inverse problems — situations where we need to find what causes something we observe.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Engineers use hand-built math to predict how air flows over a wing or how heat spreads through metal. But this math is often incomplete (missing some real effect), takes forever to run (too slow for finding the best design), or costs too much for problems like inverse design and uncertainty quantification (figuring out what set of causes would give us what we see). A pure learned-neural program (dials tuned on past examples) is fast but may lose track of the physics and give nonsensical answers.

First-Principles Model

The idea is to split the simulator into two layers. The first layer is the physics we already know: conservation rules (energy doesn't vanish), discretization structure (the grid we step through), or partial knowledge (we know some equations but not all). The second layer is neural: trained dials that learn to fill in missing closures (corrections that complete an approximate equation), fix errors, or learn the unknown terms. Since both layers are wired so that a small change to either flows smoothly as a change to the other (differentiable), we can tune both the learned and known parts together when we have real data.

Key Claims

  • Hand-built physics solvers often fail or run too slow when the underlying physics is only partially known or understood.
  • Running inverse design and uncertainty quantification (figuring out causes from observations) becomes prohibitively expensive with traditional physics solvers alone.
  • Wiringa physics solver and learned dials together as a single differentiable program (where small changes to one automatically produce changes to the other) lets us tune both at once with real data.
  • Joining incomplete physics components with trainable learned parts produces predictions that stay more grounded and reliable than learned-only models.
  • Feeding real data back into the simulation loop (data-augmented simulation) can make predictions and control much better.
  • The goal is to merge traditional physics math and deep learning so each compensates for the other's weaknesses.

Worked Example

Take a turbulent thermal-fluid flow where a high-fidelity simulation takes days or weeks per run, and you need many runs because input parameters (boundary conditions, material properties) are uncertain and you must re-run the forward solve for each scenario. Wang's setup runs the solver on a coarse, low-resolution grid — cheap — and inserts a neural correction where the coarse grid misses the physics, then trains the two together end to end because the whole pipeline is differentiable.

The concrete building block is a differentiable numerical solver: the known part is a standard discretization (the pressure gradient, diffusion, and advection terms stepped on the grid), and the learnable part is a neural closure that supplies the sub-grid-scale correction the coarse grid cannot resolve. Gradients flow through both, so a mismatch at the output is attributed back to the neural dials and the two halves are tuned jointly rather than the neural part being bolted on afterward.

Wang stresses this is tested on long-term rollouts, not just one step. The hybrid coarse-plus-neural model is shown to stay closer to the reference trajectory over long rollouts than a pure coarse solver at the same low resolution — the pure coarse solver fails at that resolution because it cannot represent the fine flow structures — while remaining far cheaper than the high-fidelity run it is standing in for.

One Level Deeper

Illustrative form: the solver advances the flow by a known update, for incompressible flow the momentum step with advection, the pressure gradient, and diffusion, u_{t+1} = Solver(u_t) + NN_theta(u_t), where NN_theta is the neural closure filling the missing sub-grid term. Because Solver and NN_theta are both differentiable, training minimizes a trajectory-matching loss L = sum_t ||u_pred(t) - u_ref(t)||^2 plus a PDE-consistency term, and the gradient dL/dtheta is backpropagated through the unrolled solver steps.

Unrolling many timesteps to compute that gradient is where training breaks: the repeated composition of solver steps makes gradients vanish or explode, the same pathology as very deep networks. Wang reframes the solver as a continuous formulation — a neural-ODE / controlled-differential-equation view where the discrete stepping scheme is one special case of an ODE solver — so the state evolves as dh/dt = f_theta(h, u) and the adjoint gives stable gradients over long horizons instead of a brittle deep unroll. A trainable hidden state (an LSTM-like memory) is added to carry long-range temporal dependence.

The division of labor is explicit: conservation structure and the discretization grid are the known layer that is never learned, so mass and momentum bookkeeping stays correct, and only the closure term — the correction that completes the approximate coarse equation — is neural. This is why the hybrid can run on a very coarse spatial-temporal resolution and still track the reference: the physics layer keeps the solution physical, the neural layer supplies what the coarse grid dropped.

Failure Boundary

The neural closure only knows the flow regimes in its training data. Wang is explicit that a pure black-box model completely fails to generalize out of distribution — change the Reynolds number, geometry, or forcing beyond what was seen and the learned closure produces the wrong correction. The observable symptom is a closure term that quickly blows up: the correction feeds back into the solver, amplifies each step, and the long-term rollout diverges rather than settling, which the closure-modeling community sees as the standard stability failure.

Even in-distribution, the guarantee is only as long as the rollout horizon it was trained and validated on. Over much longer rollouts than training (he mentions horizons like 32 steps and beyond) the trajectory can drift and look completely different from the reference, so the practitioner must watch for growing trajectory error, energy that no longer stays bounded, and closure outputs that trend upward instead of staying a small correction — all signs the coarse-plus-neural model has left the range where the learned sub-grid term is trustworthy.

Important Terms

Neural Differentiable Physics

A program that combines known physics rules and a set of trainable dials in a single pipeline where a small change to either half flows through to change the final answer.

Data-Augmented Computational Physics

A physics simulator improved by training dials on real data so that learned pieces correct or complete the known parts.

Closure Model

A learned piece that fills in hidden terms left out of an approximate physics equation — the missing link that makes the equation complete.

Inverse Problem

A puzzle where you see an outcome and need to find what caused it — for example, given measurements of a material's response, figure out what kind of material it is.

Differentiable Programming

Writing code so that when you change an input number, you can automatically compute how the output would change by following a smooth chain of dependencies.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk brings together differentiable solvers (code written so answers change smoothly with inputs), closure learning (training dials to fill in missing physics), and hybrid physics-learning approaches.
  • It builds on earlier talks about reduced models and operator learning by shifting focus from just making simulators fast to fixing incomplete physics inside the learning loop.
  • It also connects to scientific AI reliability because trusted physics stays embedded inside the trained model, anchoring it to the real world.

Practical Caution

Hybrid models can hide errors. The learned part might memorize quirks in the data or silently fix mistakes in the physics layer without us realizing it. Validation must check whether the learned correction is truly capturing a real missing piece of physics or just papering over dataset noise and flaws.

Takeaway

When physics is incomplete, the solution is not to throw it away and train a pure neural model. Instead, embed the incomplete physics inside a learnable loop. Keep the known rules. Let the dials learn what is missing. This way the learned part and the physics part stay connected and reinforce each other.
