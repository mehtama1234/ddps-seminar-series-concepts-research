Talk 11 · Max Planck Institute for Dynamics of Complex Technical Systems

Pawan Goyal: Physics-Informed Learning For Nonlinear Dynamical Systems

This whole series is about teaching computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of hand-written math. One powerful way to learn fast models is to build in what we know about the physics structure from the start. This talk focuses on a technique that learns a small reduced model—one that captures the main patterns of how a system changes over time—by studying real data, and it automatically keeps the physics structure that simplified models should have.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

To design machines, control them, optimize them, or test how they handle surprises, engineers need models that predict how systems evolve over time. But the most detailed models—ones with millions of small pieces that track every tiny part of the system—take hours or days to run. Older simulators or commercial software often hide their internal equations; you only see the output numbers. Classical techniques exist to build small, fast versions of big models, but they need the full detailed equations to work, and many data sources (old codes, vendor software, specialized tools) do not give you access to those.

First-Principles Model

Most systems have a key insight: only a handful of patterns actually matter for predicting what happens next. Imagine a spinning turbine: you do not need to track every molecule; you just track a few shapes that move and evolve. The technique here learns those core shapes and their rules directly from measurement data. It works by assuming the small model has the same structure a classical reduction would have built, then solving backward from the data to find what those equations must be.

Key Claims

  • To understand how systems evolve and respond to new operating conditions, you need equations that can be run repeatedly and modified.
  • Two stubborn problems: turning a messy real system into equations is hard work, and detailed accurate models are so huge they take forever to run.
  • Instead of deriving equations by hand or running expensive detailed simulations, you can jump straight from measurement data to small, fast equations.
  • The core idea is to learn those small equations from data while enforcing that they follow the structure physics would predict.
  • If your data samples enough different behaviors (fast and slow, different starting points, different stresses), the learned equations match what older mathematical methods would have found.
  • This approach shines when your data come from older software, commercial tools, or one-of-a-kind simulators where you cannot reach inside to see the math.

Worked Example

For a tabular reactor model learning nonlinear dynamics from data, the method collects 513 snapshots over 30 seconds. A reduced order model of dimension 10 is trained to capture concentration (phi) and temperature (theta) fields. The full finite element model requires accessing discretized operators, whereas operator inference learns the ROM directly without the high-fidelity matrices. Testing on held-out time interval 30-60 seconds shows prediction error under 1 percent—demonstrating generalization without overfitting—while compression preserves the coupling structure across both species.

One Level Deeper

Operator inference formulates a quadratic form PDE where discretization yields x-dot = Ax + f(x), but instead of solving for A and f directly, it finds reduced operators A-hat and f-hat by minimizing ||M(A-hat, f-hat) - residual||^2 subject to regularization. The method exploits that if data snapshots X and time derivatives X-dot are available, the reduced model A-hat = V^T A V and f-hat projected onto reduced basis V satisfy a least-squares problem with a Tikhonov regularizer. This avoids ill-conditioning by balancing fit against parameter norm. Gray-box variants add known physics like diffusion structure to the objective, keeping only unknown reaction terms unstructured.

Failure Boundary

Operator inference degrades when the underlying PDE has unknown nonlinearities or when data is sparse in poorly sampled regions. The method assumes access to uniform time-step snapshots; missing or irregularly sampled data requires preprocessing. For advection-dominated problems (high Reynolds number flows), the quadratic form assumption breaks down and the ROM cannot capture sharp gradients or transport phenomena without special treatment. Parameter extrapolation fails outside the training regime since the inferred operators have no embedded physical constraints—only data-driven relationships.

Important Terms

Operator Inference

A technique that figures out the equations of a small simplified model by studying how a real system moves and changes over time in recorded data.

High-Fidelity Model

A detailed mathematical description with so many pieces and variables that running it once can take hours or days, making repeated use nearly impossible.

Dominant Modes

The few most important patterns or shapes that describe how a system behaves; like the lowest notes that define a song even though many more notes could exist.

Legacy Code

Old software or a simulator that produces output numbers you can measure, but does not show you the equations or internal rules that created those numbers.

Physics-Structured Learning

Training a model to fit data while also obeying the known laws and rules of physics, rather than letting it find any pattern that fits.

Connections Across The Series

  • This continues earlier talks about building small, fast models from big ones, but now focuses on learning the small model's equations from data alone.
  • It connects to talks on tools that can take derivatives through a simulation and on solving inverse problems, because a learned small model becomes the fast engine for steering a system.
  • It reinforces a repeating thread: when you have real data and physical laws, use both together; let data fill in what is missing or too costly to compute by hand, but keep the physics rules where you know them.

Practical Caution

This is not just drawing a smooth curve through points. If you guess the wrong structure for the reduced model, or if your data only shows the system in a narrow set of conditions (say, only low-speed flows, not turbulent ones), the small model may look perfect on short simulations but break down when you try longer runs or push the system in new ways.

Takeaway

When you have data from a system but no access to its full equations, you can still build a small, fast model by learning it directly from the data while insisting it follows the structure that physics-aware reduction should have—no guessing, no hand-tuning, but honest about what the data can and cannot teach.
