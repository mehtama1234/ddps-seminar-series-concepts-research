Talk 55 · Georgia Institute of Technology

Molei Tao: Trustworthy Learning Of Mechanical Systems And Stiefel Optimization

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of only using hand-written math. One popular way teaches a learning program (a huge bank of dials the computer tunes by trial and error) to respect a hidden property of moving systems called symplecticity: energy and momentum follow rules that should never be broken. This talk shows how building this respect into the learning process makes predictions accurate far into the future, and reveals a surprising connection: the same trick works for tuning dials on curved mathematical surfaces like the space of perpendicular frames.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Real moving systems—a pendulum, a planet orbiting, a spinning top—obey hidden rules. Energy doesn't vanish; momentum stays balanced; the system's path follows a special curved surface in mathematical space (called symplectic). Standard learning programs can memorize short clips of motion while completely breaking these rules, so as you ask them to predict further and further into the future, the predictions drift further and further from what really happens. A related problem shows up in optimization (the process of tuning a program's dials): when the dials themselves live on a curved surface instead of a flat space, treating the surface as if it were flat causes the tuning algorithm to jump off the surface and make wrong moves.

First-Principles Model

A moving mechanical system is not just a snapshot of where things are right now; it is a continuous flowing motion that preserves certain hidden quantities and shapes. Symplectic learning means building the prediction rule so that it automatically preserves these hidden symmetries: the learned equations of motion will keep energy and momentum balanced no matter how far into the future you predict. For problems with dials that live on a curved surface like the Stiefel manifold (the set of all possible perpendicular rectangular frames, where each column is a unit-length vector pointing in a different direction), optimization algorithms need to be redesigned so that each step of tuning stays on that curved surface instead of wandering into flat space.

Key Claims

  • Teaching a learning program about physics directly (not as a wish, but as a hard built-in rule) can make its predictions of moving systems stay true for much longer.
  • Real mechanical systems preserve quantities and shapes: energy, momentum, and a special property called symplecticity.
  • Symplecticity is the hidden geometric rule that underlies the motion of all Hamiltonian systems (systems described by energy and momentum).
  • Embedding the symplectic rule directly into a learning program can be proven to extend how far into the future its predictions remain correct.
  • The same geometric thinking that fixes physics learning also fixes how we tune programs with many dials on curved mathematical surfaces.
  • Momentum-based tuning (the method of accelerating a downhill search) can be redesigned to work correctly on curved spaces, with perpendicular frames as a practical example.

Worked Example

Symplectic map learning for planet orbit prediction: given discrete-time observations of positions (q) and momenta (p) across multiple time steps, train a neural network parameterizing generating function S(q,q̄) such that the induced symplectic map preserves exact symplecticity. Over N prediction steps, error grows linearly O(ε_approx + c·N·h) where h is time step and c·N·h is prediction time—vastly better than exponential error bounds for non-symplectic approaches. Standard map (chaotic): visualized as Poincaré pancake map with stable islands separated by chaotic seas; gfnn captures major stable islands while other methods fail.

Vision Transformer on CIFAR-10: imposing Stiefel constraint (orthonormal attention weight matrices W_i^Q, W_i^K) within each attention head yields 8.3% error vs 9.7% vanilla (1.4% improvement). With added momentum-accelerated optimization, error drops further. Computational cost: O(n·m²) per iteration vs O(n²·m) for existing manifold methods when n >> m (n=image dimension, m=basis/rank).

One Level Deeper

Symplectic maps satisfy J_θ^T·Ω·J_θ = Ω (Ω is symplectic form [[0,I],[-I,0]]). Rather than search directly for φ, parameterize generating function S(q,q̄;θ) and define p̄ = ∂S/∂q̄, p = ∂S/∂q. This automatically induces symplecticity regardless of S approximation quality. Loss L = ||q̄-Φ(q,p)||² + ||p̄-∇_q̄S||² enforces PDE satisfaction. KAM theory applies: if perturbation (approximation error) is small, nearby tori are preserved, bounding long-time drift. For Stiefel optimization on f(X) where X ∈ St(n,m): use constrained variation principle with Lagrange multiplier λ to enforce orthogonality. Discretization: explicit method stays on manifold automatically (X^T·X = I preserved), unlike exponential maps or Cayley transforms that require expensive matrix functions. Momentum in Riemannian metric: q̇ = v, v̇ = -∇f + (v^T·∇f/||v||²)·v (projected gradient with friction to drive convergence).

Failure Boundary

Symplectic learning fails when data violates symplecticity: if true dynamics has dissipation (energy decay), minimization cannot reach zero loss, so induced map is symplectic but inaccurate. For chaotic systems: long-time Lyapunov instability makes any state prediction diverge; map captures structures (islands) not individual trajectories. Stiefel optimization over-constrains when true solution isn't orthogonal: imposing orthogonality when columns should have varying norms causes bias. Computational: Jacobian-vector products required for loss gradient become expensive if S has deeply nested derivatives. Hyperparameter tuning: friction coefficient γ(t) must decay appropriately—too slow and convergence stalls, too fast and momentum is lost.

Important Terms

Symplecticity

A hidden property of flowing systems where the system preserves area and quantity-like measures, and returns to the same state if you run it forward then backward; the defining property of Hamiltonian motion.

Hamiltonian Dynamics

A way to describe mechanics using two ingredients: where things are (position) and how fast they are moving (momentum), plus a single master quantity (the Hamiltonian) that accounts for all the energy and structure of the system.

Long-Time Prediction

Asking a learned model to forecast what happens far into the future; small mistakes in the prediction rule compound and snowball, causing the predictions to drift away from what actually occurs.

Stiefel Manifold

The collection of all possible rectangular grids of perpendicular unit vectors (think of it as all the ways you can arrange compass directions in a space); a curved mathematical surface where constrained tuning problems live.

Riemannian Optimization

A method for tuning a program's dials when those dials themselves are constrained to live on a curved mathematical surface, ensuring each tuning step stays on the surface instead of wandering off into empty space.

Practical Caution

Preserving a hidden structure helps only when the structure actually describes your system. If you force the preservation rule (symplecticity) on a real machine that loses energy through friction or gets pushed by outside forces, the learned model will hold onto the wrong properties and make terrible predictions.

Takeaway

The core message is that learning to predict real moving systems accurately requires the learning process to respect the geometry—the hidden shapes and rules—of those systems. Accurate long-time prediction comes from honoring the quantities that stay constant (like energy and momentum) and the symmetries that never break.
