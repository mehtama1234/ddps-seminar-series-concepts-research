Talk 17 · Virginia Tech

Traian Iliescu: Large Eddy Simulation Reduced Order Models

This whole series is about getting computers to predict physical behavior by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. This talk focuses on a specific challenge: when you build a simplified model (a reduced-order model) that captures only the largest motion patterns in turbulent flow, the small-scale motion you threw away still affects what happens—so you need to add a model of those missing effects to get correct predictions.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Turbulent flows—think of water or air swirling at many different scales—have large patterns mixed with tiny wiggling patterns, and they all influence each other. A simplified model that represents only the large patterns cannot capture what the small patterns do. The small-scale motion that was skipped over still feeds back and changes how the large scales move, making predictions unstable or wrong. A basic shrinking-down of the equations does not automatically account for this missing influence.

First-Principles Model

Large eddy simulation starts with a mathematical filter that divides motion into two groups: large-scale patterns you will track, and small-scale patterns you will skip. You then add an extra term that estimates how the skipped small patterns affect the tracked large ones. To apply this same idea to a simplified model (a reduced-order model), you use filters to decide which motion patterns the model represents, evolve only those patterns forward in time, and add models for how the untracked patterns influence the full motion.

Key Claims

  • Simplified modeling for turbulence remains an open problem with no single method everyone agrees on.
  • One common approach expresses the solution as a sum of basis patterns (computed from data) and projects the governing rules onto those patterns.
  • When you mathematically filter nonlinear rules, the small scales you filtered out create an extra term that must be guessed or modeled.
  • Models for the missing effects can add a dissipation-like term to the equations or alter the equations themselves.
  • Practical tricks like pressure stabilization may not have clean theoretical backing but are needed to make real simulations work.
  • Scaling rules for models of missing effects often depend on the specific problem rather than working across all flow speeds and geometries.

Worked Example

Large eddy simulation of a red sea overflow (geophysical flow) requires resolving 10 km length, 1 km height/width domain; naive calculation shows 10^18 degrees of freedom needed for all scales. Using 6 reduced basis modes (POD), a coarse mesh element method discretizes the filtered Navier-Stokes, and a Magorinsky eddy-viscosity closure adds turbulent stress. Compared to fine-mesh spectral element solution (polynomial degree 16-17 per element), the LES-ROM simulation achieves 10-100x speedup while capturing mixing with factors of 2-3 higher than fine mesh. For 3D flow past a cylinder at Re=1000, using 6 modes with dynamic Smagorinsky model shows energy spectrum matching reference solution, with error of ~0.1 relative L2 across entire prediction horizon.

One Level Deeper

LES filters Navier-Stokes equations with operator ∂u/∂t + ∇·(u⊗u) = -∇p + ν∇²u + ∇·τ_SGS where τ_SGS = -2νₜ(S - 1/3 S δ_ij) is the subgrid stress modeled via eddy viscosity νₜ. ROM projects onto POD modes V via Galerkin: ȧ = M a + B(a⊗a) + closure term. The data-driven approach uses two-scale decomposition: y₁ (large modes, ~4 modes) and y₂ (small modes, discarded), then learns correction ã_tilde and b̃_tilde via least-squares on true closure ≡ A_true a + B_true (a⊗a) - (M a + B a⊗a). Physical constraints enforce: matrix A is dissipative (diffusion), nonlinear term B is conservative (Skew-symmetric for incompressible flow).

Failure Boundary

Under-resolved channel flow (Re_τ=395, Re_bulk~5600) with 10 modes fails: Galerkin ROM alone shows 40% error in mean velocity profile and completely wrong Reynolds stress tensor components. Pure data-driven closure (least-squares fit only) performs poorly; only hybrid approach (eddy-viscosity component + data-driven correction with physical constraints) recovers statistics. At very high Reynolds numbers without enough basis modes, spectral bias occurs: ROM eigenvalue decay plateaus rather than decays, indicating inadequate basis. Closure models are problem-dependent; no universal model works across vastly different Reynolds numbers (cylinder flow vs turbulent channel).

Important Terms

Large Eddy Simulation

A method that directly solves for large flow patterns and adds a model to account for how the small-scale motion it does not track affects the large-scale patterns.

ROM Filter

A mathematical dividing line in a simplified model that specifies which sizes and speeds of flow motion the model can represent.

Closure Problem

The difficulty of figuring out how the small-scale motion you cannot represent in your model affects the large-scale motion you can represent.

Eddy Viscosity

A model that adds a damping or friction-like term to represent how small-scale turbulent swirling motion stirs and smooths out the large-scale flow.

Data-Driven Variational Multiscale ROM

A method that combines the mathematics of handling multiple scales of motion with patterns learned from actual or simulated flow data to estimate the effects of small scales the model does not represent.

Connections Across The Series

  • This connects to entropy-stable fluid schemes, interpretable machine learning for fluids, and simplified models of flow around the shared challenge of accounting for motion scales you cannot directly resolve.
  • It is a central warning for neural surrogates (learning programs trained to mimic fluid behavior): the small scales you do not track still have real effects on the large scales, even if the model ignores them.
  • It also explains why testing a neural flow surrogate over long prediction spans matters—the effects of the missing small scales pile up over time.

Practical Caution

Do not assume one set of tuned numbers or a simple scaling rule will work when the flow speed, the shape you are simulating, or the flow conditions change. Simplified models of turbulent flow must be adjusted and tested separately for each type of flow you care about. The numbers that work for one situation often do not transfer to another.

Takeaway

Turbulence simplified models are inherently filtered models. When you ignore the small scales, the simplified version is not just smaller; it is actually missing real physics that affects what happens. You cannot just shrink and hope—you have to actively model what the removed scales would do.
