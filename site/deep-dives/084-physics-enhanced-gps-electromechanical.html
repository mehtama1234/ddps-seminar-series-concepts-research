Talk 84 · Vanderbilt University

Thomas Beckers: Physics-Enhanced Gaussian Processes For Electromechanical Systems

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. Machines like motors and generators are tricky to predict from data alone — you need enough examples, and the predictions can break the laws of physics. This talk shows how to combine two ideas: a statistical method (called a Gaussian process) that doesn't just guess what happens next but also estimates how uncertain that guess is, and a way to write the rules for motion (called Port-Hamiltonian systems) that automatically preserves energy. Put them together, and you get a model that learns from data, stays physically correct, and tells you how sure it is — all without adding penalty tricks during training.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

When you try to predict how motors or generators behave using only data and machine learning, two problems show up. First, you need a lot of examples to get good predictions. Second, the model will happily make predictions that violate physics — say, claiming energy appears from nowhere. You can add penalties during training to discourage bad behavior, but the model still often breaks physics or demands very careful tuning to work well.

First-Principles Model

A Gaussian process is a statistical method that describes a cloud of possible functions instead of picking just one. For any prediction, it gives you both a best guess and a range showing how confident it is. Port-Hamiltonian systems are a way to write down the rules for how things change over time such that energy is never created or destroyed — it only flows, gets lost to friction, or stored. When you combine these two ideas, you build a cloud of possible motion-rules that all obey Port-Hamiltonian structure, rather than having the computer learn a single hidden behavior. Even fancier versions use Gaussian processes with a special structure (Green's functions, which show how simple linear systems respond to a quick push) baked into the hidden layer, so the hidden behavior itself respects what physics should look like.

Key Claims

  • Data-driven models of motors and generators should encode the laws of physics to learn from fewer examples and avoid predictions that break physics.
  • Gaussian processes let you learn models that don't assume a fixed number of dials to tune, and they track uncertainty in every prediction.
  • You can enforce physical correctness by building it into the model itself, rather than punishing the computer during training when it breaks physics.
  • A Gaussian process model of a Port-Hamiltonian system uses data to form a cloud of possible motion-rules, each one conserving energy.
  • Adding Gaussian processes with a special prior structure (Green's functions) to the hidden layer makes that hidden behavior physically plausible too.
  • Green's functions, which describe how a simple linear system responds to a sudden push, let you encode physical knowledge into the similarity measures that Gaussian processes use.

Worked Example

For an electromagnetic coil attracting an iron ball (a toy electromechanical system), physics-enhanced Gaussian processes trained on roughly 20-30 sparse data points. Using a port-Hamiltonian prior structure, the method recovered energy conservation accurately even in regions without training data: total energy stayed constant when the coil was on and decreased when the coil switched off, across all posterior samples. A vanilla GP with no physics structure produced predictions that diverged wildly outside the training region.

For a pendulum swinging in water (nonlinear friction), about 30-40 data points sampled in phase space (angle vs. angular velocity) were enough. The Hamiltonian (energy) always decreased due to water friction, a property preserved by structure-aware sampling. A physics-enhanced variational autoencoder learned latent dynamics from around 50 video sequences, respecting the known spring-mass equation while inferring the unknown external excitation.

One Level Deeper

Port-Hamiltonian systems are written ẋ = (J - R)·∇H(x) + G·u, where H is total energy, J is skew-symmetric (J^T = -J, routes energy without loss), R is symmetric positive-semidefinite (dissipation), and G maps inputs. The skew-symmetry of J guarantees energy conservation and passivity by construction. A GP prior is placed on the Hamiltonian H via a structured kernel K_ph(x1,x2) built by applying the linear operators (J, R, and derivatives) to a base RBF kernel.

Each sample drawn from this GP yields a deterministic port-Hamiltonian system ẋ = (J - R)·∇H_sample(x) + G·u that preserves energy conservation exactly—so uncertainty is expressed as a spread over physically valid dynamics, not arbitrary functions. For nonlinear friction, the dissipation matrix R is itself modeled by a GP, which loses the closed-form posterior and requires variational inference. The physics-informed VAE encodes video into a latent (x, ẋ) pair and models the latent dynamics as a linear ODE ẋ = A·x + B·u using a Green's-function kernel.

Failure Boundary

Gaussian processes scale as O(N^3), limiting exact inference to roughly 1000 samples; beyond that requires inducing points or variational approximations (as used for the nonlinear-friction case), which reduce analytical tractability. Modeling nonlinear friction by placing a GP on the matrices J and R loses the closed-form solution, and the variational approximation weakens uncertainty guarantees.

The port-Hamiltonian structure is restrictive: the choice of Hilbert space for H is difficult to generalize beyond electromechanical systems with a clear energy definition. Posterior variance from sample spread can be loose and does not directly quantify prediction error. The sparse-data advantage diminishes as state dimensionality grows; high-dimensional systems (beyond ~10-12 states) are rarely reported because both data needs and covariance-inversion cost climb sharply.

Important Terms

Gaussian Process

A statistical method that treats functions as random clouds, giving you both a best guess for what happens next and a range showing how sure you are.

Port-Hamiltonian System

A way to write the rules for how things change over time such that energy is conserved: it tracks energy storage, energy lost to friction, and how different parts push on each other.

Physically Correct By Design

A model where the laws of physics are wired directly into how it's built, rather than added as a penalty the computer must follow during training.

Green's Function

A mathematical tool showing how a simple linear system responds when you give it a single quick input; used to teach the Gaussian process what physics-respecting behavior should look like.

Latent Dynamics

The motion rules that happen inside a learned hidden layer or compressed space, such as the squeezed code in a machine that learns to compress and uncompress data.

Connections Across The Series

Practical Caution

A physics-based structure only works if the real system actually follows that structure. If your physics assumptions don't match the true system, the model can be very confident and systematic in predictions that are wrong in the same way every time.

Takeaway

You can bake both uncertainty estimates and physical structure directly into how the model is built, instead of bolting physics on as a penalty after the computer has already learned.
