Talk 12 · University of Michigan

Karthik Duraisamy: Towards Robust, Accurate And Tractable Reduced-Order Models

This whole series is about getting computers to predict physical behavior by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. One popular shortcut is to replace a huge, slow simulation with a tiny one—think of fitting the whole behavior into fewer dials. This talk shows why that shortcut has three sticky rules: the tiny model must stay accurate when things change, it must match the real physics with low error, and it must still run fast enough for engineers to use.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Reduced-order models are tempting: replace a giant system that takes hours to run with a shrunken version that finishes in seconds. But real flows like combustion or detonation pack many hard things at once—equations that change at wildly different speeds, waves bouncing around, heat releasing from chemical burning, solid shapes warping or tearing, and leftover errors (the gaps between the true rules and the approximate model) that jump around violently. A model tuned perfectly for one set of conditions may start acting wild, break the rules that should always hold (mass stays the same, energy does not vanish), or stay just as slow when the conditions or parameters shift.

First-Principles Model

A reduced-order model moves the governing equations onto a smaller geometric space—imagine projecting a high-resolution picture onto a lower-resolution grid, but for the physics. For tricky nonlinear systems, having fewer dimensions does not automatically make the leftover error cheap to compute, stable when you step the model forward in time, or conservative (keeping mass and energy intact). Robust models therefore need: variables built to respect the physical laws, a method for fitting residuals (the gap between the true rule and the model) that keeps the model stable as it evolves, a way to shift the model when parameters change, and a trick to evaluate the expensive nonlinear pieces at only a handful of locations instead of everywhere.

Key Claims

  • A reduced-order model solves the same governing equations on a smaller geometric space rather than discarding the equations entirely.
  • Difficult flows with many types of physics create problems for reduced models through equations at different timescales, layers of structure at different sizes, wave transport, and shape changes.
  • Standard Galerkin reduced models (one common way to build reduced models by projecting the equations onto a smaller space) can look stable in theory but become unstable when flows are hard or parameters drift far from the training data.
  • Least-squares Petrov-Galerkin methods improve stability by measuring how badly the approximate equations fail at selected points, rather than just checking that residuals are perpendicular to the reduced space.
  • Evaluating the expensive nonlinear pieces takes cost; you must do this work at only a small number of sample points instead of everywhere, or the speed gain vanishes.
  • Running time matters most when the full simulation is so expensive that even collecting training examples becomes the bottleneck.

Worked Example

A rocket combustor with 15 million degrees of freedom gets reduced from ~100 million spatial points to 4-60 basis coefficients. The least-squares Petrov-Galerkin ROM with hyper-reduction shows 3-4 orders of magnitude speedup: without oversampling the speedup is ~10x, but with careful QR-pivoting-based sample selection and oversampling, the speed is maintained while keeping reconstruction error low (during training intervals). Temperature predictions in true future-state mode show error jumps visible at timestep 20 and 60 modes, but projecting small perturbations at inlet conditions still demonstrates stability.

On a 1D combustor simplified test: the ROM stays stable across chamber lengths, trained on truncated domain with many forcing frequencies (sinusoids). The full system assembly then handles geometry changes by switching the acoustic region to full-order while keeping the reaction zone as component ROM.

One Level Deeper

The method uses three-level symmetrized reduction. First, variable transformation (q→s, primitive/conservative forms) via dual-time stepping ensures conservation while allowing flexible variable choices. Second, least-squares Petrov-Galerkin projection: if φ(q) = V·c + q_ref is the ROM ansatz, then minimize residual r(c) = L[V·c + q_ref] over Galerkin test space W, producing Jacobian-symmetrized system (W^T·L_φ·V)·c_dot = -W^T·r. This inherits stability from discrete consistency without requiring symmetric governing equations. Third, hyper-reduction samples the residual at m << n points: introduce basis for residual representation, use QR pivoting to find critical sample points, then carefully oversampling (2-3x the basis count) maintains fidelity while keeping online cost O(n·m·k) for n FOM DOF, m sample points, k ROM modes.

Failure Boundary

In chaotic flows (shear layers), the spectrum of POD basis does not decay: with 0.167 ms training data, spectral magnitude stays large; more data doesn't fix it. Static manifolds (linear or nonlinear) cannot handle flow topology changes when inlet conditions change. Hyper-reduction fails if samples miss localized reaction zones: QR pivoting alone can miss sharp gradients in multi-scale problems, requiring manual tuning of oversampling ratios. Forward prediction error jumps sharply beyond training window—even with many modes, long-term drift accelerates when manifold cannot follow system state.

Important Terms

Reduced-order model

A shrunken version of a full simulation that solves the same physics rules but on fewer dials, so it runs in seconds instead of hours.

Least-Squares Petrov-Galerkin

A method for building a reduced model by measuring how badly the approximate equations fail at certain spots, then adjusting the model to lower this failure score rather than enforcing a standard geometric alignment.

Structure-Preserving

A change of variables or a method built so that laws that should always hold—like total mass stays the same or energy does not disappear—continue to hold in the reduced model.

Hyper-Reduction

A trick to speed up reduced models by computing expensive nonlinear pieces at only a few hand-picked spots instead of at every grid point.

Component Reduced-Order Model

A reduced model built for one part of a larger system, which can then be connected to other reduced or full-size pieces.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk ties together earlier reduced-model talks into a practical checklist: speed and small size are not enough without stability.
  • It connects to digital twins—copies of real systems running on computers—by showing that a digital twin that works reliably in the field needs stable reduced pieces, not just fast guesses.
  • It links to entropy-stable schemes and models you can train with gradients by showing that reduced models must match the structure and conservation laws of the full system.

Practical Caution

A model can fail even if you found a good reduced basis if the leftover error does not match the physical laws, the nonlinear terms are not evaluated at just a few spots, or the operating conditions change the flow in ways your training data never showed (say, whether swirling vortices appear). Always test stability, runtime cost, and how well the model handles parameter shifts together, not one at a time.

Takeaway

The core lesson is that fewer dimensions does not make the problem simpler. Real models must preserve physical laws, adapt when physics changes, and cut the cost of evaluating the expensive nonlinear pieces.
