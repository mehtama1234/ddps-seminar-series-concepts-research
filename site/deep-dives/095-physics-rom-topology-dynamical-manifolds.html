Talk 95 · BNZ Energy Inc.

Michael Glinsky: A Physics-Based ROM Capturing The Topology Of Dynamical Manifolds

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from data instead of only using hand-written math rules. This talk focuses on one problem: when you make a fast computer copy (called a reduced model) of something expensive to simulate, like a pulsed power experiment squashing metal at extreme temperatures, the copy might look right at first but miss the actual shape and structure of how the system changes over time — and this talk shows a method inspired by scattering, renormalization, and geometry that tries to capture that shape.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

High-energy-density physics simulations like pulsed power liner implosions are expensive and highly nonlinear. Engineers need fast computer copies (reduced models that approximate expensive simulations) to do practical work. But if you build a fast model by just keeping track of numbers describing the system at each moment, you might miss something crucial: the actual geometric shape of how the system evolves — how it twists, curves, and stays confined to a lower-dimensional surface. A model that misses this shape will fail in realistic scenarios because it doesn't actually respect how the physics flows.

First-Principles Model

The proposed Heisenberg Scattering Transformation works in layers. First, it applies a structured deep convolutional transform (a program that repeatedly filters the data at many different scales, building stable hierarchical layers) inspired by Mallat's scattering transform (a method that breaks down data by filtering at smaller and smaller scales, much like zoom levels on a map, to find stable patterns). Second, it uses a Hamiltonian generating functional (a physics-based mathematical tool that describes exactly how a system evolves from one moment to the next). Together, these project the dynamics into a renormalization or scattering-matrix basis (a coordinate system where the structure of physics at different scales — from tiny to large — appears as natural and separate features). In this special coordinate system, the dynamics stay confined to a low-dimensional complex linear subspace (a flat space with fewer directions). PCA (a statistical method that finds the most important directions in data) identifies this subspace, and then a neural network with encoder and decoder parts quantifies the shape through an analytic function (a smooth, well-behaved mathematical function where calculus works).

Key Claims

  • The approach takes inspiration from Mallat's scattering transform (a method that breaks data into stable layers by repeated filtering at different scales, much like zoom levels on a map) and a Hamiltonian view of how physics evolves (a description that tells you how the system moves from one state to the next).
  • A logarithmic generating functional of the Hamiltonian (a physics-based mathematical tool derived from the energy and structure of the system) acts on the system state or the probability distribution describing the system.
  • The transform projects dynamics onto a renormalization or scattering-matrix basis (coordinates where the structure of physics at tiny, medium, and large scales appears naturally and separately).
  • PCA (a statistical method that finds the most important directions in data) identifies the low-dimensional flat space that holds the dynamics.
  • The shape of how the system evolves is quantified using a neural network with encoder and decoder parts that approximates an analytic function (a smooth, well-behaved mathematical function) on that subspace.
  • The method is tested as a fast computer copy for 2D pulsed power liner implosions, including MagLIF (Magnetized Liner Inertial Fusion).

Important Terms

Scattering Transformation

A method that breaks down data by repeatedly filtering at different scales — like looking at a map zoomed out, then zoomed in, then zoomed in more — to build stable patterns that don't change if you wiggle the data a little.

Heisenberg Scattering Transformation

This talk's method: a scattering transformation combined with physics rules (Hamiltonian dynamics) to represent how a system evolves in a coordinate system where scales of physics appear naturally.

Renormalization Basis

A coordinate system where the structure of physics at different scales — tiny details, medium-range effects, and large-scale trends — shows up as separate, natural features.

Dynamical Manifold Topology

The actual geometric shape of the low-dimensional surface where the system's motion is confined: how it twists, curves, folds, and changes — not just which points are on it, but the shape itself.

MagLIF

Magnetized Liner Inertial Fusion: an experiment where a metal cylinder with a magnetic field implodes at extreme speed and temperature to squeeze fuel and trigger fusion.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture connects finding low-dimensional representations of data (manifold learning), building fast approximations of expensive simulations (reduced models), the geometry of systems (topology), and physics-based methods for transforming data.
  • It extends earlier work on mapping how dynamics change and the geometry of probability distributions by showing that the reduced-dimensional space has real geometric shape and structure, not just arbitrary coordinates.
  • It also links to creating fast computer copies of high-energy-density physics simulations (surrogate models), where preserving the actual geometric structure is essential for predictions to stay accurate.

Practical Caution

Transforms that try to preserve the geometric shape of how systems evolve work only when matched carefully to the actual physics. If the transform structure you assume does not match the real physics, the model can preserve the wrong features and fail when you try to use it in realistic scenarios.

Takeaway

When you build a fast computer copy of an expensive simulation, you cannot just minimize how far your copy is from the original (the projection error). Some systems force you to also preserve the actual geometric shape of how dynamics live — the surface they flow on, and the scale structure within it. Models that ignore this shape miss real physics.
