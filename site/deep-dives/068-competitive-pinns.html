Talk 68 · Georgia Institute of Technology

Spencer Bryngelson: Competitive Physics-Informed Networks

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of hand-written math. One popular way is to add physics rules directly into the trial-and-error tuning: a program (with millions of dials it adjusts to fit the data) is also told that its answer must respect those rules. This talk is about why that approach, called a PINN, often gets stuck and gives up improving when the physics rules are written one way, but a new method called Competitive PINNs gets much further by creating a second referee program that learns to spot exactly where the first program is wrong, and the two compete until the answer is almost perfect.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Standard PINNs train by taking each physics rule, measuring how badly it is broken at different spots, squaring those measurements, and adding them all together to get a single score to improve. But this method hits a ceiling: on even simple physics problems, the answer stops getting better even though the program could do much better. One reason is a hidden danger in squaring: when you square those measurements, you also square a number that measures how sensitive the whole calculation is to tiny wobbles in the input—big numbers get much bigger when squared, which makes the trial-and-error tuning jerky and unstable, fighting against improvement.

First-Principles Model

Competitive PINNs turn physics solving into a two-player game. The solution network—the program trying to learn the physics—wants to minimize how much the physics rules are broken at each spot. A second referee network (the discriminator) is rewarded whenever it finds a spot where the solution network is still doing badly. The best answer both networks can reach in this competition is the same as if a mathematician had solved the physics perfectly by hand. By switching to this competitive setup, the method changes which mistakes get penalized most, which avoids some of the numerical wobbling that comes from squaring measurements.

Key Claims

  • Standard PINNs write their goal as the sum of squared measurements of how badly each physics rule is broken, but this causes the tuning to get stuck on most problems.
  • Competitive PINNs add a second program trained to find where the original PINN's answer is wrong.
  • The two programs form a competition where the best answer either can reach matches the exact solution to the physics rules.
  • This competitive approach avoids the numerical wobbling that comes from squaring those large sensitivity numbers in physics discretization.
  • On a simple standard test problem, Competitive PINNs made answers ten thousand times more accurate than strong comparison methods.
  • The method made answers so precise they matched what a computer can store exactly, with improvement continuing smoothly at each training step.

Worked Example

A competitive physics-informed neural network (CPINN) on a 2D Poisson problem reaches single-precision error (~10^-7 L2 relative error) within a few orders of magnitude fewer forward passes than standard PINN with Adam optimizer, which decreases by less than one order of magnitude over all iterations. For the steady Navier-Stokes lid-driven cavity flow, standard PINN barely decreases error (factor of 2 over all passes), whereas CPINN converges to reasonable accuracy; convergence is slower than for Poisson but demonstrates tractability.

The method replaces the standard squared-residual loss min_u ||PDE_residual||^2 with a min-max game where a discriminator (neural network D) penalizes large residuals, creating a saddle-point problem. An ablation study shows that among various optimizers (Adam, SGD, L-BFGS, several recent variants), competitive gradient descent (CGD) with the Nash equilibrium update consistently outperforms all alternatives.

One Level Deeper

Standard PINN solves min_π ||A^T A π - A^T b||^2, where A encodes PDE derivatives and π are network parameters. This squares the condition number κ(A), making convergence poor. CPINN reformulates as min_π max_D ⟨D, F(π)⟩ where F(π) is the PDE residual and D is a discriminator. The linearized local approximation yields two update equations for π and D that include cross-derivative terms (Hessian off-diagonals). The Nash equilibrium is found via a truncated LU decomposition raised to an infinite power, yielding an update that incorporates what each player would do knowing the other's strategy.

The loss now has the form L = ||D · F||_2^2 instead of ||F||_2^2, which avoids squaring the condition number of the differential operator.

Failure Boundary

CPINN is slower per iteration than standard PINN because it requires computing Hessian-vector products for the cross terms; speedup is measured in forward passes, not wall-clock time. Navier-Stokes results show convergence is noisy and significantly slower than Poisson; the method remains sensitive to initialization. Highly heterogeneous coefficients (conductivity jumping 1000×) or singular perturbation problems (steep boundary layers) are not addressed. The approach assumes the problem is well-formulated; if boundary conditions are not properly imposed or the PDE residual is numerically unstable, the discriminator cannot fix it.

Important Terms

Physics-Informed Neural Network

A program with millions of dials that the computer tunes by trial and error until its predictions both match the data you show it and also obey the rules governing how a physical system changes in space and time.

Competitive PINN

Two programs in a competition: one learns to predict the physics while the other learns to spot its mistakes, and they keep pushing each other until the answers are nearly perfect.

Discriminator

A referee program trained to identify exactly where another program's answer is breaking the physics rules.

Zero-Sum Game

A competition where one player's gain is exactly the other player's loss, so they want opposite things and push each other to find the best strategy both can reach.

Condition Number

A number measuring how much a calculation gets jittery and unreliable when the inputs wobble slightly; large values mean tiny changes in input make huge wild swings in output.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk directly builds on earlier lectures about why PINNs stop improving, and offers an adversarial solution.
  • It shows how sensitivity and numerical stability problems in optimization tie directly to why physics goals can plateau, putting the math of stability at the center of the story.
  • It sits alongside Neural Galerkin and structure-preserving closure talks as different ways to build neural physics solvers you can actually trust in real work.

Practical Caution

Adversarial training (where two networks compete against each other) introduces its own tuning choices and stability issues. Before trusting Competitive PINNs, you should check whether the method works across different physics problems, not just on one carefully-chosen example where it shines.

Takeaway

The core lesson is that how you write the goal (the score to minimize) decides whether the trial-and-error tuning will work well. If squaring measurements of broken rules causes the tuning to get jerky, changing the game setup—having a referee spot mistakes instead—can make the method actually learn.
