Talk 68 · Georgia Institute of Technology

Spencer Bryngelson: Competitive Physics-Informed Networks

This lecture introduces competitive PINNs, an adversarial method where a discriminator (a separate network trained to spot errors) learns to highlight where the solution network fails, letting the system reach much higher PDE-solution accuracy than standard PINNs that minimize squared residuals.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Standard PINNs train by squaring PDE residuals and adding them up, but they often stop improving around modest accuracy even on simple PDEs. One reason is conditioning: when you square residuals, you're also squaring the condition numbers from PDE discretization, which makes the optimization numerically unstable and hard to push to high accuracy.

First-Principles Model

Competitive PINNs turn PDE solving into a two-player game. The solution network tries to minimize how much the PDE is violated, while a discriminator is rewarded for finding mistakes the solution network makes. The exact PDE solution is the best strategy both players can reach in this game. This adversarial approach changes which errors are penalized most and sidesteps some of the conditioning problems that come from squaring residuals.

Key Claims

  • PINNs use PDE residuals as loss functions but typically hit accuracy ceilings on most problems.
  • Competitive PINNs add a separate adversarial network that is trained to spot where the PINN makes mistakes.
  • The two networks form a competition where the best strategy either can find matches the exact PDE solution.
  • The method avoids the numerical instability from squaring large condition numbers in PDE discretization.
  • On a Poisson problem (a standard test), Competitive PINNs achieved errors ten-thousand times smaller than strong baseline PINNs.
  • The method produced errors at single-precision machine accuracy with steady improvement each training step.

Important Terms

Physics-Informed Neural Network

A neural network trained to match observed data and to satisfy the residuals of a differential equation (the rule governing how a system evolves in space and time).

Competitive PINN

An adversarial form of PINN where a second network is taught to find errors in the first network's solution.

Discriminator

A learned network in Competitive PINNs that is trained to point out where the solution network is wrong.

Zero-Sum Game

A competitive setting where if one player gains something, the other player loses exactly that amount, so their interests are completely opposite.

Condition Number

A number that measures how sensitive a calculation is to small changes in input; large values mean the calculation will be noisy and fragile.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk directly builds on earlier lectures about why PINNs fail, and offers an adversarial fix.
  • It ties conditioning problems in optimization to why physics losses can stop improving, making numerical stability central to the story.
  • It also pairs with Neural Galerkin and structure-preserving closure talks as other ways to make neural PDE solvers more numerically trustworthy.

Practical Caution

Adversarial training adds its own stability and tuning challenges. Competitive PINNs should be judged on whether they converge reliably across different PDEs, not just on one carefully-chosen example.

Takeaway

The core lesson is that how you write the loss function for physics matters. If squaring residuals causes bad conditioning, changing the game setup can change whether the method learns well.
