Talk 138 · University of Wisconsin-Madison

Nan Chen: Bridging Models and Data: Assimilation, Model Hierarchies, Causal Inference and Digital Twins

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from data instead of only hand-written math. This talk focuses on one key tool: taking a program's predictions and constantly tuning them by comparing what the program says will happen to what actually happens in real measurements, then using this tuned program to combine models of different complexity, trace what caused observed events, and run "what if?" tests on a live digital copy of a physical system.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Real systems—weather, oceans, underground reservoirs, airplanes—exist in three separate worlds. One world is actual data: measurements from sensors scattered around. Another world is simplified models: rough equations scientists built that capture the essential physics without all the details. The third world is detailed models: complicated equations that try to get every aspect right. Right now, these three worlds barely talk to each other. A digital twin—a live, constantly updated computer copy of a physical system—must combine all three. It needs to handle measurements that come in at odd times and places, answer questions about what caused what (not just predict one step ahead), and figure out if predictions will hold up in extreme situations.

First-Principles Model

The core technique is data assimilation: comparing a model's prediction to a real measurement, measuring how far apart they are, and then automatically tuning the model's internal state and settings to shrink that gap. Think of it like a weather forecaster checking their prediction against the actual temperature, then adjusting their computer's picture of the atmosphere to match better. This lecture extends the idea three ways. First: latent data assimilation means the simple model and the detailed model both get translated into a shared simplified language (like two people speaking different languages but both knowing shorthand). This lets them swap information without rewriting either program. Second: assimilative causal inference means running this same tuning process backward in time—if you see an effect now, what chain of causes earlier made it happen? This uses Bayesian updates, which just means updating your belief about something by saying "here's what I thought before, here's what I just observed, now here's my updated belief." Third: digital twins combine all these tuning and causal tools together to run "what if?" tests (if this event happens, what follows?) and predict rare, extreme events that almost never happen in the training data.

Key Claims

  • Comparing model predictions to real measurements and automatically tuning the model connects theory and practice in a mathematically sound way.
  • Models arranged by complexity (simple physics first, then detail layers added) can work together when tuned to the same data.
  • Tuning inside a shared compressed code makes simple and complex models compatible without rewriting either one, saving time and money.
  • By running the tuning backward in time, you can trace from observed events to what caused them.
  • Knowing what caused what and whether predictions will hold matters for climate tipping points and rare extreme events.
  • These tools together make working digital twins that stay honest about what they don't know.

Worked Example

For El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) simulation, the team compared three operational models with their bridging model approach. The best operational models (CSM2 and GFDL CM2.5) fail to capture basic features: they cannot reproduce the two types of ENSO (Central Pacific and Eastern Pacific events), miss extreme multi-year events, and have persistent statistical biases. Their bridging model, trained on output from idealized models (a coupled stochastic 1D model and a 6-dimensional stochastic ODE), used latent space data assimilation on 600 latent dimensions capturing 99% of variance. The result: pseudo-observations from idealized models (only SST and zonal wind along equator) successfully guided the operational model to recover all missing fields including subsurface temperature and thermocline height. The corrected model reduced SST bias by orders of magnitude and reproduced extreme events, central/eastern Pacific splits, and statistical distributions matching reanalysis observations.

Runtime speedup reached 40 years of synthetic data in 8 minutes on a laptop, compared to operational models requiring days of computation. The conditioning Gaussian network framework for data assimilation achieved 300-600x speedup over ensemble Kalman filtering while maintaining equivalent accuracy.

One Level Deeper

Data assimilation combines prior model predictions (a Gaussian distribution p(u) from model dynamics) with observational likelihood (p(v|u), how consistent observations are with the model) to produce a posterior p(u|v) via Bayes' theorem: p(u|v) ∝ p(v|u)·p(u). The posterior integrates information from both, weighted by uncertainties in each source. For chaotic systems, small perturbations (0.1% in Lorenz attractor) cause exponential divergence: error ∝ exp(λt) where λ is the Lyapunov exponent; thus initialization is critical.

The conditional Gaussian network (CGN) transforms highly nonlinear systems into conditional Gaussian form by splitting state u into observed u₁ and unobserved u₂, and defining a nonlinear map u₂→V such that V given u₁ is conditionally linear: dV/dt = A(u₁)V + f₂(u₁) + stochastic noise. Although conditional on u₁ this is linear, the marginal and joint distributions remain fully nonlinear and non-Gaussian (confirmed by composition with encoder/decoder nonlinearities). This structure admits closed-form data assimilation without ensemble sampling, enabling direct loss-function integration during neural network training. The assimilative causal inference (ACI) uses sequential forward/smoothing data assimilation to identify dynamic causal interactions: comparing filtering (past data only) vs smoothing (past+future data) distributions reveals if Y causes X by measuring information gain from future observations.

Failure Boundary

Bridging models require idealized models with accurate physics at critical scales and boundaries; if pseudo-observations contain systematic biases, they corrupt the entire assimilation. Data assimilation only improves initialization; extreme events driven by boundary conditions (not initial conditions) may not improve if the operational model's boundary physics is fundamentally broken. Conditional Gaussian structure assumes the nonlinearity decomposes into a known linear part with additive unknown operators—systems with multiplicative nonlinearities (e.g., u₁·u₂) violate this structure. CGN requires sufficient observations of u₁; if u₁ is sparsely observed, the posterior for V becomes ill-conditioned. Causal inference range determination is subjective: threshold choice (ε=10⁻⁵ vs 10⁻⁷) dramatically changes inferred causality ranges; the objective integration method avoids this but requires stable numerical computation. ACI cannot distinguish simultaneous causality (both X→Y and Y→X at the same instant) from instantaneous feedback. The method also struggles with high-dimensional systems where spurious correlations masquerade as causal links when partial observations hide confounding variables.

Important Terms

Data Assimilation

Automatically tuning a model's internal state and settings by comparing what the model predicts to real sensor measurements, making the model's prediction closer to the truth.

Model Hierarchy

A set of models ordered from simplest (basic physics only) to most complex (real-world details), so you can choose speed or accuracy depending on your need.

Latent Data Assimilation

Tuning different models inside a shared, simplified code so they can talk to each other without either model having to change its own equations.

Assimilative Causal Inference

Running the tuning process backward in time to find what causes led to observed effects, using Bayesian updates which update your belief by combining what you thought before with new evidence.

Digital Twin

A constantly updated computer copy of a real physical system that predicts what will happen next and helps answer "what if?" questions without changing the real thing.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk brings together data tuning, simplified internal representations, digital twins, model hierarchies, and causal reasoning to show they are all the same underlying idea.
  • It builds on earlier talks about oceans, underground reservoirs, weather, and uncertainty by showing how data tuning is the common glue connecting all these domains.
  • It also connects to reasoning systems that can imagine outcomes because a digital twin lets you run counterfactual tests ("if we change this setting, what happens next?") to decide before acting in the real system.

Practical Caution

A digital twin can look trustworthy while secretly passing along errors from two places: first, the choice of which models to combine (maybe one is always too optimistic or too pessimistic), and second, gaps in where measurements are collected (if you only measure in one spot, you miss what's happening elsewhere). Tuning the model to match data is only as good as knowing how much uncertainty remains and admitting when the model and reality fundamentally disagree.

Takeaway

Assimilation is not just washing out noise. It is a bridge connecting what models predict, what sensors measure, what caused what, and real decisions about dangerous systems.
