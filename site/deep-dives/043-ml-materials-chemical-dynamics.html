Talk 43 · Los Alamos National Laboratory

Sergei Tretiak: Machine Learning For Materials And Chemical Dynamics

This lecture shows how machine learning models can stand in for expensive materials and chemical simulations, using smart data collection to find where the model is uncertain and dealing with the fact that what happens at a distance away can matter.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Getting precise answers from quantum and molecular simulations is costly, but finding new materials means running thousands of these simulations across different structures and conditions. Machine learning can predict how a structure's properties relate to its shape, but it fails when the training data are missing important cases or when the model only looks at each atom's immediate neighbors and ignores effects from distant atoms—like electric charge spread across the system, how the charge redistributes, attraction between far-off molecules, or excited electronic states.

First-Principles Model

A molecular ML model takes the positions of atoms and predicts the energy, forces, or properties. Local models—where each atom's energy comes mostly from atoms nearby—run fast and scale to large systems, but real chemistry includes effects that bridge across the whole molecule. Active learning works by having the model make predictions, spotting configurations where it is least confident, running expensive reference simulations at those points, retraining with the new data, and repeating. To capture longer-range effects, some models add layers that let atoms talk to each other step by step, use cycles where predictions feed back in, or add charge-equilibration (a way to spread charge across the system to match what electrostatics demands).

Key Claims

  • Machine learning builds surrogate models trained on reference chemical or materials data.
  • The quality of training data sets a hard limit on accuracy, because the model can learn only the configurations it has seen.
  • Active learning grows the dataset automatically wherever the model is most unsure.
  • Assuming each atom is predicted mainly from its nearby neighbors makes atomistic ML fast but can miss charge transfer, charge redistribution, electrostatic effects, van der Waals attraction, and excited electronic states.
  • Distant interactions can be captured with layers that pass information step by step, self-consistent feedback loops, and charge-equilibration schemes.
  • Simulating chemistry over time requires the model to stay stable as atomic configurations change, not just to predict static properties accurately.

Important Terms

Machine-Learned Potential

A trained model that predicts molecular energies and forces from the positions of atoms.

Active Learning

A loop that picks new configurations for expensive reference simulations where the model is most uncertain, then retrains.

Locality Approximation

Treating each atom's energy or behavior as if it comes mostly from atoms in its neighborhood, not from distant parts of the molecule.

Nonlocal Interaction

A physical effect where distant atoms or the overall charge distribution in a molecule influence what happens at one location.

Charge Equilibration

A step that computes how charge spreads across a molecular system to satisfy electrostatic forces and long-range effects.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture ties to data-focused scientific machine learning: choosing which data to gather is as important as the model architecture itself.
  • It also connects to multiscale modeling because the rules that apply to single atoms must add up to reliable predictions at the material scale.
  • It reinforces the series theme that when extrapolation is risky, built-in knowledge about how the physics works is often needed.

Practical Caution

A model that performs well on a test set can fail once you run it in a live simulation if that simulation explores configurations the training data never included or enters a regime where distant atoms and global charge effects dominate.

Takeaway

The underlying lesson is that molecular ML is a controlled substitute for quantum or molecular physics. Whether it works depends on where you collected training data and what physical interactions—whether distant or not—the model's design can represent.
