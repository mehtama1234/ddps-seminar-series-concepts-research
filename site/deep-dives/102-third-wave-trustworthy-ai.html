Talk 102 · Purdue University

Guang Lin: Towards Third Wave AI: Interpretable, Robust Trustworthy Machine Learning

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from data instead of only hand-written rules. This talk focuses on a third strategy: make those learning programs tell us both their answer and how sure they are, let them explain how they work, include what we already know about physics and causes, and use data from multiple places with different accuracy levels. These four pieces together—prediction, uncertainty, understanding, and physics—are what make learning programs trustworthy enough for real-world decisions where mistakes matter.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

When scientists and doctors need to make fast decisions—predicting disease spread, deciding on treatment, monitoring safety—they need computers that not only guess right but also tell them how confident to be, show their reasoning, and stay honest about what they don't know. Most learning programs today are opaque: they gobble data, tweak millions of dials to fit patterns, and spit out an answer. Nobody can see why they picked that answer. Nobody knows how wrong they might be. For real-world problems where errors have consequences, that's too risky. The question is: how do we build learning programs that work well AND let us see inside them AND quantify their uncertainty?

First-Principles Model

Third Wave AI layers four ideas together. First: use neural operators—special learning programs designed to spot patterns between functions (relationships where the input and output are themselves curves or fields, like temperature over time or pressure over space) rather than just matching numbers to numbers. Second: make these programs output not just a guess but also a measure of uncertainty—how confident the computer should be in that guess. Third: borrow what we already know about physics, causation, or domain structure and bake it into how the model is built, so it respects those constraints. Fourth: combine data from many sources, each with different precision or cost (cheap satellite images plus expensive lab measurements, for example), and handle privacy by letting data stay distributed across hospitals or labs rather than gathering it all in one place. Bayesian methods (trial-and-error tuning that tracks not just the best guess but a full range of plausible answers) make the uncertainty real. Causal discovery methods read data to infer what actually causes what, not just what moves together.

Key Claims

  • Trustworthy AI for science and medicine requires prediction, uncertainty estimates, interpretability, and domain knowledge working together.
  • Neural operators can learn nonlinear relationships between curves, fields, and other function-like objects.
  • Learning programs built with Bayesian methods can output both an answer and a confidence estimate at the same time.
  • Combining data from many sources with different costs and accuracies (multifidelity learning) and keeping distributed data private (federated learning) expands what problems you can tackle.
  • Physics rules and causal stories, discovered or known in advance, can be woven into the model structure so it respects what's actually true.
  • Real examples: predicting COVID-19 spread with honest uncertainty ranges, finding what brain changes cause Alzheimer's symptoms.

Worked Example

COVID-19 epidemiology model (SEIR variant) for New York City: susceptible S, exposed E, infected I, hospitalized H, dead D, recovered R, vaccinated V compartments. Extended ODE system with 10 parameters (transmission β, hospitalization rate p, death rate q, etc.) calibrated as piecewise-constant over policy-driven events (stay-home order, reopening dining). Using daily case data (A), hospitalization (B), deaths (C), vaccine doses 1&2 (D) from Feb-Dec 2020, Bayesian neural network with Laplace approximation estimates posterior P(μ|data). Uncertainties quantified: β has ±15% credible interval, predictions of infections 3-month ahead show 95% uncertainty bands ±20% of mean. Three scenarios evaluated (no reopening, Feb-14 reopening, March-14 reopening) showing delayed reopening reduces peak infections by ~30%.

One Level Deeper

Physics-informed epidemiology: ODE residual L_ode = ||dS/dt - (-β SI/N)||² + ... enforces compartment conservation. Identifiability analysis checks if parameters are distinguishable given available data via Fisher information matrix rank. Sensitivity analysis computes ∂(deaths|day-90)/∂β, ∂p, ∂q to rank parameter importance. Robust (Bayesian) parameter estimation minimizes L = L_data + λ·L_prior + L_constraints, where L_prior = Σ(μ_j - μ_ref)²/σ²_j encodes prior belief, L_constraints = (dS/dt + dI/dt + ... - 0)² enforces conservation law. Memory-effect model: fractional-order ODE ∂^α u/∂t with α<1 introduces history-dependence via kernel K_α(t) ∝ t^(-α); captures delayed immune response or behavioral lag.

Failure Boundary

Parameter non-identifiability: if both transmission β and susceptible fraction S₀ affect early growth rate equally, data alone cannot distinguish them; posterior is degenerate. Model mismatch: real COVID variants (Alpha, Delta, Omicron) have different transmission rates; using single β across mutation-driven waves causes systematic bias. Policy change effects not in data: if new interventions (vaccination campaigns, testing) affect dynamics differently than historical data suggested, predictions fail. Long-term forecasting beyond 2-3 weeks breaks down due to behavioral changes, variant emergence, policy shifts not encoded in ODE; epistemic uncertainty grows exponentially. Noisy hospitalization/death data contains reporting delays and artifacts (weekend under-reporting); if not deconvolved, parameter estimates are biased.

Important Terms

Third Wave AI

The next phase of AI that combines learning from data with transparency (so you can see why it chose an answer), certainty estimates (how much to trust it), and respect for real-world physics and causation (not just pattern-matching in a black hole).

Neural Operator

A learning program shaped to spot patterns between functions—where inputs and outputs are curves, fields, or surfaces (like how temperature evolves over space and time) rather than just single numbers.

Bayesian Neural Operator

A neural operator that uses a method called Bayesian inference (keeping track of all plausible answers, not just one best guess) so it can output both a prediction and a range of uncertainty.

Multifidelity Learning

Training with data of mixed quality and cost—cheap measurements alongside expensive ones, or fast rough simulations alongside slow detailed ones—so you use the cheap data to guide patterns and the expensive data to check details.

Causal Discovery

Using algorithms on data to reverse-engineer what causes what, rather than just spotting which things move together (correlation). Is high blood sugar causing high insulin, or is inflammation causing both?

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk weaves together neural operators (learning between functions), uncertainty quantification (knowing how confident you should be), federated learning (keeping data private and distributed), and trustworthy machine learning—each a thread through the seminar.
  • It builds on earlier talks about safety and reliability by making uncertainty and interpretability core ingredients in the model itself, not afterthoughts.
  • It bridges scientific machine learning to public-health and biomedical decisions, showing how the same ideas that predict physical systems can guide real clinical choices.

Practical Caution

Adding error bars to predictions does not make a model trustworthy all by itself. The error estimates themselves can be wrong. The assumptions you make about what causes what may not hold in reality. The data you trained on may be biased or stale. Real-world feedback—what actually happened after you deployed the model—is your check: does the model's claimed uncertainty match how often it's actually wrong? Does the causal story hold up? All three layers (the learned pattern, the uncertainty measure, and the causal story) need to be tested, not assumed.

Takeaway

Scientific learning programs become ready to use in real decisions only when they deliver four things as a package: a prediction, a measure of how sure they should be, a story you can follow about why they chose that answer, and respect for the physics and domain knowledge we already have. Skip any one piece and the system is incomplete.
