Talk 36 · Columbia University

Steve Sun: Data-Driven Constitutive Updates From Model-Free Poroelasticity To Level Set Plasticity

This whole series asks: can computers predict how physical systems behave by learning from examples instead of only using hand-written math? This talk looks at one specific part—the material laws that say how a piece of rock or soil responds when you stretch it or squeeze it or push fluids through it—and shows how to teach those laws directly from experimental data.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

When you simulate how materials behave—a rock that's being squeezed, soil with water flowing through it, metal that's bending—you need equations that say how much the material deforms when you push on it, how pressure and flow are related, and what happens inside. For many real materials, nobody has written down these equations with confidence, or the experiments to measure them cost too much, or you only have a handful of data points. Using data directly to skip the written-down equation sounds simple, but the answer has to make physical sense and obey the laws of motion.

First-Principles Model

The material law (called a constitutive update—a local rule for how one small piece responds) lives inside a bigger simulation that solves how the whole thing moves and deforms. For poroelasticity—which means a solid rock or soil that squeezes and also lets water or oil flow through its pores—you can use data to learn: when the rock is pushed this hard, how much does it squish, and when there's a pressure difference, how fast does the fluid flow? Instead of writing an equation, a data-driven method looks at the actual data points and finds a pattern that makes physical sense. Neural networks (programs with millions of dials the computer tunes by trial and error until their answers look right) or level-set functions (surfaces that slide and change shape to track where a material stops bending elastically and starts deforming permanently) can do this learning when you only have limited measurements.

Key Claims

  • Using data and neural networks to learn how materials behave has been done for decades.
  • The goal is to make the learned material law readable (so you can see what it's doing) and to work with the limited experimental data you actually collected.
  • Simulating fluids moving through solids requires two equations: one for how the solid deforms and one for how the fluid flows.
  • Depending on how much data you have, you can switch between a data-driven learned rule and a hand-written equation inside the same simulation.
  • Fast computer tricks (like KD-trees, which split space into smaller boxes to speed up searching) can make data-driven updates run quickly enough for real simulations.
  • Level-set plasticity (using a moving surface to mark where material stops bending elastically) can learn yield behavior from higher-resolution simulations when you don't have direct experiments.

Worked Example

Model-free poroelasticity on sandstone with naturally high variance porosity-permeability relationships (variance ~1 order on log scale). Hybrid approach uses model-based J2 plasticity for solid mechanics (tractable stress-strain from triaxial tests) and model-free search for hydraulic response. Heterogeneous domain with porosity fluctuations generates pole pressure buildup; at each quadrature point, k-d tree search identifies nearest material database point in transformed space, replacing smooth prediction with discrete data interpolation. Stress relaxation test on linear problem validates implementation; nonlinear comparison shows model-free response oscillations when data coverage sparse, resolved by adding database points.

Level-set plasticity: neural network trained to identify yield surface evolution with cumulative plastic strain. Hamilton-Jacobi PDE solved once offline; result reusable across many simulations replacing classical return-mapping. Trained on polycrystal elastoplasticity; comparison shows results nearly identical to FFT simulation but without expensive per-quadrature-point FFT evaluation.

One Level Deeper

Model-free approach minimizes distance from physical constraint manifold (balance of linear momentum, balance of mass) to material data manifold. Optimization has two steps: (1) Continuous: for fixed data point, find physical field minimizing residual, solved via Lagrangian multiplier; (2) Discrete: identify closest material point in database by k-d tree search using factorized energy norm. Energy norm uses c = inverse of compliance tensor; rewrite as c = L^T L via Cholesky, then transform material space and apply L2 distance to accelerate k-d tree (line 599-612). Hybrid formulation: when constitutive law tractable, use model-based approach; when high variance or complex, switch to model-free for that variable only.

Level-set plasticity formulates yield-surface evolution as Hamilton-Jacobi equation with state = principal stress, parameter = cumulative plastic strain. Neural network solves once to give u-surface implicit representation. Return-mapping uses this learned yield function + learned elastic energy. Enforce physics via material frame indifference test (rotation tensor in deformation gradient unchanged), acoustic tensor eigenvalue check (wave speed > 0 for stability), and symmetry verification. Training uses multi-layer perceptron with relu then hybrid activation (scaled relu layers) to achieve sufficient C² continuity for tangent stiffness derivatives (line 1077-1102).

Failure Boundary

Model-free approach fails when database has gaps: forcing solution outside data cloud produces oscillations (line 687-712, 851-862). Manifold-based distance (not norm-based) required for nonlinear constitutive response; standard L2 norm may prefer wrong points on curved manifold (line 815-828). Different material models in overlap region incompatible; must position decomposition carefully (from dive 036).

Level-set approach: LSTM recurrent networks for yield-surface evolution show oscillations with limited data (line 1276-1289); multilayer perceptron with level-set avoids these. Assumption that yield surface is smooth (scientist function) is strong—some hardening paths may not satisfy this. Interpolation to points outside training stress space unreliable (line 1673-1688 physics-informed NN example: PINNs fail on stiff equations, longer times, parametric changes).

Important Terms

Constitutive Law

An equation or model that says how a material responds locally: when you push on it (stress and strain), how much does it deform, and for fluids, how pressure and flow relate.

Model-Free Mechanics

Simulation that uses measured material data directly instead of writing down a specific equation first.

Poroelasticity

The behavior when a solid material (rock or soil) squeezes and deforms at the same time that a fluid (water or oil) flows through its tiny connected holes.

Constitutive Manifold

The set of all physically real material states—either measured in experiments or captured by a learned model.

Level-Set Plasticity

A method to represent when material stops bending temporarily and starts bending permanently (yielding) by using a moving surface that marks the boundary.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture adds material-law learning to the broader goal of the series: the learned model is the material response, not the entire physics rule.
  • It connects to hybrid systems because mixing data and written-down physics works smoothest at the material-response level.
  • It also connects to model-constrained learning because the full simulation (the big math problem of motion and deformation) decides which learned material responses are actually allowed without breaking.

Practical Caution

A material law learned from data can break the full simulation if it says something physically impossible (like negative energy), if the math becomes inconsistent step by step, or if it wastes or creates energy it shouldn't. The learned material law must be tested inside the complete coupled calculation—soil deforming AND water flowing through it together—not just checked against a graph of stress-versus-strain in isolation.

Takeaway

The insight is that data-driven mechanics often does its job at the material-law level—the local response of one small piece of material. Learn what the material does from measurements, but make sure it fits with the overall physics rules.
