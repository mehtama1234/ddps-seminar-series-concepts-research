Talk 85 · University of Iowa

H. S. Udaykumar: Data-Driven Multi-Scale Simulations For Energetic Materials

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. When you design an explosive or rocket fuel, the material's behavior depends on tiny details at many different scales — from atoms up to the size of the whole chunk. This talk is about how to stitch predictions from the smallest scales all the way up to the biggest ones using a computer program that learns which effects at each scale matter most for the final result.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Explosives and rocket fuels are sensitive: whether they burn safely or badly depends on what's happening at atoms, at grains you can almost see, at clumps of grains, and at the whole piece. Running full atomistic simulations (computing forces between all atoms) for something the size of an actual device is impossible — it would take centuries of computer time. So you need predictions that cross scales: simulations at one scale must inform simulations at the next bigger scale. Each jump from a smaller scale to a larger scale loses detail: how do you capture what matters from the details you're throwing away?

First-Principles Model

To design these materials, you need to know: what structure (atoms arranged how?) makes what properties (hardness, how fast it burns, how sensitive to shock), and what properties lead to what final performance (safe ignition, full energy release). Multiscale simulation climbs upward: simulations at the atom level tell you what happens in messy grain boundaries, those predictions feed into medium-scale simulations that show how a bunch of grains behave together, and those feed into full-device simulations. The hard part is that each jump up requires a summary: a formula or a learned model that captures the small-scale effects your bigger model can't resolve. Machine learning trains on data from smaller-scale simulations to learn these summaries automatically, then uses them to quickly predict how different material designs will perform.

Key Claims

  • Sensitivity in explosives and rocket fuels arises from physics happening at atoms, at grain boundaries, at grain clumps, and at the whole piece — and these scales are linked.
  • You cannot run atom-by-atom simulations for real-sized objects, so smaller-scale simulations must train models that predict the effects larger scales care about.
  • Computer programs that learn from examples can create these connecting models (summaries of unresolved small-scale effects) instead of hand-writing them.
  • The chain from material structure to material properties to final performance is the map you need for design.
  • Building a library of medium-scale simulations and fitting learned models to them is one way to create these structure-to-performance maps.
  • Deep learning, reusing knowledge across similar materials, and mixing cheap and expensive simulations can speed up learning across different energetic materials.

Important Terms

Energetic Material

A solid or liquid that stores energy in its chemical bonds and releases it rapidly when disturbed or ignited — examples: explosives, rocket propellants, pyrotechnic powders.

Scale Bridging

Passing what you learned from simulations at one size scale (say, atom-scale) into simulations at a bigger size scale (say, grain-scale), so smaller-scale details can shape larger-scale predictions.

Data-Driven Closure

A formula or learned model (trained by a computer program on data) that summarizes small-scale effects that a bigger-scale simulation cannot see but needs to account for.

Structure-Property-Performance

The chain of cause and effect: the way atoms are arranged determines material properties (like hardness or burn rate), and those properties determine how the material actually behaves in use (whether it ignites safely or too slowly).

Multifidelity Modeling

Using a mix of simulations that differ in accuracy and speed: expensive atom-scale simulations that are very accurate, cheaper medium-scale simulations that are faster, and faster formulas that are rough but quick — and teaching a learning program to combine them well.

Connections Across The Series

  • This work connects learning applied to material structure with learning how to bridge scales correctly, the key theme of the series.
  • It pairs with talks on building digital twins and optimizing manufacturing by showing how to create design loops that predict material performance from first principles.
  • It reinforces the series' core problem: bridging scales and learning unresolved effects accurately is what separates predictions that work from ones that fail.

Practical Caution

When you learn these summaries from one energetic material, you might hope to reuse them for a similar one. But similar materials can fail differently under shock — the small-scale details that break one may not break the other. Reusing a learned summary from one material to predict another sounds like a shortcut, but you must check whether the underlying physics at all scales is really the same.

Takeaway

Designing materials is really a multiscale closure problem: at each step from atoms up to the device, you must represent the effects you cannot see accurately. Machine learning shines when it learns the right small-scale effects and carries them faithfully up the chain.
