Talk 1 · University of Pennsylvania

Paris Perdikaris: When And Why PINNs Fail To Train

This lecture explains that physics-informed neural networks fail not because the idea of adding physics is wrong, but because the resulting optimization problem can become stiff, spectrally biased, and badly balanced across loss terms.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

PINNs turn a PDE problem into a composite neural-network training problem: fit data, boundary conditions, initial conditions, and PDE residuals at the same time. That sounds elegant, but the optimizer may not give each constraint useful gradient signal, especially for high-frequency, multiscale, or boundary-sensitive solutions.

First-Principles Model

A PINN represents the unknown field with a differentiable neural network. Automatic differentiation evaluates derivatives, the PDE residual becomes a loss term, and gradient descent updates the network. The real object being solved is therefore not just the PDE; it is a constrained optimization dynamics. If that dynamics is stiff or biased, the PDE can be simple while training still fails.

Key Claims

  • Physics can be inserted through architecture-level inductive bias or through loss-level regularization; PINNs mostly use the second route.
  • PINNs are unconventional regularizers because they regularize the function output and its derivatives rather than just the network weights.
  • Even simple linear elliptic problems can fail if the training dynamics cannot satisfy boundary conditions or high-frequency structure.
  • PINN gradient flow can become stiff, forcing impractically small learning rates if trained naively.
  • Different loss terms can produce imbalanced gradients, so one constraint can dominate while another receives almost no useful update.
  • Neural tangent kernel analysis helps diagnose spectral bias: low-frequency components are learned faster than high-frequency components.

Important Terms

Physics-Informed Neural Network

A neural network trained to represent a solution field while penalizing violations of a governing differential equation and its boundary or initial conditions.

Composite Loss

A training objective made from several terms, such as data mismatch, boundary loss, initial-condition loss, and PDE residual loss.

Gradient Flow Stiffness

A training pathology where the continuous-time optimization dynamics contains widely separated scales, making stable gradient descent require tiny steps.

Gradient Imbalance

A failure mode where different loss components produce very different gradient magnitudes, so some constraints are effectively ignored.

Spectral Bias

The tendency of standard neural networks trained by gradient descent to learn low-frequency functions before high-frequency functions.

Connections Across The Series

  • This is the natural starting point for the playlist because it separates the PINN idea from PINN trainability.
  • It connects to later talks on weak forms, adaptive weighting, operator learning, and structure-preserving architectures.
  • It gives a diagnostic mindset: do not ask only whether the physics loss is correct; ask whether the optimizer can actually use it.

Practical Caution

Do not treat a small PDE residual term as proof that the learned field is physically reliable. The loss weights, collocation points, network architecture, optimizer, and frequency content can all change whether the method is solving the intended problem.

Takeaway

The first-principles lesson is that PINNs are optimization systems, not magic PDE solvers. Their success depends on whether the training dynamics can transmit the right physical constraints into the right function components.
