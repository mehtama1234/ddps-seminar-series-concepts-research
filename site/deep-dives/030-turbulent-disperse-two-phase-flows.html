Talk 30 · University of Michigan

Jesse Capecelatro: Turbulent Disperse Two-Phase Flows

This lecture studies turbulent gas-particle flows where particles strongly change the carrier turbulence, and the math model must account for how the two phases change each other.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Dispersed two-phase flows happen everywhere: smokestacks, spray engines, ocean sediment. But modeling them is hard because particles interact with turbulence, create wakes behind them, bump into each other, pile up locally, and hit walls. The physics of a single fluid alone does not capture what happens when you add many particles that are not moving with the fluid.

First-Principles Model

A gas-particle flow has two parts: a carrier fluid (the gas) and a dispersed particle phase (the small drops or solids). Each particle feels drag and other forces from the fluid, but each particle also pushes back on the fluid through wakes and momentum transfer. When you write the math for the average flow and average turbulence, you end up with terms you cannot close because the small-scale details matter. Data-driven methods can learn those missing pieces while keeping the known physics intact.

Key Claims

  • Particles can change background turbulence by a factor of two or more.
  • Most multiphase average-flow models have been simple additions to single-fluid turbulence models.
  • These additions do not work when particle concentration swings from very low to very high, where the two-way effects become large.
  • Data-driven modeling now appears in more applications: learning closures, guessing model errors, and fixing models when physics is incomplete.
  • The talk shows approaches beyond just using neural networks to guess closure terms directly.
  • Adding chance into particle-force models can help the math keep the right particle speed variations and how far particles spread.

Important Terms

Dispersed Two-Phase Flow

A flow where droplets or solid particles are suspended and spread throughout a liquid or gas.

Two-Way Coupling

When the fluid pushes on particles AND particles push back hard on the fluid, changing how the fluid itself moves.

Multiphase RANS

Math that averages out the small wiggles in a two-phase flow to find the mean gas and mean particle motion.

Closure Modeling

A method to approximate terms that show up in the averaged equations because you averaged out the small-scale details.

Stochastic Drag/Force Model

A model where particle forces include randomness to match what you measure in real or simulated flows.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk continues the theme from earlier lectures: when you average or filter the equations, you lose information, and you have to model what you lost. Here it is two-phase flows; before it was turbulence alone.
  • It touches data-driven model repair because the learned part targets just the missing pieces, not the whole solution.
  • It shows a real case where forgetting that particles and fluid change each other leads to big prediction errors.

Practical Caution

A math model trained on single-fluid turbulence or on one particle density regime will often fail when the particle concentration changes or when particles reshape the flow noticeably. Always test across both particle-sparse and particle-dense conditions and check whether the model predicts the way particles spread and move, not just the average gas velocity.

Takeaway

The core idea is that multiphase turbulence modeling is really about getting statistics of particle and fluid interaction right: particles are not just tiny objects drifting along; they actively reshape how the turbulence behaves.
