Talk 30 · University of Michigan

Jesse Capecelatro: Turbulent Disperse Two-Phase Flows

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. One problem: when you have a gas or liquid with particles floating through it—like smoke in air or spray from an engine—the particles and the fluid push on each other in complicated ways. This talk asks: how do you write a math rule that captures both what the fluid does AND what the particles do, when they're constantly talking to each other?

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Dispersed two-phase flows show up everywhere you look: smoke stacks, spray engines, sediment stirred up in the ocean. The puzzle is hard because particles do more than just float along. Each particle stirs up a wake behind it, gets shoved around by the fluid's churning, bumps into other particles, clumps together in spots, and bounces off walls. If you only write down the math for the fluid alone, you miss all of this. Add real particles to the mix, and suddenly the old math stops working because the particles actually change how the fluid churns.

First-Principles Model

A gas-particle flow splits into two players: the carrier gas (which does all the churning) and the particles scattered through it (the tiny drops or solid bits). Each particle feels a drag force from the fluid trying to slow it down, but here's the key: each particle also shoves back on the gas through its wake and by stealing momentum. When you try to write down one averaged rule that works everywhere instead of tracking every particle separately, you run into a wall. The averaged equations have terms you cannot compute without knowing the tiny details that got averaged away. This is where learning from data comes in: train a program to guess those missing pieces while keeping all the physics you already know.

Key Claims

  • Particles can change the intensity of turbulent churning by a factor of two or more.
  • Most models for averaged flows just tack on particle terms to the single-fluid equations without rethinking the whole picture.
  • Those tacked-on additions break down when particle density swings from very sparse to very dense, where the push-back from particles becomes too big to ignore.
  • Learning from data is now being used in real applications: training a program to guess missing closure terms, detecting when the model is drifting from reality, and patching models when you don't know all the physics.
  • The talk shows ways to use learning that go beyond having a program simply guess a single number for each missing term.
  • Putting randomness into particle-force models helps the math keep the right spread in particle speeds and how far particles get scattered.

Important Terms

Dispersed Two-Phase Flow

A flow where tiny droplets or solid particles are spread throughout and tumbling around inside a liquid or gas.

Two-Way Coupling

When the fluid pushes on particles AND the particles push back on the fluid hard enough to change how the fluid moves—neither one ignores the other.

Multiphase RANS

A rule that averages out all the tiny wiggles in a two-phase flow to find the overall average gas motion and overall average particle motion, instead of tracking every speck.

Closure Modeling

The job of guessing or approximating the pieces that show up in your averaged equations because the tiny details that were averaged away are actually mattering.

Stochastic Drag/Force Model

A rule for particle forces that includes randomness—tiny random nudges—to match the real jiggling and spreading that you see in actual or simulated flows.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk carries forward a pattern from earlier talks in the series: whenever you average a rule or filter it down, you throw away information, and you have to guess what you threw away. Here you're averaging a two-phase flow; before it was one-fluid turbulence.
  • It connects to data-driven model repair, where the learned part only patches the missing pieces instead of learning the whole answer from scratch.
  • It shows a real-world case where forgetting that particles and fluid push on each other leads to huge errors in what your model predicts.

Practical Caution

A math model trained only on flowing gas (no particles) or on flows where particles are spread thin will often fail when you switch to dense swarms of particles that really change how the gas behaves. Before you trust any model, always check it against both sparse cases (few particles) and dense cases (lots of particles). Test whether it gets right not just the average gas speed but also how wide the particles spread out and where they actually go—not just which way they're drifting.

Takeaway

The real problem in multiphase turbulence is not guessing the particles or guessing the fluid—it's getting the statistics of how particles and fluid trade momentum right. Particles are not just hitchhikers. They actively reshape the turbulent eddies and swirls that the fluid makes.
