Talk 91 · University of Cambridge

Alice Cicirello: Integrating Physics Knowledge In Machine Learning Strategies

This whole series is about getting computers to predict physical behavior by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. One popular approach is to feed physics knowledge into that learning at four different points: the data you collect, the shape of the learning program itself, how you train it, and a separate model that catches where your physics rules fall short. This talk uses friction-force identification to show how all four work together.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Learning programs trained only on data often fail when faced with new situations, make predictions that break physics (like a ball falling upward), and struggle when you have few measurements or little data. Real engineering problems give you some physics knowledge—some equations you know, some you don't—along with measurements that are noisy and scarce. The question is: where and how should you inject physics knowledge into your learning process?

First-Principles Model

Physics knowledge can enter learning at four places. Observational bias shapes the data by choosing what to measure or creating artificial measurements that physics tells you matter. Inductive bias builds physics rules, symmetries, or conservation laws directly into how the learning program is structured. Learning bias changes the training process itself—what cost the program tries to minimize, rules it absolutely cannot break, or how it searches for the best answer. Discrepancy bias adds a separate program that catches the gap between your partial physics equations and what you actually see. Friction identification shows how finding which forces matter most and using a method that represents uncertainty in predictions can use all four types together.

Key Claims

  • Physics knowledge improves prediction on new situations when you have small to medium amounts of training data, and can enforce that predictions respect physics even with lots of data.
  • Training only on data without physics can hide biases in your data and produce impossible predictions.
  • You can control data bias by collecting measurements through physics-guided choices or creating new artificial examples that physics tells you should exist.
  • You can embed prior knowledge such as boundaries, conservation laws (energy or momentum never disappears), and symmetries (unchanged behavior under transformations) directly into the program's structure.
  • You can change the training process: the cost you minimize, hard rules the program must follow, or the algorithm you use to find the best answer.
  • You can use partial physics knowledge or fix known gaps between simplified equations and real data, as shown through friction-force identification.

Important Terms

Observational Bias

Physics-informed choices about which data to measure, which to create artificially, or which to emphasize when training a learning program.

Inductive Bias

A built-in assumption or constraint that the learning program carries from the start, woven into its structure or how it represents information.

Learning Bias

Physics information introduced through the cost function (what you minimize), hard rules the program must satisfy, or the search method itself.

Discrepancy Bias

A separate program trained to catch the gap between an imperfect physics model and real observations.

Friction Identification

Finding friction forces or friction rules in a moving system from data and physics constraints, when friction is the main unknown quantity.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture gives a clear taxonomy that unifies earlier talks on physics-informed learning, physics-guided learning, and physics-enhanced learning.
  • It connects to physics-informed neural networks (learning programs that include physics rules), finding the few most important forces in a system, learning methods with physics starting-points, and models that catch gaps between simplified physics and real data.
  • It gives precise language for deciding where physics should enter a program, moving past loose advice to simply add physics.

Practical Caution

Adding physics knowledge carelessly can fail or give wrong answers. For example, adding a soft penalty (a gentle nudge in the cost function) may not work when a physical law must never be broken—you need a hard constraint built into the structure itself.

Takeaway

Physics can enter learning at four points: the data, the program's structure, how you train it, and the discrepancy model. Good physics-informed learning chooses where to insert physics deliberately rather than piling it in everywhere.
