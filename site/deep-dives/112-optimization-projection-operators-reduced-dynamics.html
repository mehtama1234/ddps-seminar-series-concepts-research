Talk 112 · University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign

Alberto Padovan: Model Reduction via Optimization of Projection Operators and Reduced-Order Dynamics

This lecture shows how to learn oblique projection operators and reduced-order dynamics directly from data without access to the underlying equations.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Data-driven model reduction usually starts with orthogonal POD (Proper Orthogonal Decomposition—finding the strongest patterns in measurement data). But when a system has large sudden swings in its transient behavior, projection along the orthogonal directions can actually distort the reduced dynamics even though POD identified the right low-dimensional subspace. Systems with opaque solvers are still attractive for model reduction, yet the standard approach falls short.

First-Principles Model

Projection-based reduced models need two things: a low-dimensional subspace to project onto, and a projection direction that determines how the full state maps down. Orthogonal projection (perpendicular directions) is simple but may not preserve the important behaviors you're trying to capture. Oblique projection (directions not perpendicular to the subspace) can keep input-output relationships or transient dynamics intact, but it normally requires direct access to the governing equations to compute. This method learns both the oblique projection operators and polynomial reduced equations from measurement trajectories by solving an optimization problem on special curved spaces called Grassmann and Stiefel manifolds, which respect the geometric constraints of projection operators.

Key Claims

  • Data-driven model reduction becomes possible when the solver is opaque and you cannot access the underlying equations.
  • Large sudden changes in transient behavior make orthogonal projections fail even when POD finds the correct subspace.
  • Oblique projection operators can capture dynamics more accurately than orthogonal ones.
  • Standard oblique projections require direct access to the governing equations, limiting their use.
  • This method identifies both projection operators and reduced equations directly from measurement data.
  • Polynomial reduced equations and optimization on curved spaces (Grassmann and Stiefel manifolds) provide the mathematical structure for solving this problem.

Important Terms

Oblique Projection

A projection onto a subspace where the projection direction is not perpendicular to that subspace.

Proper Orthogonal Decomposition

A method that extracts the strongest repeating patterns from a collection of measurements by finding the directions along which data varies the most.

Transient Growth

Temporary amplification of small disturbances before the system settles down, even if it is stable in the long run.

Grassmann Manifold

A curved mathematical space where each point represents a choice of subspace with a fixed number of dimensions.

Polynomial Reduced Dynamics

Simplified equations where the rate of change is given as a polynomial (sum of powers and products) of the reduced variables.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture ties together data-driven model reduction, projection geometry, transient behavior, and optimization on curved spaces.
  • It builds on balanced truncation and control-oriented model reduction by showing that the projection geometry itself is a design choice, not just a byproduct.
  • It also addresses settings where the solver is opaque and the governing equations are unavailable, making intrusive methods impossible.

Practical Caution

Learning oblique projections from data can boost accuracy, but it introduces challenges: the learned projection may not be unique, and the optimization can get stuck in local minima. Always test the learned projection on new measurement trajectories outside the data used to train it.

Takeaway

The core lesson is that model reduction is not just about picking the right subspace—the way you project matters as much as what subspace you choose. Different projection directions can mean the difference between a reduced model that predicts well and one that fails.
