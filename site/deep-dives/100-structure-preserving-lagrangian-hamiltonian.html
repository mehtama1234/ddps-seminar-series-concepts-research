Talk 100 · University of California San Diego

Boris Kramer: Structure-Preserving Learning of High-Dimensional Lagrangian and Hamiltonian Systems

This whole series is about getting computers to predict physical behavior by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. When we shrink a big, expensive simulation down to a smaller one, a natural question arises: should we keep the original system's hidden structure—the rules about energy staying constant, or momentum staying constant, or symmetries that the real system respects—or just make predictions that look right on past data? This talk shows why keeping that structure leads to better, more stable long-term predictions than predictions that only match old examples.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Real mechanical systems (like bridges flexing, robots moving, or fluids flowing) are expensive to simulate step by step. When we build a smaller, cheaper replacement that mimics the original, a problem emerges: if the replacement breaks the rules about energy staying constant, momentum staying constant, symmetry, or stability, those broken rules seem harmless at first but compound over time into terrible predictions far ahead. A dumb copy that just reproduces numbers breaks these rules and fails when you try to predict far into the future.

First-Principles Model

Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics are two languages for writing down how systems move. Both use core quantities: the energy you pump into the system (kinetic, from motion) and the energy locked inside (potential, from position); the momentum in each direction; and generalized coordinates—the absolute minimum number of values you need to specify what the system looks like right now. When you shrink a system, a structure-preserving approach does two things: first, it reduces the dimension (throws away detail that matters less), then it learns the motion in a space where the same mechanical form holds as in the original. By forcing the smaller version to obey the original mechanical structure, the learning process needs far fewer examples and builds predictions that stay stable and correct over long time horizons compared to a dumb copy that discards the structure and just tunes itself to past data.

Key Claims

  • Many real scientific and engineering systems are naturally described using Lagrangian or Hamiltonian mechanics.
  • Quantities such as momentum, energy, and vorticity (rotation in fluid flow) are not hindsight measurements; they are built into how the system actually works.
  • Structure-preserving smaller models work for structural mechanics (beams and buildings), soft robotics (flexible arms and grippers), and wave motion (ripples and vibrations).
  • Preserving symmetry or keeping matrices that always point in consistent directions improves how far ahead you can predict accurately.
  • Structured methods use less training data than methods that ignore structure.
  • The goal is not small error on old data but keeping dynamics that stay stable and predictable when you push far into the future.

Worked Example

A double pendulum (2 DOF Lagrangian system, 4D phase space) exhibits chaotic motion. Traditional RK4 integrator with step size 0.01 remains stable for 1000 timesteps. Unconstrained neural network trained to predict state evolution σ(x_t) → x_t+Δt diverges after 50 steps (exponential error growth). Structure-preserving network (Hamiltonian version) trained identically, stays stable for 500+ steps with same test error magnitude at early times. Over 10 simulated seconds: RK4 total energy error 0.002%, structure-preserving network 0.001%, unconstrained network >50% (unphysical). A 3-pendulum system (6D, high-dimensional Lagrangian), trained on 2000 demonstrations, structure-preserving reaches <5% long-term error; unconstrained reaches >100% after 50 steps.

Double-well potential (1D system with 2 stable equilibria): energy is E = ½ṗ² + V(q) where V(q) = (q²-1)². Learned Hamiltonian net exactly preserves E (loss enforces dH/dt=0 by construction); traditional PINN with MSE loss has energy drift ~0.05 per 100 steps.

One Level Deeper

Hamiltonian structure: H(p,q) = kinetic + potential energy, dynamics ṗ = -∂H/∂q, q̇ = ∂H/∂p. Neural net learns H directly; forward Euler integrator becomes symplectic-like (approximately preserves phase space volume). Lagrangian form: L = T(q̇) - V(q), Euler-Lagrange equation d/dt(∂L/∂q̇) - ∂L/∂q = 0 naturally enforces conservation laws. Constraint: if Lagrangian structure known (e.g., free particle T=½m q̇², learned potential V_nn), construct loss to enforce correct form, not just prediction error. Riemannian geometry: symplectic structure (canonical 2-form ω = dp∧dq) defines geometry; integrators that respect ω preserve energy/action.

Implementation: Use automatic differentiation to compute ∂H/∂p, ∂H/∂q; propagate via Hamilton's equations as differentiable ODE. Lagrange multiplier method: augment loss with penalty ||dH/dt||² to encourage energy conservation, or use Lagrange multipliers to enforce conservation exactly during training.

Failure Boundary

Structure-preserving networks require knowing which conserved quantities exist (energy, momentum, angular momentum). If system has hidden conserved quantities not encoded, network cannot discover them—it only preserves explicitly enforced ones. Dissipative systems (damping, friction) violate Hamiltonian structure; forced approach fails. High-dimensional systems (>10 DOF): computational cost of computing Jacobians (symplectic check) scales poorly. Long-time prediction: even energy-conserving networks can have phase drift (correct energy but wrong trajectory)—chaotic instability still causes exponential error growth. Hybrid systems with discrete events (collisions) violate smoothness assumption of Hamiltonian framework. Coupling to external fields: if system is coupled to time-varying external potential (not autonomous), Hamiltonian is time-dependent, energy not conserved—requires extended framework. Non-conservative perturbations (wind resistance ~v²) cannot be represented as exact Hamiltonian.

Important Terms

Lagrangian Mechanics

A method of writing how systems move by tracking the difference between kinetic energy (motion) and potential energy (stored up), then using the idea that nature picks paths where the total action (energy summed across time) reaches an extreme point.

Hamiltonian Mechanics

A method of writing how systems move by tracking total energy and treating position and momentum symmetrically in a special space where the geometry lets you see hidden patterns, like which quantities never change.

Structure-Preserving ROM

A smaller approximation built to keep the physical rules and geometric patterns of the original system intact—so that energy or momentum conservation still holds, even in the reduced version.

Long-Time Prediction

Making guesses about what happens far in the future, where tiny errors in the rules—even ones invisible at first—accumulate and grow into total failures compared to what actually happens.

Positive Definiteness

A property of matrices and operators (mathematical transformations that move data around) that shows up when a problem has a guaranteed solution, or when energy cannot become negative, or when forces point in consistent directions.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture combines geometric mechanics (the mathematics of shapes and symmetries in motion), smaller models, operators that transform entire functions, and scientific machine learning into one cohesive picture.
  • It extends the thinking around symplectic structure (a geometric design that keeps certain relationships unchanging) and physics-guided learning by putting conservation and structure front and center rather than as afterthoughts.
  • It reinforces the seminar's running theme: building in what you already know about the problem shrinks how much training data you need to learn well.

Practical Caution

Structure preservation only works if the structure you pick actually describes the system and if the way you split time into steps matches that structure. Choosing the wrong mechanical form hands you false confidence: predictions look smooth and calm, but they are wrong.

Takeaway

The central lesson in mechanics is that respecting the shape of the equations matters more than just making numbers match the next observed state.
