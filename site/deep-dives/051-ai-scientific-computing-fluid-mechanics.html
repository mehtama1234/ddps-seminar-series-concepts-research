Talk 51 · Harvard University

Petros Koumoutsakos: Artificial Intelligence And Scientific Computing For Fluid Mechanics

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples. Fluids — air, water, wind — are especially hard because they change in countless connected ways at the same time. This talk is about why letting a learning program (a system that tunes itself by trial and error) predict fluid motion alone often fails, and shows how to build a hybrid: mix the trial-and-error learning with traditional step-by-step math solvers that engineers have used for decades, then pick the right balance between them.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Fluid mechanics studies how water and air move and push on things around them. The challenge has many layers: motion is nonlinear (small changes can cause huge effects), chaos shows up at many size scales at once (tiny swirls mix with big currents), turbulence (chaotic, irregular motion) dominates, and objects in the fluid push back on it as it pushes on them. Over decades, engineers built slow, careful step-by-step math (numerical solvers — programs that predict what happens next by tiny increments) to handle this. Now people want learning programs (systems that tune themselves by trial and error on data) to do the same job, faster. The real problem is: how do you combine old math solvers, new learning programs, and human knowledge to predict and steer fluids in ways you can trust?

First-Principles Model

Two tools exist. The old tool: step-by-step math solvers. They follow the rules of physics exactly (or nearly) and know how to avoid numerical crashes if you set them up right. The new tool: learning programs with millions of adjustable dials that learn patterns by trial and error on data. They are flexible and can spot trends humans miss, but they have no built-in physics knowledge. The smart move is to combine them: use the solver to generate data or to check that the learning program's answer makes physical sense; use the learning program to learn a smaller description of the full motion (so you only track what matters); and use reinforcement learning (learning by trial and reward signals from a simulated environment) to discover good ways to steer the fluid.

Key Claims

  • AI has grown because of more powerful computers and more available data, but claims about AI for fluids need hard proof to separate fact from hope.
  • Complex flows and steering should combine learning programs with numerical solvers rather than trust learning alone.
  • One successful hybrid approach: use detailed or high-resolution simulations to teach a smaller model that still captures the important motion patterns.
  • The equation-free method (a technique developed years ago by Kevrekidis and others) showed how to learn smaller descriptions of complex systems; this is a direct ancestor of modern learned reduced models.
  • Multiple independent agents learning together (each adjusting actions based on rewards from a simulation) can solve fluid steering and interaction problems when mixed with scientific computing.
  • Both successes and failures matter because they show whether the underlying physics structure is truly necessary.

Important Terms

Computing Alloy

A calculation system that weaves together step-by-step math solvers, learned models, trial-and-error tuning, and traditional physics knowledge into one integrated process.

Effective Dynamics

A simplified description that captures how a complex system evolves at the particular scale or detail level you actually care about.

Flow-Structure Interaction

The coupled dance between a moving fluid and solid objects inside it that can shift position or bend in response to the fluid's push.

Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

A method where several independent entities each learn by taking actions in an environment and receiving reward signals, often learning to work together or compete.

Scientific Computing

Using algorithms and computers to solve the step-by-step math rules that describe physical systems, turning abstract equations into numerical predictions.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture builds directly on earlier work by Kevrekidis about equation-free methods and effective dynamics, adapting those ideas specifically to fluid motion.
  • It connects to later talks about surrogate acceleration because the goal is not just speed but keeping predictions accurate and staying in control.
  • It also joins with talks on reinforcement learning in scientific control, where simulations become the training ground for learning strategies.

Practical Caution

In fluids, disaster hides in the details. A learned model might predict one test case perfectly, then fail completely when conditions shift slightly. Predictions can drift over hours of simulated time because tiny mistakes compound. When you feed a learned prediction back into a real system — steering a plane wing, for example — small errors can grow fast. Any hybrid system must include checks: does the prediction violate conservation laws? Does it stay stable over long times? Does control actually work when tested?

Takeaway

The lesson is that AI for fluids must be built as a partnership between step-by-step math solvers and learning programs, with continuous checks that the answers make physical sense. The real question is not whether to use AI or solvers, but how to combine them so the result is stable, stays accurate, and can actually be controlled.
