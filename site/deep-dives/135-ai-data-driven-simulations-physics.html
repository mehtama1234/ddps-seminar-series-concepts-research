Talk 135 · ETH Zurich

Siddhartha Mishra: AI for Data-Driven Simulations in Physics

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave—air over a wing, heat in metal, water sloshing—by learning from examples instead of hand-written math. This talk focuses on a particular way to do that: instead of teaching a learning program the physics rules line by line, we give it examples of 'before and after' from a simulator and let it learn the whole jump from start to finish in one go, like learning a shortcut through a forest by walking it many times.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Rules that describe how things change moment to moment and place to place—heat spreading through a bar, air pressure around an airplane—are the backbone of physics and engineering. But running the exact step-by-step calculations to solve these rules takes hours or days on a computer, even for a single prediction. A faster substitute would save time and money, but it faces a hard challenge: a learning program trained on one set of starting conditions or materials must somehow predict correctly for different starting conditions, different materials, different shapes—situations it never trained on.

First-Principles Model

The idea is to teach a learning program (with millions of little dials tuned by trial and error until its answers look right) to be a shortcut itself. Feed it the starting conditions, material properties, and geometry of a problem; the program outputs the full solution instantly. The talk walks through three main recipes: (1) sliding-filter designs that sweep across the data to find patterns, (2) attention designs that ask 'which parts of the input matter most for this prediction', and (3) mesh-aware networks that work on irregular grids (like the cracks in a real airplane wing) instead of clean square grids. It also covers a recent method: building the solution step by step by removing noise, steered by the input conditions.

Key Claims

  • Rules that describe how things change are the language of physics and engineering.
  • Solving these rules by hand-written step-by-step math takes too long for practical work.
  • Learning programs can be trained to jump straight from starting conditions to solution, skipping the slow calculations.
  • Teaching a learning program to do this is now a major direction in machine learning.
  • Sliding filters and attention are the two winning designs so far.
  • Newer methods that build solutions layer by layer (like removing fog to reveal a landscape) are opening new doors, especially for generating many different possible solutions instead of just one.

Worked Example

DrivAero dataset (MIT): 8,000 car shapes, each ~2 million surface nodes, RANS simulation took 300 node-hours per sample to generate; geometry-aware operator transformer inference: 0.4 seconds, achieving significantly better accuracy than leaderboard models. DrivAeroML (Neil Ashton, Nvidia): 500 car shapes, 10 million surface nodes, ALES (unstructured) fidelity. Turbulent DNS at (tailor-green initial condition perturbed): 64³ discretization resolves multiscale, chaotic Navier-Stokes; diffusion-based model recovers full variance and spectral statistics (variance previously zero with deterministic neural operators). Tsunami simulation (shallow water): 1 millisecond inference (vs. 30 min-1 hour with UQ). Race car aerodynamics: 0.1 seconds (vs. 500 node-hours).

One Level Deeper

Operator learning as infinite-dimensional neural networks: kernel integral operator replaces matrix-vector multiply. Fourier Neural Operator (FNO): convolution in Fourier space with learnable multiplier, inverse transform, pointwise nonlinearity. Convolutional Neural Operator (CNO): local convolution + non-local activation (fights spectral aliasing from pointwise nonlinearity on band-limited functions). Transformer-based operator transformer: softmax kernel (attention) for global information transfer, windowed attention (domain decomposition across layers) for scalability. Geometry-aware operator transformer: graph neural network (complex point cloud) → structured latent grid → transformer latent space → inverse to original coordinates. Diffusion models (GenCFD): condition on initial data, add noise in forward step to Gaussian, reverse step removes noise given initial condition via trained reverse SDE; trains denoiser (handles small perturbations better than direct approximation).

Failure Boundary

Deterministic neural operators fail on chaotic systems: neural networks insensitive to small perturbations (edge-of-chaos training), but chaotic flows highly sensitive. Collapse to mean: variance entirely absent despite ground truth having high variance from perturbation sensitivity. Aliasing errors when CNNs treat grid-independent operator learning: error increases both coarser and finer; CNO and FNO more robust. Transformers quadratic scaling O(n²) in spatial dimension; patching (finite volume averaging) + windowed attention mitigates. Sample efficiency: even best models need 1000-10000 samples for 1% error (exponent α~0.5, limiting for engineering without vast data sets). Transfer learning (fine-tuning) on unseen PDE classes requires foundation models; foundation model requires pre-training on multiple PDEs (data generation bottleneck with 5 million image pairs, 630M parameters for reasonable coverage).

Important Terms

Neural Operator

A learning program that takes in the starting conditions and rules of a physics problem (like 'this bar starts at room temperature' or 'air flows at this speed') and outputs the full solution (temperatures everywhere after 10 seconds, or pressures everywhere around the wing).

PDE Surrogate

A trained stand-in that mimics a simulator: you feed it a problem setup and get the answer almost instantly, instead of waiting hours for the real step-by-step calculator.

Graph Neural Operator

A learning program that uses map-like connections between points (instead of assuming a neat grid) to handle bumpy, irregular shapes like real engineering geometries.

Transformer Architecture

A design that learns which parts of the input to pay attention to and how they influence each part of the output; used here to handle arbitrary shapes and detect long-range effects (like how a change on one side of the domain echoes to the other side).

Conditional Diffusion Model

A method that constructs a solution by repeatedly removing noise from random static, step by step, while being steered by the input conditions (like sculpting a statue by chipping away stone guided by a blueprint).

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk sits at the center of this whole series: it collects threads from every seminar—how to replace slow solvers with fast learned ones, how attention learns dependencies, how to work on irregular shapes, how to build systems that learn directly from real data.
  • It directly connects to earlier talks on learned operators, on solvers you can take gradients through, and on methods that compress solutions into smaller learned spaces.
  • It also points forward: neural operators are emerging as the physics-specific version of huge foundation models—general-purpose learning machines that might handle many types of physics problems if we scale them up enough.

Practical Caution

Speed matters only if the answer is right. A learning program trained to predict fast must be checked: Does it conserve energy and momentum like the real physics does? Does it blow up or go wild on inputs far outside what it trained on? Does it tell us when it is uncertain? Does it work on edges and corners, or only in the middle of familiar territory? Skip these checks and the model becomes a beautiful hallucination.

Takeaway

The core idea is that a learning program can learn the whole 'before to after' map—if you show it enough real examples, it can learn to predict what comes next nearly as fast as looking it up in a table, but without the table.
