First-Principles Thesis

Data-driven simulation is not just replacing solvers with neural nets. It is a scientific modeling problem: governing equations, numerical stability, training data, discretization, uncertainty, and downstream decisions must fit together.

The seminar series is useful because it shows the field from multiple angles: why PINNs fail, how reduced models work, how differentiable simulators enable inverse design, and how learned surrogates interact with physics.

Concept Route

Physics-Informed Learning

This whole series is about teaching computers to predict how physical things behave — how air pushes over a wing, how heat spreads through metal, how structures flex — by learning from examples instead of only writing down step-by-step math by hand. Physics-informed learning adds a new ingredient: as the computer learns, you also tell it the laws of physics you already know (conservation of energy, momentum, mass). The computer then finds a guess that both matches the examples AND obeys those laws.

Reduced-Order Modeling

Reduced-order modeling is the idea that when you are simulating something physical—how air flows, how heat spreads, how a bridge bends—most of the interesting behavior happens in a small set of 'directions' or 'patterns'. Instead of tracking every single detail (which takes forever), you track only those key patterns. A neural network (a program with millions of dials that learns by trial and error) can learn to predict what those patterns will do next, far faster than running the full simulation.

Differentiable Simulation

A way to run a simulator (a program that steps a physical situation forward in tiny increments to predict what happens next) and trace backwards through every choice it made, so you can see exactly how to tweak each input or design choice to get a better result. Like rewinding a video and nudging each decision along the way.

Operator Learning

Suppose you want to solve lots of slightly different physical problems — maybe heat spreading through bars of different shapes, or water flowing with different starting conditions. Instead of running a simulator each time, you train a program to learn the pattern: given one function (say, the starting temperature everywhere), spit out another function (the final temperature everywhere). That learned mapping between functions is an operator, and operator learning is the skill of training programs to capture these mappings fast.

Fluid Mechanics Simulation

Building a program that predicts how water, air, or any fluid will move and behave — where it flows, how fast, where swirls form, where shocks (sudden sharp changes) appear, and how the fluid's mass, momentum, and energy stay balanced as it moves.

Hybrid Twins

A hybrid twin is a digital copy of a real physical thing—a machine, a structure, the weather—built by combining two approaches: a hand-written step-by-step math program (the simulator) that predicts what happens next, and a learning program (neural network: millions of little dials the computer tunes by trial and error until its answers match reality) that watches the real system and corrects the simulator's mistakes. Fresh data from the real thing feeds in constantly, so the digital copy stays in step with what is actually happening.

Scientific Machine Learning

This series is about getting computers to predict how physical systems behave — how heat spreads, how structures bend, how air flows — by training a learning program on real measurements and simulation data, instead of (or alongside) hand-written equations. The program tunes millions of little dials by trial and error until its predictions match reality, then we use those predictions to run what-if scenarios, control the system, or fill in missing information.

Uncertainty And Robustness

Uncertainty and robustness ask: does a learned simulation model stay trustworthy when faced with gaps in the data it trained on, new conditions it never saw, tiny rounding errors that pile up, or running it forward for many steps in a row?

Inverse Problems And Control

Inverse problems are about working backward: you see what happened (the effect) and need to figure out what caused it (the hidden reason). Control is about picking actions now so that a physical system (a robot, a chemical reaction, a building in an earthquake) does what you want later. Both require a way to connect observations or goals back to the hidden causes or actions that produced them.
