Talk 32 · Max Planck Institute for Dynamics of Complex Technical Systems

Peter Benner: Identification Of Nonlinear Dynamical Systems From Noisy Measurements

This whole series is about getting computers to predict physical behavior by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. This talk focuses on a tricky case: figuring out the hidden rules of a system by watching it move over time, especially when the measurements you're seeing are blurry or incomplete. The challenge is that if you try to work backward from the data to find what the rules are, small errors in measurement get blown up and lead you astray—so instead, you step the equations forward through time in tiny increments to find rules that could have generated all the data you actually saw.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Imagine watching a system move—a pendulum, a heated rod, a chemical reaction—and you want to write down the hidden rules that govern it. You write down what you see at different moments, but your measurements aren't perfect. The big headaches: if you try to compute how fast things are changing by looking at your data (the way you might compute speed from position readings), small measurement errors explode into huge errors in your answer. Also, there are infinitely many possible rules you could write down—how do you pick the right ones? And when there's noise in the data, it's easy to accidentally pick rules that really just describe the noise, not the true physics.

First-Principles Model

Any system that moves has a hidden rule: at each moment in time, the rule says how fast the system's state is changing, depending only on what the state is right now. To discover this rule from data, you make a big list of candidate terms (maybe powers, products, sines, anything you think could be useful) and ask: which terms from the list are actually active in the real rule? Instead of taking derivatives directly from noisy data (which gets unreliable), you try stepping the equations forward through tiny time steps (using a procedure like Runge-Kutta, which estimates the state by taking weighted averages of how fast things are changing at a few points within each step) and checking whether the stepped-forward states match what you actually observed. This turns the problem into picking the right terms from your list.

Key Claims

  • Discovering the rules that govern a physical system is essential if you want to predict what it will do, optimize it, or control it.
  • One practical approach is sparse identification: look at all your candidate terms and use math to pick only the few that are really active.
  • Measurement errors and sparse, incomplete observations are the biggest practical obstacles when trying to reverse-engineer nonlinear systems.
  • Stepping the equations forward through time, rather than computing derivatives from the raw data, leads to more stable and reliable discovery.
  • Automatically choosing which candidate terms to offer is still an unsolved problem.
  • Different types of systems (like waves spreading through space or material flowing) need different time-stepping methods to get stable results.

Important Terms

System Identification

The process of figuring out a mathematical rule that describes how a system behaves by comparing the rule's predictions to measurements you've made.

SINDy

Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics—a method that searches a list of candidate terms and picks only the few that best explain the observed behavior.

Dictionary

A list of candidate building-block functions (like x, x squared, x times y, sine of x) from which the discovery method selects the true terms in the rule.

Runge-Kutta Scheme

A procedure that steps a system forward in time by computing the rate of change at several points within each time step and combining them into a weighted average—used to approximate how a system evolves.

Noisy Measurements

Real-world data that is corrupted by sensor error, rounding, or other imperfections; the extra error hides the true signal and can trick identification methods into picking the wrong terms.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk picks up where earlier talks about interpretability ended, diving into the numerical and mathematical details of how to actually find equations from data.
  • It relates to talks about Koopman operators and Green's functions because all three aim to uncover the hidden structure of a system from what you can measure.
  • It also connects to control theory, since you almost always need an accurate model of a system before you can design a strategy to steer it or keep it stable.

Practical Caution

When you discover equations this way, you might end up with rules that look perfectly reasonable but actually just fit the noise in your data, not the true underlying physics. The list of candidate terms you choose, the method you use to step through time, and how you treat measurement errors are all assumptions—pick them wrong and you'll find an equation that's wrong. There's no automated way to tell if your candidate list is complete enough or if you've accidentally picked noise instead of signal.

Takeaway

Finding the hidden rules of a system by watching its behavior is not just a matching problem—you can't just fit an equation to the numbers. You have to combine it with physics-based reasoning, careful handling of measurement errors, and good choices about which candidate terms to offer the discovery method.
