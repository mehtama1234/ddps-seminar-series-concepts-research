Talk 60 · CWI, Dutch National Institute for Mathematics and Computer Science

Benjamin Sanderse: Structure-Preserving Learning Of Embedded Discrete Closure Models

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things work by learning from data. One popular way uses learning programs (enormous banks of little dials the computer tunes by trial and error) to fill in the missing pieces when a simulator can't track all the tiny, fast details. This talk shows that when you train such a learning program on its own and then drop it into a running simulator, it often breaks because the learning program never saw what actually happens inside the simulator. The answer: train it while it's already sitting inside the simulator, and add rules (like making sure energy doesn't magically appear) to keep things from flying apart.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

When you run a simulator for fluids or structures, you can't compute every single detail—there are too many, and they're too small and fast. Instead, you track only the big ones and use a 'closure'—a guess about how the tiny, unresolved details average out and affect what you're tracking. Learning programs can become very good at this guess when you train them on plain data. But the moment you glue that learned guess into the actual simulator and step it forward in time, it often crashes or produces nonsense because the learned guess never trained on what the simulator actually does step-by-step.

First-Principles Model

A closure isn't a puzzle you can solve by itself. It's a piece that has to live inside the simulator. When you train it separately, you're training it on a different job than the one it will actually do. The fix: teach the simulator to learn—put the closure inside it while you're tuning the dials, so the computer can see how every mistake the closure makes ripples forward and breaks future guesses. Then add hard rules to the closed, like 'energy can't appear out of nowhere.' That way, even if the learned piece isn't perfect, the simulator knows the answer has to respect those rules and won't fly apart.

Key Claims

  • When simulators can't track every detail, you need a guess—a 'closure'—about how tiny, unresolved details affect what you're tracking.
  • Learning programs can make very accurate closures, but if you train them separately and then drop them into the simulator, they break because they never saw what the simulator actually does.
  • The breaking happens partly because the learning program trained on a different job than it will actually do.
  • A closure should be trained while it sits inside the discretized simulator, seeing real step-by-step math.
  • Training inside the simulator with 'differentiable' learning (a way to compute gradients through the whole simulator) is hard but makes the learned piece see how its mistakes spread forward.
  • Adding rules like 'energy stays constant' can guarantee the simulator won't blow up, even when the learned piece is imperfect.

Worked Example

Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation ∂u/∂t + ∂u³/∂x + ∂³u/∂x³ = 0 with 600 spatial points reduced to 30 points via quadratic projection. Spatial filter W (course grid) and reconstruction R (fine-to-course mapping). Semi-discrete system retains all 600-point fine-grid modes as extended state y=(y_bar, y_prime) where y_bar = W u, y_prime = u - R y_bar. Energy evolves: ∂E_bar/∂t = -small-scale dissipation (computed). Using approximate deconvolution of y_prime via matrix A_reconstructed satisfying A_reconstructed · A = I, closure model learns A_corrected minimizing |E_bar_true - E_bar_predicted|². Trained on 4 initial conditions, tested on non-overlapping 2 initial conditions, reduces mean-squared error from 10% (standard CNN closure) to <1% (structure-preserving model) over 600 timesteps.

One Level Deeper

Decomposition: u = u_bar + u_prime (large + small scales). Discrete energy E_bar(t) = 1/2 ∫|u_bar(x,t)|² dx evolves via ∂E_bar/∂t + ∫ u_bar · τ dx = 0 where τ encodes small-scale effects. Differential filter solves low-dimensional Helmholtz equation on course grid to compute approximate u_prime ≈ u - R(W u). Galerkin-Helmholtz projection ensures exact energy coupling: if closure operates on filtered domain only, spurious energy generation in large scales is prevented. Loss function: L = ||∂a/∂t - (M a + B(a⊗a) + C_closure(a))||²_L2 where C_closure is learned CNN applied to ROM coefficients. Oversampling at domain boundaries (sampling beyond domain boundary to enforce periodicity via padding) prevents spurious Gibbs modes.

Failure Boundary

Without energy conservation structure, pure neural-network closures generate fake energy in test predictions; even if trained well on derivatives, long-time solutions exhibit unphysical drift. If small-scale compression (dimension reduction) is too aggressive relative to nonlinearity scale, approximate deconvolution becomes ill-conditioned and reconstruction fails. Standard derivative fitting (matching ∂u/∂t on short windows) becomes inaccurate at long times due to accumulated phase error from stiff differential operators; solution appears correct visually but energy conservation is violated. Stiff gradient regions (shocks in hyperbolic problems) cause spectral methods to misrepresent high-frequency content, degrading closure accuracy.

Important Terms

Closure

A guess about how tiny, fast details that the simulator skips (to save time) average out and affect the big details the simulator tracks.

Embedded Learning

Training a learned piece while it sits inside the actual simulator, so it learns what the simulator really needs, not just pattern-matching on data alone.

Discrete Closure

A closure designed specifically for the step-by-step math version of the physics rule, not just the smooth mathematical version.

Adjoint

A backward calculation that tells you how each dial tweak earlier in the simulator ripples forward and changes the final answer, so you know which dials to turn to improve.

Energy Conservation

A rule built into the simulator that says the total energy of the system can't increase on its own (you can't create energy from nothing).

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk connects directly to how simulators skip small details and still work, how to learn pieces that live inside simulators, how to teach the computer to tune itself through a running solver, and how to bake physical rules into learning so it doesn't break.
  • It also highlights a real trap: a learned piece that works perfectly alone can fail catastrophically when coupled to physics, a danger repeated across many talks.
  • It builds on earlier talks about why some learning-physics pairs fail by pointing to the simulator itself as the teacher, not just the data.

Practical Caution

Do not train a closure by watching it guess things correctly on static data and then assume it will work fine when you drop it into your simulator and let it run. Always test—and ideally train—your learned piece while it's running inside the actual simulator, with the actual step-by-step math that will use it.

Takeaway

Learning a closure is not just about pattern matching. It is about building a piece of a simulator that stays stable over many steps. Stability comes from putting the learned piece inside the simulator as you train, and building in the physical rules that have to hold.
