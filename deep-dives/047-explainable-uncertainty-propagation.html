Talk 47 · TU Delft

Alice Cicirello: Interpretable, Explainable And Non-Intrusive Uncertainty Propagation

This seminar series chases getting computers to predict physical behavior by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. One key challenge: when you run a simulation and the inputs (like material stiffness or heat source intensity) might change slightly, how do you find the range of outputs that could happen without running the simulation thousands of times? This talk shows a method that uses machine learning to pick the smartest few simulation runs to find that range, then explains to engineers exactly why those bounds are what they are.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Engineering decisions rest on knowing whether a system will stay safe or perform well when real-world conditions wiggle. Realistic physics simulations can run for hours or days each, so trying thousands of runs to map out all possible outcomes is not practical. Finding the upper and lower bounds on performance usually requires either running the model vastly many times, rewriting how the solver works (which changes what you're testing), or simplifying the physics in ways that may hide the truth.

First-Principles Model

Uncertainty propagation means watching how small changes in inputs (like tolerances in a design or errors in sensors) turn into changes in outputs as they travel through a simulation. If you can't rewrite the simulation's code or replace it with a simpler one, you still need to pick which runs matter most. Machine learning optimization can hunt through the input space for which combinations give the highest and lowest outputs. Once you find them, you build a fast replacement model (a surrogate) — a simpler mathematical description that mimics the expensive one's behavior. You can then point at that simpler model and explain: 'Here's why the output gets so high' or 'Here's why it gets so low.'

Key Claims

  • Uncertainty quantification answers a vital question: when inputs vary (due to measurement error, manufacturing tolerance, or operating conditions), how far do the outputs spread? This variation ripples through the entire calculation.
  • For simulations that run slowly (hours or days per run), the bottleneck is not math—it is the sheer cost of testing many input combinations.
  • Non-intrusive methods don't rewrite the simulator's code or replace the physics with a simpler version; you just run it as-is and learn from the results.
  • Interpretability means a human can follow the chain of logic: 'We ran the simulation with these inputs, here's what came out, and here's why.'
  • Explainability means packaging the results in forms engineers actually need: 'When temperature can vary this much, performance is guaranteed to stay between these bounds.'
  • Machine learning can find the input combinations that make outputs as large or as small as possible, rather than blindly testing every combination.

Important Terms

Uncertainty Propagation

Tracking how small wobbles or ranges in inputs (like a measurement being off by 5%, or a material property changing with temperature) flow through a calculation to create wobbles or ranges in the answer.

Non-Intrusive Method

A method that treats the simulator as a sealed tool—you feed it inputs and read off outputs, but you don't rewrite any of its code or swap out any of its physics.

Interval Uncertainty

Describing uncertainty as a simple range: 'The output will be between 100 and 150.' This is simpler than saying 'The output has a bell-curve probability distribution' but less detailed.

Bayesian Optimization

A strategy for picking which experiments or simulation runs to do next when each run is expensive: instead of testing all possibilities, you build a fast statistical guess-model that predicts which untried runs might give the most useful information, then try those.

Performance Bound

A guaranteed limit—like 'this system cannot fail worse than X' or 'this system cannot perform better than Y'—that applies when inputs are allowed to vary within specified ranges.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk addresses the seminar series' core theme by showing how to handle variability: when inputs shift or wobble, how do we still know the system stays within safe bounds?
  • It connects to talks on smart sampling: both use strategies to decide which experiments or simulation runs are worth the cost when resources are scarce.
  • It builds on talks about interpretability by showing that explaining uncertainty results—not just computing them—is what engineers actually need to trust and use the answers.

Practical Caution

If your search strategy overlooks some regions of the input space, or your replacement model doesn't truly capture all the physics's behavior, you might miss the real highest and lowest values. Only trust your output bounds after confirming that your search strategy actually visited the important parts of the input space and your replacement model can reproduce the physics on a variety of test cases.

Takeaway

Finding the extremes of model outputs with a limited computing budget is hard. The real work is picking your simulation runs wisely to find the bounds that matter, then showing engineers a clear picture of why those bounds are correct.
