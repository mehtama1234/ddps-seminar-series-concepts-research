Talk 88 · ETH Zurich

Eleni Chatzi: Twinning And Model Discovery For Engineered Systems

This whole series is about teaching computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from data instead of only hand-written math. Here's one powerful way: build a living computer copy of a real structure (a bridge, a building, a plane wing), feed it measurements from sensors scattered around the real thing, and use that copy to spot damage, measure how bad it is, guess how long it will last, and decide what to fix. This talk is about why that works—and what you have to do to make it work right.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Real structures—buildings, bridges, aircraft—are complicated, exposed to weather and heavy use, and change over time as materials wear. We have sensors that pick up vibrations and movements. These sensors can tell you 'something is wrong here', but they don't automatically tell you how bad the damage is, when the structure will fail, or what to fix. The sensors see only a few spots. The structure is everywhere. To make smart decisions about repairs, you need to connect what the sensors see to the whole structure, account for the fact that you don't know everything (the sensors have noise, materials vary), and imagine what happens under future stress.

First-Principles Model

A digital twin is a computer copy of your real structure that stays up to date. Sensors feed real measurements into this copy. Physics equations (the rules describing how structures bend, how forces spread, how materials degrade) let the computer spread those few sensor measurements to places you can't measure and into the future. Model discovery means tuning that computer copy until it matches what the real structure does—by learning what damage it has, what the materials are actually like, or what its current state is. When the twin is built right, it tells you: is damage there? where exactly? how much? what will happen next? should we fix it now?

Key Claims

  • Detecting damage is easiest; then you must locate it, measure how big it is, and finally predict when it will fail—each step is harder than the last.
  • Just fitting numbers to sensor data is not enough for the harder jobs, like measuring damage or predicting how long the structure lasts.
  • A real digital twin of a monitored structure must be built from physics equations, not just recorded data.
  • A good twin represents the structure as it actually is right now, including damage and wear—not just as the engineers designed it.
  • A twin can imagine how the structure will hold up under future loads and stresses if you give it the right information.
  • A useful twin should tell you what to do (fix it, inspect it, leave it alone), not just show you numbers.

Worked Example

Digital twin of a damped pendulum: physical system generates position measurements y_obs(t), model is ∂²θ/∂t² + c·∂θ/∂t + sin(θ) = 0. PINN simultaneously learns the true state trajectory u(t) and parameter c. Training on 50 time measurements over [0, 10s], PINN recovers c ≈ 0.25±0.01 (true value 0.25) and predicts θ(t) beyond training window with <2% error. For engineered system (e.g., structural vibration sensor network with 100 nodes), sparse measurements at 10 sensor locations constrain model; PINN infers missing DOFs and locates damage (stiffness reduction) to within 5% of true location.

One Level Deeper

Physics-informed discovery combines constraint enforcement with supervised learning: L_total = λ_data L_data + λ_physics L_physics where L_data = ||u_pred(t_i) - y_obs(t_i)||² measures fit to observations, L_physics = ||∂u/∂t - f(u,μ)||² enforces dynamics (μ are unknown parameters or system matrices). Automatic differentiation computes ∂u/∂t, ∂²u/∂t² via chain rule through neural network. Parameter discovery: if μ appears in loss via residual, gradient descent updates both network weights θ_net and parameters θ_μ simultaneously: ∂L/∂μ_j = ∫ (∂r/∂μ_j) · r dt. This couples model error with parameter error, allowing joint identification.

Failure Boundary

If model structure is wrong (e.g., assuming linear damping c·ż when system is nonlinear frequency-dependent), parameter identification fails catastrophically; discovered c varies with amplitude and time. Sparse measurements (few sensors) combined with complex dynamics (high-dimensional state) leads to unobservable modes; PINN latent representation can fit noise while predictions outside sensor region are phantom. Long-term prediction (beyond training window) accumulates phase error; solutions that match training data closely can diverge exponentially if true system is chaotic or stiff. If measurement noise is high (>5% of signal), parameter uncertainty propagates and identified parameters become meaningless.

Important Terms

Structural Health Monitoring

The job of placing sensors on a structure, reading their signals, and deciding whether there is damage, where it is, how bad it is, and when things might break.

Augmented Twin

A computer copy of a real structure that is constantly updated with fresh sensor readings and uses physics equations to understand the whole structure, not just the spots where sensors sit.

Residual Life

How much longer a structure or part can keep working safely before it wears out, breaks, or gets so weak that it has to be replaced.

Full-Field Reconstruction

Taking the few measurements you have from sensors and using math and physics to figure out what is happening everywhere in the structure—even the spots with no sensor.

Model Discovery

Using the real measurements from sensors to tune a computer model, adjusting numbers for damage, material properties, or the structure's actual state until the model matches what the real thing is actually doing.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture brings together digital twins, damage detection, uncertainty accounting, and the job of learning what a structure's properties actually are—all aimed at making decisions about real structures in the field.
  • It moves forward from earlier talks about spot damage detection to the larger question: how do we predict what happens next and decide what to do about it?
  • It shares thinking with reduced-order models and fast surrogate models because twins need to run fast enough to be useful, while still respecting the physics of how structures actually work.

Practical Caution

A screen showing sensor numbers is not a digital twin. To actually use a twin for repair decisions, you need the physics equations, you need to track your uncertainty (sensors wiggle, math isn't perfect), and you need to think about what could stress the structure in the future. Skip any of those, and your twin will steer you wrong.

Takeaway

A useful digital twin does three separate jobs. First, it figures out what the structure looks like right now (state estimation). Second, it uses physics to guess how it will behave (mechanics). Third, it tells a human what action to take—fix it now? check it later? (decision support). Sensors alone, without the other two jobs, don't give you a real twin.
