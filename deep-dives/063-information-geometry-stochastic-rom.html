Talk 63 · University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill

Zorin Mitran: Data-Driven Information Geometry For Stochastic Model Reduction

This whole series is about teaching computers to predict physical behavior by learning from examples instead of hand-built math. But what if your system has randomness in it — heat jiggling in a bar, noise in a sensor, molecules bouncing unpredictably? Then we care about probability distributions, not single answers. This talk shows how to shrink down complex models with randomness by respecting the actual shape of those distributions, rather than treating them as if they lived in flat featureless space.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Most reduction methods chop a large model down to a smaller one by putting data onto a flat straight space and measuring distance the ordinary way. But a stochastic system (one with randomness and jiggle) is not really a collection of individual states — it is a collection of probability distributions (the odds of finding the system in each state). Probability distributions have a natural curved shape; they do not live in flat space the way ordinary vectors do. And for systems far from equilibrium, you might not even have a clean formula for what distribution shape to use.

First-Principles Model

Think of probability distributions as living on a curved surface (like how the globe is curved, not flat). Information geometry is the study of how these surfaces curve and where distributions sit on them. Instead of squashing your data onto a flat subspace (a straight line, a plane), you can move along curved paths on the surface where distributions naturally belong. When you do not have a named distribution family ready, you can build one by taking small neighborhoods around your data points, finding the bell-curve shape that fits each neighborhood using a standard matrix trick called SVD, and gluing these bell-curves together to make a personalized curved surface for your data.

Key Claims

  • Standard reduction methods project data onto flat straight spaces and use ordinary distance.
  • Models with randomness are fundamentally about probability distributions, not single answer values.
  • Probability distributions have natural curved shapes; you can think of them as living on geometric surfaces.
  • For systems far from equilibrium, you may not have a standard named distribution family available.
  • You can build a personalized curved surface from your data by taking small neighborhoods, fitting bell-curves to each one using matrix decomposition, and connecting them.
  • Moving along curved paths (geodesics) on this surface gives you a reduced model that respects how distributions behave.

Worked Example

Stochastic PDE: ∂u/∂t + N[u] = σ ξ(x,t) (multiplicative noise). Data-driven ROM via snapshot POD on random realizations, then project to reduced space: u_rom(t; β) = Σᵢ βᵢ(t) ϕᵢ. Information geometry: measure manifold of distributions via Wasserstein or Fisher metric, adaptively refine basis when KL divergence between FOM and ROM exceeds threshold.

One Level Deeper

Fisher information matrix F_ij = -E[∂²log p(u|θ)/∂θᵢ∂θⱼ] encodes local geometry of likelihood surface. Geodesic on manifold parameterized by (reduced state, noise intensity σ). Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence: D_KL(p_fom || p_rom) = E_fom[log(p_fom/p_rom)]. Greedy: find μ_new = argmax KL(p_fom(·,μ), p_rom(·,μ)) to extend ROM basis.

Failure Boundary

Computing Fisher/Hessian requires densities p(u); Monte Carlo estimation needs many samples (~10³). Curse of dimensionality in parameter space: Fisher matrix is P×P. Method assumes Gaussian or mild non-Gaussianity; heavy-tailed noise breaks information-geometric assumptions.

Important Terms

Information Geometry

The study of probability distributions as shapes on a curved surface; how to measure distance and direction between different distributions.

Statistical Manifold

A curved surface (like a globe or a potato chip) where each point on the surface stands for one specific probability distribution.

Geodesic

The shortest or most natural path between two points on a curved surface, like a great circle on Earth; it is curved in ordinary flat space but straight on the surface itself.

Local Normal Distribution

A bell-curve (Gaussian) shape you fit to a small pocket of data points around one spot.

Stochastic Model Reduction

Shrinking a complex model down to a simpler one when the model includes randomness, jiggle, or uncertainty in its states or answers.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture extends the reduction-modeling theme from simple point-value systems to systems where what matters is the probability distribution, not a single answer.
  • It touches uncertainty quantification because instead of tracking one state value, you are tracking an entire probability distribution.
  • It also connects to learning the shape of data: the reduced space sits on a curve you learn from data, not on a pre-made flat space.

Practical Caution

If you treat probability distributions as if they live in flat space and measure distance the ordinary way, you can lose or distort their true structure. For a model with randomness, how you measure distance and define your reduced space actually changes what the reduced model predicts, so the curved surface you build must match how your actual distributions sit and move in your data.

Takeaway

How you do stochastic reduction depends on geometry — the shape of the space you are working in. If you are modeling probability distributions, your reduced model should live on a surface that respects how those distributions actually behave, not on a flat artificial space.
