Talk 96 · University of Twente

Mengwu Guo: Probabilistic Methods for Data-Driven Reduced-Order Modeling

This whole series is about getting computers to predict physical behavior by learning from examples. This talk focuses on models that run fast AND tell you how confident they are: if you run a simulation many times with different starting conditions, you want it quick — but you also want it to admit when it's unsure, especially when the input situation is new or unusual.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Engineers often build digital twins — computer copies of real systems like engines or bridges — that must answer 'what if' questions thousands of times. A full physics simulator is accurate but slow. A cheap copy built from past examples is fast but doesn't warn you when it's unreliable, especially when you ask it to predict for conditions it never saw during training.

First-Principles Model

A reduced-order model is a trick: instead of tracking millions of details in a system, you track just the handful of important ones, shrinking it from millions of unknowns to maybe 100. A probabilistic reduced-order model adds honesty to that shrinking — it says 'this is my best guess, but I have uncertainty here.' Gaussian process regression is one way: treat your expensive full simulator as a tool you run offline to collect examples, then build a fast replacement that not only guesses what happens next but also says how likely it is to be right. Deep kernel learning mixes neural networks (programs with millions of tunable dials that learn patterns through trial and error) with Gaussian processes (a method for tracking uncertainty): neural networks discover which few dimensions matter in a big messy image or sensor reading, then a Gaussian process takes over to predict dynamics with built-in uncertainty. Bayesian operator inference takes a different angle: if you know the reduced system has a certain structure (say, it follows a rule about how things change), you can estimate just the pieces of that rule directly from data while computing how much doubt to attach to each piece.

Key Claims

  • Probabilistic reduced-order models work best when you need both speed and a real measure of how sure you are.
  • Gaussian process reduced-order models use expensive solvers as offline teachers to build data, then run entirely without the solver after that.
  • Neural networks can learn which parts of your data really matter, then pass that knowledge to a Gaussian process to handle prediction and uncertainty.
  • Bayesian inference for reduced operators estimates the rules of your shrunken system straight from data and delivers uncertainty ranges on those estimates.
  • Bayesian methods naturally fold in both mathematical stabilizers (to stop noise from wrecking the answer) and uncertainty tracking.
  • The three approaches range from completely ignoring the physics structure, to using partial structure, to enforcing full structure — each trades off speed for trustworthiness.

Important Terms

Probabilistic ROM

A reduced-order model that doesn't just give you one predicted answer but also gives you uncertainty — how confident it is in that answer.

Gaussian Process Regression

A method that learns from examples and gives both a best guess at the answer and a range of how wrong it might be.

Deep Kernel Learning

A hybrid approach: use a neural network to automatically discover which few things from your data actually matter, then use a Gaussian process to make predictions with uncertainty on top of that discovery.

Bayesian Operator Inference

A way to estimate the rules of a shrunken system directly from data, where the rules come with ranges of uncertainty attached.

Tikhonov Regularization

A mathematical penalty that stabilizes a shaky inverse problem — it stops the answer from going haywire when noise is present.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk brings together reduced-order models, surrogate modeling (learning replacements for expensive simulations), uncertainty tracking, and digital twins.
  • It sits alongside earlier talks on operator inference and neural operators by asking: how much of the physics should you bake into your learned model?
  • It also points to active learning and experimental design: if you know which parts of your prediction are most uncertain, you can ask 'where should I run expensive simulations next to reduce that doubt?'

Practical Caution

Uncertainty numbers only make sense if your model class is right, your starting assumptions (priors) are reasonable, and your training data covers the ground you'll work on. A fast online copy can give wildly wrong answers when you feed it an input far from anything in the training set — your uncertainty might not even flag this, because the model assumes it will stay close to what it learned.

Takeaway

A simulation that you can ship and trust must be fast, stay true to physics as much as you can, and be honest about what it doesn't know.
