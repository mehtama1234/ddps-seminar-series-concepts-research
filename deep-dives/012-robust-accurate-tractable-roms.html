Talk 12 · University of Michigan

Karthik Duraisamy: Towards Robust, Accurate And Tractable Reduced-Order Models

This lecture organizes practical reduced-order modeling around three competing needs: building models that stay accurate even when conditions change, match the real physics with low error, and run fast enough to be useful for design and prediction.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Reduced-order models are appealing because they replace a huge full-order system with a much smaller one that runs in seconds instead of hours. But flows like combustion combine many hard things at once: equations that evolve on different timescales, transport and acoustic waves, heat from chemical reaction, shapes that change topology, and residuals with strong nonlinearities. A model that works perfectly under one operating condition may become unstable, violate conservation laws, or stay expensive when the physics or parameters shift.

First-Principles Model

A reduced-order model represents the governing equations on a lower-dimensional manifold, a simplified geometric space. For nonlinear systems, working in fewer dimensions does not automatically make the residual (leftover error from approximate equations) cheap to evaluate, stable during evolution, or conservative (preserving mass or energy). Robust model design therefore needs structure-preserving variables that respect physical laws, discrete consistency in how residuals are minimized for stability, adaptivity that adjusts the model when parameters change, and hyper-reduction that evaluates nonlinear terms at only a small subset of locations instead of everywhere.

Key Claims

  • Reduced-order modeling means solving the same governing equations on a lower-dimensional manifold.
  • Complex flows with multiple physics create reduced-order modeling challenges through equations at different timescales, multiscale structure, wave transport, and topology changes.
  • Standard Galerkin reduced-order models can be stable in theory but may become unstable when flows are difficult or parameters move far from training conditions.
  • Least-squares Petrov-Galerkin formulations improve discrete consistency by minimizing actual residuals rather than just enforcing orthogonality.
  • Hyper-reduction is required because nonlinear residual evaluation can still scale with the full-order model size if every degree of freedom must be evaluated.
  • Tractability matters most when full-order simulations are so expensive that even generating training data becomes a bottleneck.

Important Terms

Robust ROM

A reduced model that stays stable and respects physical laws through challenging dynamics or when parameters move away from the training data.

Least-Squares Petrov-Galerkin

A projection method that minimizes the residual (leftover error) measured at selected points rather than enforcing the standard orthogonality condition of Galerkin projection.

Structure-Preserving Transformation

A change of variables designed to preserve conservation laws, symmetries, numerical stability, or other important mathematical properties.

Hyper-Reduction

Cutting cost by evaluating nonlinear residuals at a small number of selected sample points instead of at every full-order grid point.

Component ROM

A reduced model built for one part of a system that can be connected with other reduced or full-order subsystems.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture ties together earlier ROM talks into a practical checklist: speed and low dimension are not enough without stability.
  • It connects to hybrid digital twins because a digital twin that works in the field needs stable reduced components, not just fast approximations.
  • It also links to entropy-stable schemes and differentiable simulation by showing that reduced models must match the numerical structure and conservation laws of the full system.

Practical Caution

A model can fail even with an excellent basis if the residual is inconsistent with physical laws, the nonlinear terms are not hyper-reduced, or operating conditions shift the flow in ways the training data did not see (changing whether vortices form, for example). Always check stability, computational cost, and how well the model handles parameter changes together rather than separately.

Takeaway

The core lesson is that having fewer dimensions does not mean the problem is simpler. Practical models must preserve physical structure, adapt when physics changes, and cut down the cost of evaluating nonlinear pieces.
