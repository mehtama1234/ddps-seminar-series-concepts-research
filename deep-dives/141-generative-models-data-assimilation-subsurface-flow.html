Talk 141 · Carnegie Mellon University

Su Jiang: Generative Models for Data Assimilation in Subsurface Flow

This whole series is about getting computers to predict physical behavior by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. One big challenge is forecasting what happens deep underground—oil, water, heat moving through rock—because there are billions of unknowns to track. This talk shows how a learning program that generates realistic pictures of underground structures can help solve this: instead of solving an impossible puzzle with billions of pieces, the computer learns to sample from likely possibilities, then tunes those guesses against real measurements.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

To predict what happens underground in a reservoir or aquifer, engineers must guess at invisible properties they cannot see: how cracked is the rock, where do the fractures run, what is the pressure. Running enough computer simulations to check all possibilities is too expensive, and the rock structures themselves are hard to describe mathematically because fractures and faults are so irregular. The puzzle becomes a twisted inverse problem: given what you measure at the surface, work backward to figure out what the rock looks like and how fluids will move.

First-Principles Model

A generative model is a learning program trained to create new pictures of possible underground structures that look realistic based on examples it learned. One approach trains the program to compress complicated 3D rock pictures into a much smaller description—like squeezing a photograph into a tiny code. Then, the program learns to gradually add realistic noise and then remove it step by step until a new picture emerges. This compressed diffusion model can quickly generate many candidate rock structures. Those structures feed into an ensemble simulation: run many forecasts in parallel using different guesses, then compare them all to real measurements and nudge the guesses closer to what matches. For regions with carbon storage in faulted rock, another method directly inverts the observed measurements into predictions about flow and deformation by training the generator on system responses instead of just rock pictures.

Key Claims

  • Forecasting subsurface behavior requires inferring hidden properties from limited surface measurements.
  • The inverse problem is high-dimensional and nonlinear, making brute-force simulation too expensive.
  • Running many parallel simulations (ensembles) with different guesses is a practical approach but still costly.
  • Learning programs that generate realistic rock structures provide a compact way to represent the space of possibilities.
  • A compressed diffusion model can generate fracture networks quickly and feed into ensemble assimilation workflows.
  • Measuring responses directly instead of only rock properties enables even more targeted posterior prediction for storage and safety.

Important Terms

Generative Model

A learning program trained to create new samples that look like the examples it studied—like learning to draw new portraits after seeing hundreds of faces.

Data Assimilation

The process of taking a computer prediction and real measurements and blending them together to get a better guess—nudging the prediction closer to what you actually observed.

Subsurface Flow

Water, oil, gas, or heat moving through underground rock, soil, or fractures—places you cannot see or measure directly.

Latent Diffusion Model

A learning program that first compresses pictures into tiny codes, then learns to gradually add and remove noise to transform one picture into another—working in this compressed code space instead of the full picture.

Data-Space Inversion

Instead of trying to guess the rock properties directly, the program learns to predict what you would measure on the surface, then works backward from real measurements to estimate what happened underground.

Connections Across The Series

  • Connects learning programs, compressed representations, and measurement-based inference—three recurring themes across the series.
  • Builds on earlier talks about combining predictions with measurements, but adds the specific idea that a learned generator can compress the space of possibilities.
  • Relates to themes about trustworthy predictions because the posterior—the final refined guess—must actually cover real scenarios, not just fit measurements.

Practical Caution

The danger is that a learned generator can only create structures similar to the training examples it saw. If the real underground rocks have a pattern the program never learned, the posterior—the final updated guess after comparing to measurements—will miss real possibilities. You must check whether the updated guesses still look geologically reasonable and whether they actually cover all scenarios that could match the measurements.

Takeaway

Generative models transform an impossible inverse problem—billions of unknowns, no direct equation—into a structured sampling problem. The program learns a compressed description of plausible scenarios, then uses real measurements to filter and refine those scenarios toward the truth.
