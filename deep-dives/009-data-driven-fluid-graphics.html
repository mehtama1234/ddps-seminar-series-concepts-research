Talk 9 · ETH Zurich / Disney Research Studios

Byungsoo Kim: Data-Driven Methods For Fluid Simulations In Computer Graphics

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of just hand-written math. One version of this is building a simulator that guesses the next moment of a fluid like water or smoke. This talk is about a shortcut: instead of doing all the math for every tiny piece of the fluid, you train a learning program (a huge bank of dials the computer tunes by trial and error until its answers look right) on examples of fluids, and let it predict or speed up pieces of the simulation. The goal is not scientific accuracy; it is convincing motion that artists can control and that runs fast enough for a movie or game.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Movie and game studios need to simulate fluids like water, smoke, and splashes that both look convincing and run fast enough to finish on time. The old way—solving the physics step by step for every tiny piece—works but is slow. The main question is not 'does every number match the physics equations exactly' but 'does the motion look right, can the artist steer it, and will it finish before the deadline?'

First-Principles Model

A physics simulator takes the laws of motion and pressure (rules for how something changes from moment to moment) and steps the fluid forward in tiny increments. A data-driven shortcut: train a program on many examples of fluids in motion, and let it learn to predict the next moment directly, or learn to speed up parts of the step-by-step math. You can teach a program to work in compressed space—a smaller, squeezed version of the full fluid field that is cheaper to evolve. You can add surface turbulence or splashes that would be too detailed to simulate by hand. You can tune how forces affect the fluid (art direction) by taking gradients through the simulator—poking the numbers in directions that push the result toward what you want—instead of re-running the whole calculation.

Key Claims

  • Physics-based animation has been around for decades, but studios still need fast and controllable ways to simulate fluids on set.
  • In graphics, losing some accuracy is acceptable if you gain speed, artist control, and visual appeal.
  • A learning program can speed up the particle solvers (tracking many tiny bits of fluid) and the pressure step (the expensive calculation that keeps fluid incompressible).
  • You can train a program to work on compressed representations of the fluid field—smaller versions that are cheaper to advance in time—then decode them back to full resolution.
  • A learning program can add detail—turbulent swirls, water splashes, sharp motion—that would take forever to simulate by hand.
  • By computing gradients through a simulator, you can tune starting states or force settings to reach a target motion without re-running the full calculation repeatedly.

Important Terms

Art-Directable Simulation

A simulator that artists can steer by adjusting forces, starting conditions, or parameters, and the result stays physically plausible enough to look real.

Fluid Stylization

Applying artistic choices to how a fluid flows or looks—like changing the turbulence style, thickness, or color—without re-running the core physics.

Super-Resolution For Flow

Training a learning program on pairs of low-resolution and high-resolution fluid simulations so it learns to add sharp detail when given a coarse version.

Latent-Space Fluid Solver

A simulator that compresses the full fluid field into a smaller squeezed form (like compressing a video), evolves the squeezed form (much cheaper), then expands it back to full size for display.

Differentiable Physics Control

Taking gradients (directions of change) backward through a simulator so you can tweak starting conditions or forces in ways that push the final motion toward a target without running the whole calculation over.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk reveals that data-driven simulation has different goals depending on the field: graphics optimizes for control and convincing appearance in ways that engineering prediction does not.
  • It shows a concrete example of differentiable physics—using gradients through a simulator to enable artists to steer the result toward what they want.
  • It also demonstrates compressed-space approximation and super-resolution as real practical ways to make simulation fast enough for production while keeping visual quality.

Practical Caution

A shortcut that works for movies may not work for engineering or safety predictions. A motion that looks visually convincing in a graphics demo may fail badly when you need precise numbers—like predicting how a real flood will behave, or designing a dam. The opposite caution is also true: demanding engineering accuracy for a movie animation wastes time and money.

Takeaway

The deep lesson is that your choice of what to measure drives the entire design. A graphics simulator measures 'does this look convincing and do artists have control?' and optimizes for speed and creativity. An engineering simulator measures 'is this prediction accurate?' and optimizes for truth. They are solving different problems, even though they both simulate fluids.
