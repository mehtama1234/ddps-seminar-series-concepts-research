Talk 34 · University of Toronto

Masayuki Yano: Reliable, Efficient, And Automated Model Reduction Of Parametrized Nonlinear PDEs

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical systems behave by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math rules. One challenge: to learn correctly from examples, you often start with a smaller, simpler model that captures the essence of what matters. This talk is about how to automatically build such simplified models for complex physics problems (where behavior changes in weird ways, not just linearly), control the mistakes that slip in during simplification, and know which new examples to test next to make the model trustworthy.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

When you want to predict how something physical will behave—say, air flowing over a wing or heat spreading through a metal block—you write down the rules that govern it. These rules (called differential equations, or PDEs) are so complicated that solving them for many different setups takes forever. So engineers build a smaller, faster copy of the full model that captures the essential behavior. But this smaller copy can betray you: if the full model wasn't built carefully enough, or if the simplification threw away the wrong details, the smaller copy gives wrong answers. Building these smaller copies by hand takes months and needs someone who understands both the physics and the tricks—and for tangled, unpredictable flows, you might not even know which tricks will work.

First-Principles Model

This talk describes a method that makes computers do the hand work. Instead of trying to predict every detail everywhere, you first pick the one thing you actually care about—maybe lift on a wing, or the maximum temperature reached. The method then: carefully divides the space into small pieces (a finer or coarser mesh) using an automatic rule that judges whether the answer is good enough; finds a smart choice of 'basis shapes' (simple building blocks that get mixed together to form the final answer) by looking at examples across different parameters and cutting out shapes that barely matter; speeds up the expensive calculation of leftover errors (how badly the physics rules are violated) by checking them only on a few pieces instead of all; and uses information from running the physics rules backwards to weight where mistakes hurt most. Finally, a loop greedily picks the next example to test by choosing the setup where the method predicts the biggest potential mistake.

Key Claims

  • When you build a simplified model, you pick up mistakes from three places: the way you chop up space into pieces, the way you pick your simple building blocks, and the way you compute the numbers. You have to watch all three.
  • Using curved pieces and adjusting their size based on current errors can make the expensive practice phase—where you compute the examples the model learns from—much faster.
  • Solving the physics rules backwards (a technique called the adjoint or dual problem) tells you where mistakes matter most for the answer you care about.
  • You can speed up the expensive calculation of leftover errors by computing them only on a handful of pieces instead of the whole space.
  • A step-by-step loop can automatically build the mesh, the building blocks, and the integration rule all at once.
  • When you have many knobs to turn or flows with sudden jumps, the challenges are much harder and stay mostly unsolved.

Worked Example

Illustrative: A parametric PDE (e.g., nonlinear diffusion with parameter μ) is sampled at training points in parameter space. A POD basis {ϕ₁,...,ϕᵣ} is extracted from full-order snapshots. The reduced solution u_rom(μ) = Σᵢ αᵢ(μ)ϕᵢ is projected via Galerkin: find α such that (∂u_rom/∂t + N[u_rom], ϕⱼ) = 0 for all j ∈ [1..r]. The greedy algorithm adaptively selects parameter samples where certified error bounds exceed a tolerance.

One Level Deeper

A posteriori error bound ε(μ) provides guaranteed rom accuracy: ||u_fom(μ) - u_rom(μ)|| ≤ ε(μ). Dual-norm (residual-based): ε(μ) ~ ||∂u_rom/∂t + N[u_rom]||*_{H'}/α_LB(μ), where α_LB is the coercivity lower bound. Greedy loop: sample μ with largest ε(μ), add FOM solve, augment POD basis. Nonlinear term evaluation via hyper-reduction (gappy POD or DEIM: Discrete Empirical Interpolation Method) maps N_full(u_rom) → N_red(u_rom) using interpolation indices.

Failure Boundary

Coercivity lower bound α_LB is expensive to compute rigorously; many implementations use heuristic estimates. For strongly nonlinear systems (shocks, bifurcations), certified bounds become loose and greedy sampling loses effectiveness. Parameter space exploration scales poorly in high-dimensional μ ∈ ℝ^P.

Important Terms

Reduced Model

A smaller, faster version of a full physics simulator that you build by keeping the most important pieces and throwing out the rest.

Adaptive Discontinuous Galerkin

A way of dividing space into pieces and solving the physics rules on each one, where the computer automatically decides how fine to make the pieces based on where the answer is most uncertain.

Dual-Weighted Residual Error

A method to measure mistakes by running the physics rules backwards and using that backward solution to weight where forward mistakes hurt the answer you care about most.

Empirical Quadrature

A trick to speed up calculations by computing expensive sums (like errors) on just a few chosen pieces instead of every piece.

Greedy Selection

A simple but effective strategy that picks the next example to test by always choosing the one where the method guesses the error will be largest.

Connections Across The Series

  • This work continues earlier talks in the series about building simplified models, but switches focus: instead of explaining how an expert tunes a model by trial and error, it shows how to make the tuning itself automatic and trustworthy.
  • It builds on earlier talks about controlling errors and picking training examples through principled choices—especially the idea of working backwards through the problem to understand where mistakes matter most.
  • It also touches talks about complex, choppy flows (where air rushes or shocks appear) because those are the problems where automatic tools are most needed and hardest to get right.

Practical Caution

The language in this talk says 'certified'—meaning the method gives you a bound on error—but some of these bounds are careful estimates, not ironclad guarantees. When you run the whole automated pipeline on a real problem, especially one with many knobs to turn or sudden jumps (shocks), you still need to run sanity checks by hand and think about whether the answer makes sense.

Takeaway

The lesson is this: if you automate the building of simpler models, you have to automate the checking too. You must know exactly what you're squeezing out and what you're keeping, have a way to measure mistakes in the thing you care about, and pick new test cases that fill the biggest gaps. Automation without that thinking is dangerous.
