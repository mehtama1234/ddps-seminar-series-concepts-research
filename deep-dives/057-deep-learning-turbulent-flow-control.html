Talk 57 · KTH Royal Institute of Technology

Ricardo Vinuesa: Modeling And Controlling Turbulent Flows Through Deep Learning

This lecture covers deep learning applied to turbulent flows: wall-based sensing to predict hidden flow patterns, transfer learning to adapt across flow speeds, nonlinear reduced models, GAN-based image sharpening, and reinforcement learning to find better control strategies.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Turbulent flows are high-dimensional, nonlinear, and change at many scales at once. They are expensive to measure or simulate accurately. For flows bounded by walls, the flow away from the wall is difficult to observe without sensors that disturb it. Meanwhile, control strategies need fast, accurate estimates of the swirling structures (coherent structures) that drive the flow.

First-Principles Model

Turbulent flows contain flow properties that vary through space but are never fully visible from any one spot. Sensors, computer simulations, and neural networks each give partial views. Convolutional neural networks can use wall measurements to predict velocity (speed and direction) patterns away from the wall. Transfer learning reuses patterns learned at one flow speed to work at another. Autoencoders (neural networks that compress and decompress data) build simplified nonlinear models. GANs (generative adversarial networks, systems where two neural networks compete to generate realistic data) can fill in high-resolution flow details from low-resolution or sparse data. Reinforcement learning can search for control strategies by trial and reward in simulation.

Key Claims

  • Deep learning is now widely used in computational fluid dynamics and turbulent-flow analysis.
  • Non-intrusive sensing can predict flow away from the wall using only wall measurements, avoiding sensors that would disturb the flow.
  • Convolutional neural networks outperform traditional linear models at predicting turbulent flows.
  • Transfer learning can move trained representations from one flow speed to nearby flow speeds.
  • Autoencoders can build simpler nonlinear models of turbulent flows.
  • GANs and reinforcement learning can create high-resolution flow images and find control strategies, but need careful real-world testing.

Important Terms

Wall-Bounded Turbulence

Turbulent flow shaped by solid boundaries such as pipes, aircraft wings, or building surfaces.

Non-Intrusive Sensing

Estimating flow properties without placing physical sensors in the flow that would alter what you are trying to measure.

Transfer Learning

Reusing a neural network trained on one situation (such as flow at one speed) to work better on a related situation (such as flow at a different speed).

Autoencoder ROM

A simplified model built by compressing flow patterns into a small set of hidden numbers and then reconstructing full patterns from those numbers.

Flow Control

Actively changing a flow to achieve goals such as reducing drag or improving mixing.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture builds on Petros Koumoutsakos' work on AI and fluid mechanics by showing specific neural network tools for predicting flow and designing controls.
  • It connects to talks on reduced-order models because autoencoders offer a nonlinear alternative to traditional linear methods like POD (proper orthogonal decomposition).
  • It reinforces a core theme across the seminar: physical measurements are always partial and incomplete, so models must infer the full flow state from what we can actually observe.

Practical Caution

Machine learning for turbulent flows often overfits to one flow speed, one geometry, or one simulation setup. Claims about transfer learning, control, and super-resolution need to be tested in conditions different from the training data: different flow speeds, different shapes, and different real-world conditions.

Takeaway

The core lesson is that turbulence learning is about inferring hidden flow properties and finding control strategies in a world where flow changes at multiple scales. Deep learning succeeds when the way it represents patterns matches what your sensors measure and what your control needs to do.
