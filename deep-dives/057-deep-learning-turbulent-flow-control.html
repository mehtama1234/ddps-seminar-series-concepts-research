Talk 57 · KTH Royal Institute of Technology

Ricardo Vinuesa: Modeling And Controlling Turbulent Flows Through Deep Learning

This seminar series aims to predict how physical things behave by teaching programs to learn from data instead of only hand-written math. This talk shows how to do that for turbulent flows — using wall sensors to guess hidden flow patterns, training a program on one flow speed and reusing it at another, building simplified models by compressing flow data, sharpening blurry flow images, and testing control strategies by trial and reward in a simulator.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Turbulent flows are chaotic — properties shift wildly across space and happen at many different scales at once. They are costly to measure directly or simulate accurately. For flows near walls (pipes, aircraft wings, channels), the flow far from the wall is nearly invisible without intrusive sensors that physically interfere with the flow itself. Controllers need fast, dependable estimates of the vortex patterns (twisted flow structures) that actually drive the motion.

First-Principles Model

Turbulent flows have properties that change across space but can never be observed completely from one spot. Sensors, simulations, and learning programs each capture only a slice. A learning program called a convolutional neural network (millions of tiny dials tuned by trial and error) can learn patterns from wall measurements and predict velocity (speed and direction) patterns in unseen regions away from the wall. Transfer learning means: train the program at one flow speed, then tweak it to work at another nearby speed, reusing the patterns already learned. An autoencoder is a learning program that squeezes flow data down to a few hidden numbers (compression) and then rebuilds the full flow patterns back out (reconstruction), creating a much simpler model. A GAN (two rival learning programs: one that makes up realistic fake flow images, one that judges which are real) can add fine detail to low-resolution or incomplete flow pictures. Reinforcement learning means: the program suggests a control action in a simulator, gets rewarded if the flow improves, and learns by trial and reward which actions work.

Key Claims

  • Learning programs are now widely used in computational fluid mechanics and turbulent-flow analysis.
  • Non-intrusive sensing — using only wall measurements to guess flow away from the wall — avoids physical sensors that would disturb the flow.
  • Convolutional neural networks (learning programs that detect spatial patterns) outperform old linear methods at predicting turbulent flows.
  • Transfer learning can shift trained patterns from one flow speed to nearby flow speeds.
  • Autoencoders can build simpler nonlinear models by compressing turbulent flow data.
  • GANs and reinforcement learning can create sharp flow images and find control strategies, but must be tested in real conditions, not just simulators.

Worked Example

For non-intrusive sensing, the group builds a direct-numerical-simulation database of turbulent open-channel flow (one wall, symmetry at the top) at friction Reynolds numbers Re_tau = 180 and 550, using a Fourier code. The network reads three wall quantities -- streamwise and spanwise wall-shear-stress and wall pressure -- and predicts the three velocity fluctuations at a chosen wall-normal height. A fully convolutional net (six hidden layers, 3x3 kernels so each output point sees a 9-point neighborhood) predicts the near-wall peak at y+ = 15 with under 1% error in the RMS fluctuation, essentially nailing the streaks.

Farther from the wall the signals decorrelate, so accuracy drops: at y+ = 100 the direct FCN is beaten by an FCN-POD variant that first does POD on the target plane and only predicts the temporal coefficients of the leading modes; the error there is about 26%, which they call acceptable for flow control because you can still locate the large-scale streaks. Transfer learning helps efficiency: freezing the three first (small-scale) layers and retraining only the output-side layers reaches the same accuracy at a new height with over 4x less training time, and initializing the Re_tau=550 network from the 180 weights cuts required training data by about 4x -- important because the 550 run is far more expensive than the 180 run.

On the reduced-order-model side, a beta-variational autoencoder on a wall-resolved LES of flow between two buildings (1,200 snapshots, full sim ~100 million grid points, a 96x192 center plane extracted) reconstructs about 87% of the energy with 5 orthogonal modes, versus only 72% for 5 POD modes -- and its modes stay interpretable (they show the large-scale shedding) where plain CNN-autoencoder modes do not.

One Level Deeper

The convolutional net exploits spatial correlation: a kernel sweeps the 2D wall image and builds feature maps, and stacked convolutions build progressively larger, more turbulence-like structures -- a hierarchy that matches how turbulent scales interact. The linear baseline (extended POD, equivalent to linear stochastic estimation) captures only the superposition of large scales; the CNN adds the nonlinear modulation the linear map misses, which is why the near-wall peak error falls below 1%.

The beta-VAE learns a probabilistic latent code: an encoder outputs a Gaussian q(r|x) with mean mu and std sigma, samples r = mu + sigma * epsilon (epsilon ~ N(0,1), the reparameterization trick), and a decoder reconstructs the field. The loss is a reconstruction term plus beta times a KL penalty that pushes the latent distribution toward a factorized prior: L = E[ -log p(x|r) ] + beta * KL( q(r|x) || prod of marginals ). Raising beta forces statistically independent, near-orthogonal latent variables (they report >99% orthogonality, vs 100% for POD) and fewer active dimensions -- 10 nonzero latents at low beta, only 3 at high beta -- trading reconstruction energy for a parsimonious, disentangled, interpretable basis.

For the PINN-RANS work, the loss is the residual of the 2D incompressible RANS equations inside the domain plus a boundary-condition matching term, with Reynolds stresses solved as unknowns (no turbulence model); automatic differentiation supplies the derivatives, so coarser collocation meshes suffice, and on a separated periodic-hill flow they get ~2-3% error in the mean. Flow control uses deep RL: an agent picks a blowing/suction body-force amplitude to maximize a reward (minimal drag / shortest separation bubble), reaching ~30% bubble reduction versus ~20% for periodic forcing.

Failure Boundary

Predictions degrade monotonically with distance from the wall because the wall signal and the flow above it decorrelate: near-wall peak error is under 1%, but by y+ = 100 it is ~20-26%, good enough to locate large structures but not to impose as a quantitative wall model. Predicting inward (outer flow -> near wall) is harder still: you lose the small-scale imprint that lives at the wall, and a CNN's receptive field covers only a small part of the large input scales, so the spectra miss small scales and sit near 20% error -- too high for a wall boundary condition.

The autoencoder ROM was only demonstrated on a 2D center-plane; scaling training to 3D runs into TensorFlow parallelization limits, so the compression that looks cheap in 2D is not yet shown to hold in 3D. PINNs will always return some solution even when the boundary data are inconsistent, so a too-coarse or poorly distributed collocation mesh silently propagates bad boundary information into a plausible-but-wrong field. And deep-RL control is expensive -- hundreds of episodes even in 2D, more in a turbulent channel -- so the exploration cost, not the network, is the limiting factor.

Important Terms

Wall-Bounded Turbulence

Chaotic, swirling flow shaped by solid boundaries such as pipes, aircraft wings, or building surfaces.

Non-Intrusive Sensing

Guessing flow properties without putting physical sensors into the flow that would change what you are trying to measure.

Transfer Learning

Training a learning program on one situation (such as flow at one speed), then tweaking it to work well on a related situation (such as flow at a different speed), reusing patterns already learned.

Autoencoder ROM

A simplified model built by squeezing flow patterns down into a few hidden numbers (compression) and then rebuilding full patterns back out (reconstruction).

Flow Control

Actively changing a flow to reach goals such as cutting drag, speeding up mixing, or improving safety.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk extends work on pairing artificial intelligence with fluid mechanics by showing concrete learning programs for predicting flows and finding controls.
  • It connects to talks about simplified flow models because autoencoders offer a curved, nonlinear alternative to older straight-line methods like POD (a standard way to extract the most important flow patterns).
  • It reinforces a recurring theme: real measurements are always incomplete, so models must fill in the missing pieces of the full flow state from whatever we can actually see.

Practical Caution

Learning programs for turbulent flows often overfit — they work only at the exact flow speed, geometry, or simulation they trained on. Whenever researchers claim transfer learning, control strategies, or sharpened images actually work, those claims must be tested at different flow speeds, different shapes, and in real-world conditions, not just the training setup.

Takeaway

The core lesson is that learning turbulent flows means inferring hidden flow patterns and searching for control strategies in a world where motion happens at many scales. Learning programs succeed when the way they represent and compress patterns matches what your sensors can actually measure and what your controller needs to do.
