Talk 44 · Caltech

Kaushik Bhattacharya: Multi-Scale Modeling And Neural Operators

This whole series is about teaching computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from data instead of writing down all the rules by hand. In engineering, materials have behavior that depends on incredibly tiny details—atoms, defects, the arrangement of crystals—but you can't actually simulate every single atom when you're designing a bridge or an airplane wing. This talk is about a learning program that learns the effective, simplified rules that capture what a zillion tiny details do, and then plugs those learned rules into the big-scale simulation, keeping accuracy without the crushing cost.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

When you try to predict how a material will behave, the real physics happens at many different scales at once—the level of individual electrons, the level of atoms, the level of tiny defects and crystal structures, and all the way up to the size of a real part. You could try to simulate every single detail, but that takes forever and costs too much. You could just average everything and ignore the details, but then you miss the important stuff that actually controls whether the material breaks or bends. So the real problem is: how do you capture the tiny-scale physics without having to compute it all?

First-Principles Model

Multiscale modeling is a strategy that breaks the problem into layers. The big layer (the scale that engineers actually care about) sends information down to the tiny layer and asks, 'Given these conditions, what effective response do I get?' The tiny layer does the hard work and sends back an answer. A neural operator is a learning program that learns exactly that map—from the tiny-scale physics to the coarse, effective answer. Instead of writing down the equations by hand or running the expensive tiny-scale simulation every time, you train a program to learn this map. If you train it carefully on examples that match the cases you'll actually encounter, you can drop it into the big-scale simulation and get accurate answers for a tiny fraction of the cost.

Key Claims

  • Material behavior is controlled by physics happening at many different scales at the same time—tiny details like atoms and defects all the way up to the size you can see with your eyes.
  • Multiscale modeling splits the job into layers: the big scale controls what questions to ask the tiny scale, and the tiny scale returns what actually happens.
  • The practical problem is that running the tiny-scale simulation over and over inside the big-scale simulation is too slow and too expensive.
  • Neural operators learn the map from input to output as functions—like learning a whole physical rule, not just learning numbers in a fixed list.
  • This is different from ordinary learning programs because you are learning a rule that applies to whole functions (fields that vary in space or time), not just learning a single answer from fixed-size numbers.
  • If trained correctly, a neural operator can keep the important tiny-scale details while making the big-scale simulation run fast enough to be useful.

Worked Example

For an elliptic operator (map from coefficient field a(x) to solution u(x)) on 421×421 grids, FNO training on downsampled 64×64 data achieves ~5% relative error. The learned operator generalizes to test data at 64×64, 100×100, 256×256 resolutions—the same error (~5%) regardless of resolution, showing the architecture captures the true functional operator rather than grid-dependent weights. A crystal plasticity example with 3D FFT calculations and 30,000 training trajectories (strain history to stress history) achieves ~10% total error. DFT electronic structure: learning electronic fields reduces reconstruction error from direct energy prediction by ~10x when evaluating total energy.

For single-processor performance: traditional Johnson-Cook material model costs 50 seconds per Taylor anvil simulation; the learned neural operator material model costs 450 seconds (~9x slower). Offline cost: generating 10^6 trajectories + training (~10^4 GPU steps) amortizes over many runs; one full concurrent multiscale simulation would cost ~10^8 single-CPU units.

One Level Deeper

PCANet architecture: projection P (via PCA or autoencoder) maps infinite-dimensional function space to finite-dimensional latent space with dimension d; neural network θ operates in latent space; lifting operator L reconstructs output function. For operator ψ: ψ ≈ L ∘ network ∘ P, enabling resolution independence. The PCA step captures statistical structure of the data distribution and is the key to generalization across grid resolutions.

Recurrent Neural Operators (RNO) replace full-history input with state-update dynamics: σ(t) = f(F(t), dF/dt, ζ(t)); dζ/dt = g(F, dF/dt, ζ), where ζ is learned hidden state. Fourier Neural Operators use integral kernels in Fourier space: (Kv)(x) = F^(-1)[K̂(ω) ⊙ F[v](ω)], learning spectral convolution directly—this is a Fourier-domain integral operator approximating the Green's function for PDE solution operators.

Failure Boundary

PCANet requires sufficient training data; higher-dimensional reduced spaces need exponentially more trajectories. Higher PCA dimensions d → error first decreases then increases (overfitting regime) when training data is small. Architecture is non-causal by design; while data teaches causality through examples, extrapolation beyond training deformation range is unreliable. Constraints: (1) must know entire strain/deformation history a priori; (2) separate model needed for each initial microstructure; (3) inertial effects not modeled in micro-scale; (4) Fourier operators expensive at evaluation time (O(n log n) FFT per forward pass). For vastly different physical regimes (linear vs. nonlinear PDE, bifurcations), transfer learning fails. Hidden state discovery works for weakly-nonlinear systems but struggles with strongly chaotic or switching regimes.

Important Terms

Multiscale Modeling

A way of predicting what happens by connecting physics descriptions that work at different sizes—from tiny atomic scales up to human-sized or building-sized scales.

Neural Operator

A learning program that learns the connection (the map) between one set of functions and another. For example, it learns how a stress field at the tiny scale determines the material's response at the big scale.

Function Space

A collection of all possible functions (mathematical rules that vary smoothly in space or time) rather than just a list of numbers. When learning programs work with function spaces, they are learning rules that apply everywhere, not just at discrete points.

Coarse Scale

The large-scale view that engineers use—the level of a whole bridge, airplane wing, or building—where you want the final answer.

Fine Scale

The small-scale physics—like atoms, defects in crystals, or microscopic structure—where the detailed behavior happens but is too small and numerous to compute directly.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk is a centerpiece in the series' journey through operator learning and connects directly to earlier talks about specific operator methods like Fourier Neural Operators and DeepONets, but from the viewpoint of what multiscale modeling actually needs.
  • It roots operator learning in classical physics and materials science instead of treating it as a generic trick that works on any data.
  • It also touches on uncertainty and trust: when you use a learned approximation of the tiny-scale physics, errors from that learned piece get passed up to the big-scale prediction, so you need to understand what you learned and whether it is honest.

Practical Caution

A neural operator only works if it has learned the right map on the right kinds of cases. If you train it on one kind of microstructure or one range of loads and boundary conditions, but then test it on a completely different microstructure or load, it can fail badly. The program might fit the training data perfectly but give nonsense on new situations. You need to be honest about which scenarios the learned map is reliable for, and test it hard on cases it has never seen.

Takeaway

When operator learning works, it works because the thing the program learned to do matches what the physics actually does: it learned a true map between changing fields (like temperature or stress) at one scale to the response at another scale, and it correctly handles the constraints and boundary conditions. That alignment between the learned computation and the physical reality is what makes the whole thing work.
