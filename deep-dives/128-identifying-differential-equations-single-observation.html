Talk 128 · Georgia Institute of Technology

Sung Ha Kang: Identifying Differential Equations from Single Observation with Numerical Methods

This lecture shows numerical methods for discovering the equations that govern a system from a single noisy measurement taken over time.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Finding equations usually works better with many separate measurement runs or clean derivative data. But often you have only one noisy sequence of measurements, which makes taking derivatives, picking the right terms, and solving for the equation coefficients all unstable and unreliable.

First-Principles Model

The governing equation is built as a sum of candidate terms—basic pieces like powers of the variable or combinations of derivatives. This turns discovery into solving a linear system where the columns are the candidate terms tested against the data and the unknown values are how much each term contributes. The talk covers methods including IDENT, step-by-step denoised differentiation, subspace pursuit (a method for picking the most important terms), weak-form recovery (using integrals instead of point-by-point values), and group subspace pursuit to handle noise and limited measurements better.

Key Claims

  • The problem is finding differential equations from a single noisy measurement recorded over time.
  • A library of candidate terms—built from the data—forms the feature matrix used for recovery.
  • The unknown coefficients (telling you how much each term matters) are found using sparse methods or structured linear algebra.
  • Applying noise reduction before taking derivatives helps make the derivative estimates more stable.
  • Integral-based methods handle high noise and derivatives of higher order better than point-by-point approaches.
  • Group subspace pursuit extends sparse recovery to select related terms as groups rather than individually.

Important Terms

Equation Identification

Finding the differential equation that describes a system by working backward from observed data.

Feature Matrix

A table whose columns hold each candidate term calculated on the observed data.

Subspace Pursuit

An algorithm that finds a small set of candidate terms that best explain the data.

Weak Form

A version of the equation written using integrals, which avoids needing to calculate derivatives at each point (making it more stable when data is noisy).

Denoised Differentiation

Computing derivatives by first reducing noise in the measurement, then calculating the rate of change.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk builds on symbolic regression (finding equations as formulas), weak-form discovery (using integrals for stability), sparse recovery (picking the right small set of terms), and numerical differentiation (computing rates of change from data).
  • It extends the WSINDy and WENDy lecture by tackling the hardest case: when you have only one measurement sequence.
  • It ties to model discovery where making equations interpretable and working with scarce data are both critical.

Practical Caution

Identifying an equation from one measurement is highly sensitive to which candidate terms you include in the library and how you model the noise. Finding a sparse (few-term) equation does not mean you've found the true equation without testing it separately on new data.

Takeaway

The lesson is that finding an equation from a single trajectory only becomes practical by imposing strong structure: sparse candidate libraries, noise reduction, and integral-based (weak-form) methods.
