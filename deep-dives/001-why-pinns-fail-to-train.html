Talk 1 · University of Pennsylvania

Paris Perdikaris: When And Why PINNs Fail To Train

This whole series is about getting computers to predict physical behavior by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. One popular way is to let a learning program (a program with millions of dials it tunes by trial and error until its answers look right) also receive the laws of physics as it learns. This talk is about why that approach, called a PINN, so often refuses to learn well: the trial-and-error tuning gets stuck because some of the physics rules 'shout' much louder than others, so the program never lands on an answer that respects all of them.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

PINNs turn a physics prediction problem into a learning task: fit data, respect the boundary conditions (the fixed facts at the edges), respect the initial conditions (what things looked like at the start), and satisfy the physics rule all at once. That sounds sensible, but the computer's trial-and-error process may not push each of these demands equally. The direction and strength that tell the learning program which way to adjust its dials (what we call a gradient signal) can be weak or even point different ways for different constraints. High-frequency wiggling, multiple scales, and boundaries especially suffer because the signals pointing toward satisfying each constraint can be weak or work against each other.

First-Principles Model

A PINN uses a learning program to represent the answer to a physics rule. A special mathematical trick called automatic differentiation lets the computer compute what the rule demands at any spot, turn that demand into a score for how wrong the answer is, and then nudge the dials in the direction that makes the score go down. The real problem being solved is not just the physics rule itself; it is a constrained guessing game: many simultaneous goals must be met by adjusting the dials. If that guessing process is stubborn (needing tiny nudges) or biased (strongly preferring some wiggles over others), the physics rule can be simple while learning still fails.

Key Claims

  • Physics can be added through the learning program's structure (certain shapes of dials work better) or through the score (certain penalties favor physics). PINNs mostly use penalties in the score.
  • PINNs are unusual in that they penalize the program's output and how fast it changes, not just the dial settings themselves.
  • Even simple physics rules can fail if the trial-and-error tuning cannot satisfy the boundary conditions or the rapid wiggles.
  • The trial-and-error process for PINNs can require tiny adjustment steps, making training impractically slow if done the usual way.
  • Different demands in the score can produce vastly different adjustment signals, so one requirement gets met while another is nearly ignored.
  • A mathematical tool called neural tangent kernel analysis explains spectral bias: the learning program picks up smooth, slow wiggles before sharp, fast wiggles.

Important Terms

Physics-Informed Neural Network

A learning program trained to guess an answer to a physics rule while also being punished when the physics rule is violated at the edges or start.

Composite Loss

A single score made by adding up several different scores: how wrong the guess is on the data, how badly the boundary conditions are violated, how badly the starting conditions are violated, and how badly the physics rule is violated.

Gradient Flow Stiffness

A tuning problem where the continuous trial-and-error process has some parts that change very fast and others that change very slowly, forcing stable methods to take tiny steps.

Gradient Imbalance

A failure where different demands in the score produce very different adjustment signals, so some physics demands are effectively ignored while others make the program change.

Spectral Bias

The tendency of learning programs trained by trial-and-error adjustment to pick up smooth, gentle changes in their input before sharp, rapid changes.

Connections Across The Series

  • This is the starting point because it shows the difference between the idea of a PINN and whether it can actually learn in practice.
  • It connects to later talks about alternative math forms, automatic rebalancing of demands, learning operators instead of single answers, and learning program designs that respect physical laws.
  • It teaches a habit: never ask only whether the physics rule is correct; always ask whether the trial-and-error process can actually use it to improve the program.

Practical Caution

Do not trust a low score for physics rule violations as proof that the learned answer is correct in the real world. The choice of how to weight different demands, where to check the physics rule, the structure of the learning program, the tuning method chosen, and the types of wiggles that exist in the true answer can all determine whether this method actually solves what you care about.

Takeaway

PINNs are optimization systems that must balance many goals at once, not automatic solvers. They succeed only when the trial-and-error process can send each physics demand into the right parts of the learning program.
