Talk 13 · University of Muenster

Mario Ohlberger: Model Reduction With Adaptive Enrichment For Large Scale PDE-Constrained Optimization

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of hand-written rules alone. One way to do that is to build a faster stand-in (a simplified version that runs quickly) for the slow mathematical rules that describe real physics. This talk is about adaptive enrichment: instead of building one giant simplified model upfront (which costs a lot), you start with a small cheap one and grow it as you need it during optimization, so you only pay for accuracy where the algorithm actually looks.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

When you want a computer to optimize a design (say, the shape of a wing) while obeying physics, you must solve the physics rules (written as equations describing how air flows or heat spreads) many times, each time with different design choices. To make this fast, you can build a simplified stand-in model that runs in seconds instead of hours. But building that simplified model itself costs a lot of expensive full solves upfront (the 'offline' stage), and if you do build it, the simplified model might be wrong in the parts of design-space the optimizer actually visits. For hard problems like battery degradation, uncertainty handling, or multiscale systems, even the initial setup is too expensive to afford.

First-Principles Model

A PDE-constrained optimization problem (a math puzzle where you change design or control variables while a physics equation keeps being solved) must solve that physics equation over and over. A simplified stand-in model can speed this up, but the optimizer needs to know when to trust it. Adaptive enrichment works like this: start with a small simplified model. During optimization, watch how wrong it is (using error estimates that you can compute quickly after each solve). When the simplified model's error is small, use it; when error grows large, pay for a full expensive solve and add that solution to your simplified model to make it better for next time. Often you wrap this in a trust-region step: a safety zone where you only use the simplified model if you know its error is controlled.

Key Claims

  • Adaptive enrichment steps beyond the old split where you do all the expensive work upfront and then queries are cheap.
  • The focus is on large-scale optimization problems governed by physics equations, plus related problems like uncertainty quantification (testing how sensitive answers are to small changes in inputs).
  • Building a rich simplified model ahead of time can require solving the full physics so many times that it is too expensive to afford.
  • To know when a simplified model is accurate, you need a quickly-computable error estimate that tells you how far off the simplified solution is.
  • Trust-region methods (a way to limit optimizer steps to regions where you trust the approximate model) can control whether the simplified model is reliable enough.
  • For certain types of physics (like shocks that form suddenly, or equations where the width of the essential information decays slowly), a linear simplified model might not work well and needs nonlinear extensions.

Worked Example

For lithium-ion battery degradation modeling with a 1.7 million degree-of-freedom finite volume discretization, the full high-dimensional charging sweep took 6.5 hours of computation. Applying reduced basis methods with adaptive enrichment and empirical operator interpolation brought this down to 1.5 seconds maximum simulation time, yielding a relative L2 error of 10^-3 in both ion concentration and electrostatic potential. This represents approximately 280-290× speedup while maintaining fidelity across complex pore-scale electrode geometries.

The offline phase requires solving several parameter-dependent snapshots to construct the reduced basis via POD-Greedy algorithm. For a 2D parameter domain in heat transfer optimization (trust region method with 710 optimization steps), local subdomain enrichment required solving only 709 local sub-problems across the domain without ever solving the full global high-dimensional model—averaging about 11 local problems per subdomain per optimization step.

One Level Deeper

The method combines three ingredients: (1) reduced basis projection—expressing high-dimensional solutions u_h in a low N-dimensional space spanned by basis functions φ_i, so u(μ) ≈ Σ c_i(μ)φ_i; (2) offline-online decomposition exploiting affine parameter dependence to precompute reduced operators A_h^red = V^T A(μ) V where V contains basis functions; and (3) empirical operator interpolation (DEIM) for non-linearities, replacing expensive nonlinear operators with interpolated surrogates A_μ^DEIM = ΣV_r a_r(μ) with discrete basis V_r.

The trust region approach for optimization minimizes a corrected reduced functional that accounts for non-conformity between primal and dual reduced spaces. The key correction is to the Lagrangian: J_corr = J_red + (ψ_red, r_primal)_L2, where r_primal is the residual of the primal state equation tested against the dual solution from a different space. Error estimates for the functional and gradient exhibit quadratic convergence in primal-dual residuals, guaranteeing certified model quality within adaptively growing trust regions.

Failure Boundary

Classical offline-online reduced basis methods fail in large-scale optimization because the offline phase requires solving many high-dimensional problems to populate the basis, which becomes prohibitive for expensive simulations (e.g., 6.5 hours per solve). The greedy POD algorithm assumes fast decay of Kolmogorov width—failure occurs with hyperbolic problems exhibiting shocks or slow polynomial decay, where even N=100 basis functions cannot capture the solution manifold's richness.

Adaptive enrichment can stagnate if the trust region radius shrinks too aggressively or if the error estimator overestimates, causing the optimization solver to stop early without reaching sufficient accuracy. For multiscale or strongly nonlinear problems where the kernel structure is unknown, DEIM hyper-reduction introduces an additional approximation layer that can accumulate error if the number of interpolation points is chosen too small, leading to observable loss of accuracy in gradient information.

Important Terms

PDE-Constrained Optimization

A math puzzle where you want to pick the best design or control variables (like wing shape or material thickness), but the variables must satisfy a physical rule written as an equation (a PDE: a rule that says how something changes across space and time, like how heat spreads or air flows).

Adaptive Enrichment

Growing your simplified model during the optimization by adding new physics solves when the current simplified model is not accurate enough in the region where the optimizer is looking.

Reduced Basis

A low-dimensional simplified model built by collecting solutions from expensive physics solves and finding the common patterns (like a compressed picture that captures the essential idea without storing every detail).

A Posteriori Error Estimator

A quickly-computable score that tells you how wrong your simplified model's solution is after you have already computed it, so you know when to trust it and when to do a full expensive solve instead.

Trust Region

A safety zone in the design space where you believe the simplified model is accurate enough to guide the optimization algorithm's next step, so you only use the simplified model in that zone and do expensive full solves elsewhere.

Connections Across The Series

  • This extends the earlier talks about simplified models (reduced-order models) from just prediction to optimization loops, where you must solve the physics equation repeatedly and guarantee the simplified model is reliable.
  • It connects to inverse problems and control design because optimization means deciding which solves can use the cheap simplified model and which need the expensive full physics.
  • It also hints at the later talks on nonlinear simplified models: when the physics has sharp changes (shocks) or certain mathematical properties (slow Kolmogorov-width decay), a linear simplified model cannot keep up and needs nonlinear tricks.

Practical Caution

Do not build a giant simplified model upfront without asking: do I actually need it? If the optimization algorithm only visits a small corner of the design space, adaptive enrichment saves money by spending expensive full solves only where the algorithm actually goes, not everywhere.

Takeaway

Reduced-order models (simplified stand-ins for expensive physics solves) in optimization must be managed on the fly: use the cheap model when it is accurate enough, enrich it (make it better by adding more data) when needed, and let error estimates control when to switch.
