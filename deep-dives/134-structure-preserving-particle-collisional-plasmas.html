Talk 134 · University of Washington

Jingwei Hu: Structure-Preserving Particle Method for Collisional Plasmas

This whole series asks whether computers can predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of only using hand-written math. This talk focuses on plasma—a soup of charged particles that push and pull on each other both through their electric and magnetic fields and by colliding head-on. The challenge is to build a simulation that correctly tracks all these effects while obeying the rules that physics demands: mass stays constant, momentum is conserved, energy doesn't vanish, and disorder always increases. This talk shows how to design a particle simulation method that keeps all those rules intact.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Plasma is made of fast-moving charged particles (electrons and ions). When they crash into each other, they collide (called Coulomb collisions—the push and pull from their electric charges). At the same time, the particles create electromagnetic fields that travel through space and push other particles around. Engineers describe this tangle with the Vlasov-Maxwell-Landau equation (Vlasov tracks how many particles have each velocity in each spot; Maxwell describes the electric and magnetic fields; Landau describes collisions). But here's the hard part: when you write a computer program to step this forward bit by bit, you have to be careful. Any mistake in the calculation can break conservation laws—mass vanishes, energy appears from nowhere, or entropy (disorder) decreases when it shouldn't. A simulation that breaks these laws produces plasma behavior that isn't real.

First-Principles Model

Start with a variational formulation (a way to write the collision equations as a principle that minimizes something, like finding the lowest point in a valley). Regularize it (smooth out mathematical singularities—places where the math has infinities or wild jumps). This gives you a particle discretization—a recipe for how to step particles forward. The collisions become a deterministic effective force (a push that is calculated exactly, not by random chance). This force works alongside classical PIC (particle-in-cell: a standard plasma method where you track individual particles moving through space while gluing their charge onto a grid, then solving for fields on that grid). The result: the discretization automatically conserves mass and momentum and energy. Disorder (entropy) always increases by the right amount—exactly as real collisions do.

Key Claims

  • The Vlasov-Maxwell-Landau equation is the first-principles description of plasma: it tracks particle velocities, electromagnetic fields, and how collisions reshape the velocity distribution.
  • A new particle method can model transport, electromagnetic fields, and Coulomb collisions all together without breaking conservation laws.
  • The method comes from starting with a variational formulation of the Landau collision operator and smoothing out mathematical singularities.
  • The discretization preserves the total mass, total momentum, and total energy.
  • Entropy (disorder) always increases in the correct direction, just like in real collisions.
  • The deterministic collision force integrates naturally with the standard particle-in-cell simulation approach.

Worked Example

Collisional plasma (ions + electrons, magnetic field B). Kinetic equations (Vlasov-Boltzmann): ∂f_s/∂t + v·∇_x f_s + (q_s/m_s)(E + v × B)·∇_v f_s = C[f_s, f_s'] (collision term). Structure-preserving particle method: discrete particles carry (x,v,q), updated via: Δx = v_old Δt, Δv = (q/m)(E + v_mid × B) Δt + collision_kick. Conserves charge and momentum exactly (C_i = Σ m_i Δv_i = 0), energy to machine precision.

One Level Deeper

Staggered leap-frog integrator: v_{n-1/2}, x_n, v_{n+1/2}. Collision operator C[f] computed via pair-wise binary collisions: Δv_post-collision ∝ relative velocity angle, magnitude calibrated by cross-section σ(v_rel). Energy conservation: collision updates chosen to scatter energy but preserve total kinetic energy (within Coulomb potential). Ampere's law ∇ × B = μ₀ J updated via particle current J = Σ q_i δ(x-x_i) v_i.

Failure Boundary

Large time-steps (Δt > ω_c^{-1}, cyclotron freq) cause spurious trapping modes in EM fields. Collision operator scales as O(n_part²) in naive form; tree-codes needed for large plasmas (>10⁶ particles). High-velocity-space resolution required near sound speed; rare high-energy events (e-e collisions) need importance sampling or adaptive refinement.

Important Terms

Vlasov-Maxwell-Landau Equation

A rule that couples three things together: how many particles have each velocity at each location (Vlasov), the electric and magnetic fields they create (Maxwell), and how head-on collisions between charged particles push velocities around (Landau).

Landau Collision Operator

A mathematical rule that describes how charged particles redistribute their speeds and directions when they collide with each other due to the push from their electric charges.

Structure-Preserving Method

A numerical recipe designed to keep the conservation laws and dissipation rules of the physics intact as you step the simulation forward.

Entropy Dissipation

The one-way growth of disorder in the system—a measure of randomness that always increases when particles collide, spreading organized motion into heat.

Particle-in-Cell

A plasma simulation method where you track individual particles moving through space and glue their charge onto a grid; the grid then solves for the electric and magnetic fields everywhere, and those fields push the particles around in return.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk brings together structure-preserving numerics (methods that keep physics laws intact), kinetic theory (the math of particles and velocities), and plasma particle simulation.
  • It builds on earlier talks in the series by emphasizing that numerical methods must respect the conservation laws and dissipation rules that define the physics model.
  • It shows that the core DDPS theme holds for plasma: a simulation should obey the same laws that the real physics obeys.

Practical Caution

Preserving conservation laws and entropy dissipation is necessary, but it doesn't guarantee the simulation is correct or fast enough. You have to test it on different plasma scenarios: damping (how wiggles die away), instability (how waves or structures grow), and shock-like behavior (sharp jumps in density or temperature). Real plasma is complicated; keeping the math laws intact is only the first step.

Takeaway

When you build a particle simulation of plasma, the method should preserve the conservation and dissipation laws that the kinetic equation demands. This is the foundation of getting real physics out.
