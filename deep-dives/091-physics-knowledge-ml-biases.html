Talk 91 · University of Cambridge

Alice Cicirello: Integrating Physics Knowledge In Machine Learning Strategies

This lecture organizes physics-informed machine learning around four ways to inject physics: through the data itself, through the model's built-in structure, through how you train it, and through a separate model that captures what the physics equations got wrong, using friction-force identification as an example.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

ML models trained only from data often fail when predicting new situations, make unrealistic predictions that violate physics, and work poorly with limited measurements. Real engineering problems have partial physics knowledge—some equations you know, some you don't—plus noisy measurements and limited data. The question is: where and how should you feed physics knowledge into your learning process?

First-Principles Model

Physics knowledge can enter machine learning in four places. Observational bias changes the data by selecting or creating measurements that physics tells you matter. Inductive bias builds physics constraints, symmetries, conservation rules, or boundaries directly into the model's structure. Learning bias modifies the training procedure—the loss function (what you tell the model to minimize), hard constraints it must satisfy, or how you search for the best model. Discrepancy bias adds a separate model that captures known gaps between your partial physics equations and what you actually observe. Friction identification shows how sparse system identification (finding which forces matter most) and Gaussian processes (a method that represents uncertainty in predictions) can use all four types of bias.

Key Claims

  • Physics knowledge improves prediction on new data when you have small to medium amounts of training data and can enforce that predictions stay physically consistent even with large data.
  • Training only on data without physics can introduce observational bias (the data you have is misleading) and physically impossible predictions.
  • Observational bias can be controlled by using physics-informed data sources or artificially creating new training examples that physics tells you should exist.
  • Inductive bias embeds prior knowledge such as boundaries, conservation laws (like energy or momentum), and symmetries (unchanged behavior under transformations) into the model's structure.
  • Learning bias changes the training process: the loss function you minimize, hard rules the model must obey, or the algorithm you use to find the best model.
  • Discrepancy bias uses partial physics knowledge or corrects for known gaps between idealized equations and real data, as shown through friction-force identification.

Important Terms

Observational Bias

Physics-informed choices about which data to collect, which to create artificially, or which to emphasize when training a model.

Inductive Bias

A built-in restriction or assumption that the model carries from the start, baked into its structure or how it represents information.

Learning Bias

Physics information introduced through the loss function (what you minimize), hard constraints the model must satisfy, or the optimization method itself.

Discrepancy Bias

A separate model trained to correct for known gaps or errors between an imperfect physics model and real observations.

Friction Identification

Estimating friction forces or friction laws in a moving system from data and physics constraints, often when friction is the main unknown.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture provides a clear taxonomy that unifies earlier talks on physics-informed machine learning, physics-guided machine learning, and physics-enhanced machine learning.
  • It connects to PINNs (physics-informed neural networks), sparse system identification (finding the few most important forces), Gaussian-process methods with physics priors, and discrepancy modeling.
  • It gives precise language for deciding where physics should enter a model, moving beyond vague advice to simply 'add physics'.

Practical Caution

Adding physics knowledge the wrong way can fail or mislead. For example, adding a penalty term (a soft rule) in your loss function may be much weaker than building a hard constraint into the model's structure when a physical law must absolutely never be broken.

Takeaway

The core lesson is that physics can enter at four points: the data, the model's structure, how you train it, and the discrepancy model. Good physics-informed machine learning (SciML) chooses where to insert physics knowledge deliberately rather than adding it everywhere.
