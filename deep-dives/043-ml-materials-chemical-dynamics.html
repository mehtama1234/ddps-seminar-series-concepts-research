Talk 43 · Los Alamos National Laboratory

Sergei Tretiak: Machine Learning For Materials And Chemical Dynamics

This whole series is about getting computers to predict physical behavior by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. One common problem in chemistry and materials science is that running accurate quantum physics simulations is very expensive—you need thousands of them to hunt for new materials, and each one takes hours. This talk shows how a learning program can learn to mimic those expensive simulations, running millions of times faster. But there's a catch: the program can only learn what the examples showed it. If atoms are far apart in a molecule, they can influence each other through electric charge or attraction—but if the program was trained only to look at each atom and its close neighbors, it will miss those long-distance effects and give you wrong answers.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Getting precise predictions from quantum physics and molecular simulations costs a lot of computer time. To discover new materials, you need to run thousands of these simulations, each testing different atomic arrangements and conditions. A learning program can learn to connect a structure's shape directly to its properties much faster, but it breaks in two common ways. First, if the training examples didn't show a particular arrangement of atoms, the program has no idea what to predict. Second, atoms can influence each other across distance—through spread-out electric charge, attraction between distant molecules, or changes in electronic states (how electrons occupy energy levels). If the program only looks at each atom and its immediate neighbors, it will miss all those long-distance effects.

First-Principles Model

A molecular learning program takes the positions of atoms as input and predicts how much energy the structure has, what forces push on each atom, or what properties it has. The simplest approach—local models—treats each atom's energy as coming mostly from atoms sitting nearby. This is fast and can handle huge molecules, but real chemistry doesn't work that way. Atoms can influence each other across the whole molecule through several channels: electric charge spread throughout (not pointlike), how that charge reshuffles itself, attraction pulling distant molecules toward each other, and shifts in electronic energy levels. To capture these longer-distance effects, researchers add layers that let each atom receive information from all other atoms, step by step. Some models use a cycle where a prediction for one atom feeds back and helps predict another atom, repeating until everything stabilizes. Others add a charge-equilibration step: a calculation that figures out how electric charge actually spreads through the molecule to satisfy the forces it creates.

Key Claims

  • A learning program can be trained to mimic expensive chemistry simulations, producing answers in milliseconds instead of hours.
  • The program can only predict configurations it has seen before, so where and how you gather training data sets a hard ceiling on accuracy.
  • Active learning (having the program flag cases where it is least sure, running a real simulation at those cases, and retraining) automatically fills in the worst gaps.
  • If you design the program to predict each atom's energy only from nearby atoms, it runs fast but will miss electric charge spread across the molecule, how charge redistributes itself, attraction between distant molecules, and changes in electronic energy levels.
  • You can add mechanisms to capture long-distance effects: layers where atoms exchange information step by step, cycles where predictions feed back and re-adjust, or a charge-equilibration calculation.
  • Running a learned model in a real simulation requires it to stay stable as atoms move around, not just to match static measurements in the lab.

Worked Example

Illustrative (the talk states orders of magnitude but not a single benchmarked table of numbers). A machine-learned interatomic potential is trained to reproduce quantum (DFT-level) energies and forces for a molecule or material. Where one DFT single point of a modest system might take hours on a cluster, the trained surrogate returns energy and per-atom forces in milliseconds -- the speaker's claim is roughly a million-fold speedup, which is what lets you run the thousands-to-millions of evaluations a materials search or a molecular-dynamics trajectory needs.

Active learning is the loop that decides which expensive calculations to actually run. You start with a small training set, run molecular dynamics with the current model, and at each configuration estimate the model's uncertainty (for example the disagreement across an ensemble of models). Whenever the uncertainty crosses a threshold, you flag that configuration, run one real DFT calculation there, add it to the training set, and retrain. The gaps that hurt most get filled automatically, so accuracy rises where the trajectory actually goes rather than uniformly.

The failure mode made concrete: a local model that only reads each atom's near neighbors can look excellent on a held-out test set of static structures, then drift once you run dynamics. As atoms move, the trajectory wanders into configurations the training never showed, and any regime dominated by long-range charge or electrostatics -- which a nearest-neighbor model structurally cannot see -- gives wrong forces, so the simulation becomes unstable or unphysical.

One Level Deeper

A machine-learned potential approximates the potential energy surface E(R) as a function of atomic positions R, and forces come from its gradient: F_i = -dE/dR_i. The locality approximation writes E = sum_i epsilon_i where each atomic energy epsilon_i depends only on neighbors within a cutoff radius. That is what makes the cost scale linearly with system size and lets you reach huge systems, but it structurally cannot represent effects that reach beyond the cutoff: delocalized (spread-out, non-pointlike) charge, charge redistribution, dispersion attraction between distant fragments, and shifts in electronic occupation.

To restore nonlocality the talk names three mechanisms. Message-passing / interaction layers let each atom's representation receive information from all others in repeated rounds, so influence propagates step by step across the molecule. Self-consistent cycles feed a prediction for one atom back to re-adjust others and iterate until the fields stabilize (a fixed-point iteration). Charge-equilibration schemes solve an explicit electrostatics problem -- distribute charge q_i to minimize an electrostatic-plus-chemical-hardness energy subject to total-charge conservation -- so the model computes how charge actually spreads to satisfy the forces it creates, rather than assuming fixed per-atom charges.

Training minimizes a combined loss on energies and forces, roughly L = sum ( E_pred - E_DFT )^2 + lambda * sum || F_pred - F_DFT ||^2 over the reference set. The force term matters because dynamics is driven by gradients, not by matching energies alone.

Failure Boundary

The hard ceiling is the training distribution: the model can only predict configurations similar to what it was shown, so wherever you sampled data sets the reliability. A model validated on static held-out structures can pass and still fail in a running simulation, because time evolution pushes atoms into unseen arrangements -- the practical caution the speaker stresses. Stability under dynamics, not static test error, is the real test.

The second boundary is physics the architecture cannot represent. If a regime is governed by long-range electrostatics, charge transfer, dispersion, or changes in electronic state, a purely local model will be wrong there no matter how much local data you add; you need interaction layers, self-consistency, or charge equilibration built in. Active learning mitigates coverage gaps but cannot invent a physical channel the model has no terms for.

Important Terms

Machine-Learned Potential

A program trained on examples to predict how much energy a molecule has and what forces push on each atom, based on where the atoms sit.

Active Learning

A loop: the program makes a prediction, flags the configurations where it is most uncertain, you run a real (expensive) simulation at those cases, then retrain the program with the new data.

Locality Approximation

A simplification where you assume each atom's energy depends mostly on atoms sitting right next to it, not on atoms far away in the molecule.

Nonlocal Interaction

A physical effect where atoms or charge far apart in a molecule influence what happens at one location, such as electric attraction or charge that spreads across distance.

Charge Equilibration

A calculation step that figures out how electric charge distributes itself through a molecular system to match the forces and attractions it creates.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture connects to learning from data: deciding which examples to collect is as important as choosing what the program looks like.
  • It also relates to multiscale modeling, where the rules for single atoms must combine to give right answers at the material scale.
  • It reinforces the series theme that when you cannot collect data for every possible case, teaching the program the actual physics rules you do know helps it extrapolate safely.

Practical Caution

A program that looks good when tested on held-back data can suddenly fail once you use it to simulate chemistry over time. As the simulation runs, atomic positions shift, and the program encounters arrangements it never saw during training. If the simulation ventures into a regime where distant atoms and overall charge distribution dominate the behavior, the program will stumble because its design only lets it see nearby atoms.

Takeaway

Molecular learning is a stand-in tool for expensive quantum and molecular physics. Whether it works depends on two things: where you collected the training examples (which configurations did you actually show the program?) and what kinds of physics interactions the program's design can represent (just nearby atoms, or long-distance effects too?).
