Talk 112 · University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign

Alberto Padovan: Model Reduction via Optimization of Projection Operators and Reduced-Order Dynamics

This whole seminar series chases one goal: getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of hand-written math. This talk takes on a specific puzzle: when you have data measurements from a system but can't see the equations running underneath, how do you build a fast, smaller version of the system that still predicts well? The answer is to let the computer learn not just which directions to pay attention to, but also how to project the data onto those directions—without needing the original equations.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

When engineers want to speed up predictions for a complicated system, they often start with a method called POD: a program that looks at measurement data and finds the strongest repeating patterns by looking for the directions where the data bounces around the most. But POD assumes you project by dropping perpendicular (like a shadow straight down). When a real system has huge sudden swings during its transient behavior (the wild motions before it settles down), perpendicular projection can twist and ruin those transient dynamics even though POD spotted the right lower-dimensional directions to work in. Many systems also hide their equations behind opaque solvers—software you can't see inside—so learning a better way to reduce them from measurement data alone would be useful, but the standard POD method doesn't provide it.

First-Principles Model

A reduced model of a system needs two pieces: first, a low-dimensional subspace—a direction or plane in the data to project onto; second, a direction to do the projecting—which decides how the full high-dimensional state maps down to the low-dimensional space. Perpendicular projection is simple and common (it points straight in), but it can miss transient behavior you care about. Tilted projection (directions that don't point straight in) can protect input-output relationships or the system's wild early motions, but usually you need the governing equations to compute it. This method solves that by learning both the tilted projection directions and simplified polynomial equations directly from measurement sequences. It does this by solving an optimization problem—a search over special curved spaces (called Grassmann and Stiefel manifolds) that automatically respect the geometric constraints that projection directions must follow.

Key Claims

  • You can build a reduced model from measurement data alone, even when you cannot access the equations running the full system.
  • When a system has large sudden motions early on, perpendicular projection breaks those motions even if it found the right low-dimensional space.
  • Tilted projection directions can capture the early wild motions more accurately than perpendicular ones.
  • Standard tilted projection needs direct access to the equations, which blocks you from using it when the solver is hidden.
  • This method learns both the projection directions and the simplified equations straight from measurement data.
  • Using polynomial equations and searching on curved spaces (Grassmann and Stiefel surfaces) provides the mathematical structure to solve this.

Important Terms

Oblique Projection

A way to map a high-dimensional state down to a lower-dimensional space by pointing in a direction that is not perpendicular—tilted or angled—to the target subspace.

Proper Orthogonal Decomposition

A method that looks at a collection of measurement snapshots and extracts the leading directions—the directions where the data bounces around the most, in order of strength.

Transient Growth

The temporary amplification of small disturbances during the early wild motions of a system before it settles down, which can happen even in a system that is stable over time.

Grassmann Manifold

A curved space where each point represents one possible choice of subspace with a fixed number of dimensions—like a surface of all k-dimensional planes floating in n-dimensional space.

Polynomial Reduced Dynamics

Simplified equations that describe how the reduced state changes over time, written as combinations of powers and products of the reduced variables.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture brings together data-driven model reduction, the geometry of how you project, early transient behavior, and optimization on curved spaces to solve a single puzzle.
  • It extends control-oriented model reduction by showing that the angle and direction of projection is a design choice, not a fixed consequence of finding the right subspace.
  • It addresses real-world cases where the solver is opaque and the governing equations are locked away, making direct mathematical methods impossible.

Practical Caution

Learning tilted projections from data can improve accuracy, but it brings two traps: the learned projection may have multiple equally-good solutions (non-uniqueness), and the search process can get stuck in local low points rather than finding the best global solution. Always check the learned projection on new measurement sequences that were not in the data used to train it.

Takeaway

Picking the right low-dimensional direction is only half the job in reducing a system. The way you project onto that direction matters just as much. Different tilts and angles of projection can be the difference between a reduced model that predicts reality and one that fails.
