Talk 38 · University of California, Santa Barbara

Igor Mezic: Koopman Operator Theory For Dynamical Systems, Control And Data Analytics

This whole series aims to get computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples. One major challenge: many real systems change in complicated, nonlinear ways—the next step doesn't follow a simple rule, so prediction and control are hard. This talk introduces Koopman operator theory, a trick that rewrites these complicated systems as if they were simple and linear, but in a specially chosen space of observations. The cost: that space can be infinite-dimensional and tricky to find, but when you find the right one, you turn a hard problem into an easier one.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Nonlinear dynamical systems (systems where the way things change from one moment to the next depends on where they are in a complicated, twisted way, not a straight-line way) are very hard to predict, break down into simpler pieces, or steer. Engineers and scientists have a tool: instead of watching how the state itself changes, watch how specific measurements or functions built from the state change. Koopman theory shows that when you pick the right measurements, these functions follow linear rules (straight-line rules)—but the space of all possible measurements is infinite-sized, so the real work is finding which measurements matter and understanding the deep structure of how they connect.

First-Principles Model

Start with an observable: just a function—a formula that takes the current state and outputs a number, like 'the distance from center' or 'the temperature at one point'. The Koopman operator takes that observable and shows what it will equal one time-step later, by composing it with the system's actual evolution. Special observables, called eigenfunctions, are ones that evolve in the simplest way: they just get multiplied by a fixed number (an exponential factor) at each time step. If you can find enough of these eigenfunctions, you can rewrite the whole nonlinear system as a linear system—a system where the next state is just a multiplication by a matrix. Methods like DMD (dynamic mode decomposition—a recipe that learns patterns from time-series data) give you a way to guess what those eigenfunctions and factors are, starting only from recorded data.

Key Claims

  • Koopman theory restates nonlinear systems by tracking how measurements of the state evolve, rather than how the state itself evolves.
  • The Koopman operator is linear (follows straight-line rules) even when the system it describes is wildly nonlinear.
  • Special measurements called eigenfunctions evolve in the simplest way—they get multiplied by the same number at each time step—so they give you coordinates where everything follows straight-line rules.
  • Koopman decompositions break things up by how they change over time (the spectrum in the time direction), not necessarily by how they spread through space.
  • DMD is one recipe for learning the Koopman operator's behavior from recorded data, picking out the most important pieces.
  • POD (proper orthogonal decomposition, a common method that finds patterns based on how spread-out things are in space) can miss important structure if that structure moves and rotates in a Koopman way that POD does not capture.

Worked Example

For a jet exiting a circular orifice (a noisy flow with cross-flow), the Koopman operator spectral analysis extracted clean eigenvalues represented as colorized structures overlaid on the messy flow field—bright blue and red dots showing coherent vortex dynamics embedded in apparent randomness. For the van der Pol oscillator, the method recovered the periodic limit cycle and its stability properties directly from data without solving ODEs. Power grids with 10 generators were analyzed to find coherent generator pairs (generators 2-3, 6-7-9, 10 formed distinct blocks) that must be stabilized as synchronized units.

One Level Deeper

The Koopman operator U acts on observables f as U f(x) = f(phi(x)); its eigenfunctions phi satisfy phi(phi^t(x)) = e^(lambda*t) phi(x). Eigenvalues form a lattice: for stable systems, the lattice lives in the left half-plane; for limit cycles, eigenvalues are products of integers times base frequencies. Spectral analysis uses the Hankel matrix constructed from impulse response sequences; singular value decomposition yields eigenvalues and modes. For nonlinear systems with unknown dynamics, spectral factorization of the Hankel matrix bypasses explicit matrix construction, enabling data-driven extraction of the entire spectrum.

Failure Boundary

Koopman methods collapse on systems with strong continuous spectrum (turbulence, noise-driven chaos). The separation between discrete eigenvalues and continuous spectrum—critical for clean mode extraction—vanishes when noise dominates or dimensions become very high. Finite-time data provides noisy spectral estimates prone to spurious peaks; spectral truncation requires choosing a cutoff (number of modes, eigenvalue threshold) and no principled method exists for chaotic systems. Bifurcation phenomena with zero-real-part eigenvalues and the interplay with continuous spectrum remain theoretically open and numerically fragile.

Important Terms

Koopman Operator

A tool (a mathematical map) that shows how any measurement built from the state will evolve forward in time by applying the system's actual flow and then reading the measurement.

Observable

Any function or measurement you can compute from the state—like 'the height', 'the speed', 'the distance from the center'—whose time-evolution you want to study.

Koopman Eigenfunction

A special observable that evolves in the simplest possible way: multiply by a fixed number at each time step. Finding these is the goal because they let you write the whole system in linear form.

DMD

Dynamic mode decomposition: a recipe that starts with recorded data over time (like a video of a system evolving) and extracts the Koopman operator's most important pieces by spotting repeating patterns.

Coherent Dynamics

Organized, repeating behavior (like a whirlpool or a wave) that stays stable over time and can be captured by Koopman modes—the building-block patterns of how the system moves.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk shows how equation-discovery, Green's functions (special solutions that show how systems respond), and reduced-order models (simple copies of big systems) all fit together through the structure of the Koopman operator.
  • It connects to control (steering a system) because linear representations of nonlinear dynamics make feedback and planning much simpler—you can use old, reliable linear control recipes.
  • It offers a different way to simplify systems than POD: instead of keeping the directions where energy spreads the most, you keep the directions where Koopman modes move in simple ways.

Practical Caution

Koopman methods only work as well as the measurements (observables) you choose. If you pick the wrong ones or miss some, your linear model looks clean but hides important behavior. A finite (limited-size) approximation can seem linear on paper yet still fail to capture the system's true motions if the space of measurements is incomplete.

Takeaway

Nonlinear systems can become linear if you measure the right things. Koopman theory is the systematic search for what to measure and how.
