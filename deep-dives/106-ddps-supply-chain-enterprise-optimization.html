Talk 106 · University of Connecticut

Burcu Beykal: A DDPS Engineering Approach for Supply Chain Management and Enterprise-Wide Optimization

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical systems behave by learning from data. This talk shifts focus: instead of predicting motion or heat flow, it asks how to help a company make good decisions across interconnected layers — planning what to make, scheduling when to make it, managing supplies — where each layer's choices affect what other layers can actually do.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Real factories and supply chains don't operate in isolation. A decision made by the planning team (make 500 units next month) only works if the scheduling team (who decides the day-to-day timetable) can actually fit it in, and if supplies are available when needed. If you optimize each layer separately, you often get a plan that looks good on paper but is impossible to execute because the layers conflict with each other. The problem: many interconnected decisions depend on each other, and finding a solution that works at every level is hard.

First-Principles Model

Think of supply-chain decisions as a chain of bosses making decisions. A top manager (the leader) chooses a high-level plan: how much to make, which customers to serve. Then the operating teams (the followers) look at that plan and decide the detailed schedule: which equipment runs when, which workers shift when. But often the followers discover the leader's plan is impossible—there isn't enough time or equipment. In math, this two-level game is called bi-level programming: the leader picks first, then the followers react, and the leader has to pick something the followers can actually execute. The real difficulty is that at the operating level, decisions come in two flavors: continuous (a machine can run for any amount of time) and discrete (a shipment either goes out or doesn't). Mixing these two types of decisions creates enormous math problems that regular solvers struggle with. A data-driven process systems approach uses three tools together: learning patterns from historical data about what the factory can do, thinking of the factory as a network of connected parts that constrain each other, and using smart algorithms to find a plan that works at all levels.

Key Claims

  • Real factories serve many products at once and must handle many different kinds of decisions, making them inherently complicated.
  • To keep a factory running well and making profit, different teams (planning, scheduling, supply) must coordinate and not work against each other.
  • The two-level leader-follower structure captures this: the planner must choose something the operator can execute.
  • The operating layer has many yes-or-no decisions (which pieces get made, which routes get shipped) mixed with smooth decisions (how long things run), and that mix makes the math very hard to solve.
  • Thinking of supply chains through the lens of process systems (how materials flow through interconnected stages) gives structure to otherwise overwhelming optimization problems.
  • Using data to learn realistic models of what plants can do, combined with smart optimization, makes enterprise decisions more reliable and practical.

Worked Example

Bilevel planning/scheduling integration: 7-day production planning with 2 products and dynamic demand profile (low, peak midweek, decline). State-task network with 3 feeds, 4 units, 8 tasks producing 2 products. Upper level: minimize inventory + production cost; lower level: minimize production cost subject to continuous-time schedule constraints. Demand peak at midday 3 results in production targets; scheduling must verify realization. Cost difference between Nomad (sample-based) and Argonaut (model-based) reflects ~5-30 unit material discrepancy depending on parametrization; both achieve feasible, optimal schedules. Extension to quadratic variable costs and cubic inventory costs (NLP-MIQP) shows cost Amplification at small carryover differences.

One Level Deeper

Bilevel program (upper minimizes over x, lower solves MINLP yielding y*(x)): upper objective subject to constraint 'y* is globally optimal for lower level given x'. Complementary slackness from KKT conditions introduces nonlinearity even in LP-LP case. Domino framework: design-of-experiments on upper-level variables, deterministic solution of lower level to global optimality (via CPLEX, BARON, Antigone), derivative-free optimization (Nomad via compass moves, Argonaut via Kriging surrogate + bound tightening) on upper level. Quadratic programming problem with L1 regularization (sparsity constraint) formulated via auxiliary variables (+, -) for absolute value decomposition. Inventory balance equations: ending_inventory = production + prior_day_inventory - demand.

Failure Boundary

Feasibility not guaranteed without global optimality of lower level (feasible solution must have lower level globally optimal). Branch-and-bound suffers with variable count; genetic algorithms lack constraint handling robustness. Mixed-integer formulations prevent KKT-based reduction to single level. Sample-based optimizers very sensitive to starting point for highly nonlinear lower-level problems; 121 seconds per sample for complex 3-charging-tank scheduling makes Nomad exhaustive. One infeasible run observed in 3-tank multi-product case; Argonaut unable to find solution within 24 hours. Stochastic demand adds dimension: must solve scheduling for each scenario branch, increasing computational burden; no guarantee worst-case solution generalizes to all scenarios.

Important Terms

Enterprise-Wide Optimization

Making the best decisions across all the layers of a company — planning what to make, scheduling when and how, managing supplies — at once, rather than having each team decide alone.

Supply Chain Management

Keeping track of and coordinating everything required to get materials and finished products where they need to be: buying supplies, shipping, storing, moving through the factory, and getting to customers.

Bi-Level Programming

A math structure where one decision-maker (the leader) chooses a plan first, then another (the follower) has to execute it and can only do certain things; the leader has to pick something the follower can actually carry out.

Mixed-Integer Optimization

A math problem involving two types of decisions mixed together: smooth choices (like how much of something to make, which can be any decimal number) and yes-or-no choices (like whether to run a machine, which must be 0 or 1).

Process Systems Engineering

The field that studies how materials, energy, and information flow through interconnected industrial processes, combining math models, optimization, and decision-making to run factories and chemical plants well.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk takes the data-driven approach the series started with and moves it from predicting physics (heat, flow) to solving practical business decisions.
  • It connects machine-learned models with constrained optimization: using what data teaches us about realistic operations as part of deciding what to do.
  • It also mirrors a big problem in planning systems: local choices (what my team should do) must align with global goals (what the whole company needs).

Practical Caution

A smart computer program that recommends decisions can steer you wrong if the model of "what's actually possible" oversimplifies the real operating layer. If the detailed constraints are left out or weakened too much, the planner might recommend something that scheduling or operations cannot execute. Before acting on any recommendation, the operations team must verify it actually works in practice.

Takeaway

Supply-chain and factory decisions form a nested stack: every choice only succeeds if it remains doable at the detailed operating level. Decisions are only truly good when they survive the whole chain from planning all the way down to execution.
