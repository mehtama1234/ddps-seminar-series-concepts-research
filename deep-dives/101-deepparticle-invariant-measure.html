Talk 101 · University of Hong Kong

Z. Zhang: DeepParticle: Learning Invariant Measure by a Deep Neural Network Minimizing Wasserstein Distance

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical systems behave by learning from examples instead of just using hand-written math rules. This talk shows a new way to do that for systems with lots of tiny interacting pieces (like particles moving in fluid or cells crowding together) that are too expensive to simulate directly at extreme conditions. The idea: train a learning program on easier, cheaper conditions where you can actually run the simulation, and have it predict what happens at the harder, more expensive conditions without running the full simulation.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

When you have a rule (called a PDE, a recipe that says how something changes from place to place or moment to moment, like how heat spreads through metal) with many dimensions and tricky features like sharp boundaries or places where something piles up, mesh-based math is slow and inaccurate. You could simulate many particles directly, watching how they push and pull each other — that works well for those tricky features but takes forever if you want to know what happens over long times. The real wall: you want to know how the system behaves when conditions are extreme and expensive (like very high flow speed), but you can't afford to actually run the full particle simulation there.

First-Principles Model

In one specific case — fast-moving fronts spreading through incompressible flows (imagine dye spreading through water that's not getting compressed) — the math tells us that the front speed comes down to finding a single number (the largest eigenvalue: a special number that comes from an operator, which is like a machine that takes in a function and cranks out another function) that controls how disturbances grow and spread. There's a formula called the Feynman-Kac formula that builds a bridge: it says that certain PDE quantities show up as averages along the paths of random particles — so instead of solving equations, you can sprinkle particles, let them wander randomly, and collect statistics. DeepParticle learns a whole family of these long-time distributions (the invariant measures: the probability pattern that the particle system settles into after running for infinite time) by having a neural network (a program with millions of tiny dials it tunes by trial and error) take in a single input — the Peclet number, which is how fast particles move compared to how much they jiggle randomly — and output a learned guess at that long-time distribution. The program trains on moderate Peclet values where simulation is cheap, using a Wasserstein loss (a score for how much effort it takes to physically move the probability mass from what the program guessed to what the true simulation found) to measure how wrong the guess is. Once trained, it can guess the distribution at much higher Peclet values where running the simulation directly would be prohibitively expensive.

Key Claims

  • DeepParticle joins three ideas: learning programs that tune millions of dials by trial and error, optimal transport (a way to measure and move probability distributions), and particle algorithms that watch many objects push and pull each other.
  • The method targets a specific case: fast-moving fronts spreading through water and other flows that don't get compressed.
  • The speed of a spreading front comes down to finding one special number from an operator that controls how fast changes grow.
  • The Feynman-Kac formula gives a recipe: instead of solving a PDE, let random particles wander and collect statistics about them.
  • What the program learns is a whole family of long-time probability patterns, each one matched to a different speed-to-jiggle ratio.
  • The method also works for learning how stochastic particle systems evolve, like cells clumping together by chemical attraction (Keller-Segel chemotaxis).

Important Terms

DeepParticle

A method that trains a learning program using a handful of cheap simulations to predict the long-time probability distributions of expensive particle systems at extreme conditions.

Invariant Measure

A probability distribution (a recipe for how likely each outcome is) that stays the same as a dynamical or stochastic process evolves over long times — the system's settled pattern.

Feynman-Kac Formula

A mathematical rule that says: instead of solving a PDE directly, you can sprinkle particles, let them wander randomly following the rules, and take averages over those random paths to get the same answer.

Interacting Particle Method

A simulation where you track many particles whose motions affect each other directly, and you reweight or resample them to represent a probability distribution.

Wasserstein Distance

A way to measure how different two probability distributions are by asking: if particles were objects, how much total distance do they all have to move to turn one distribution into the other?

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture joins stochastic dynamics (rules that involve randomness), PDE analysis (understanding how changes propagate), optimal transport (measuring and reshaping probability), and learning programs.
  • Unlike other talks that teach learning programs to predict solutions of PDEs given new inputs, this one aims at learning the long-time property that controls the answer — a different target and a tighter focus.
  • It also touches on rare-event simulation and multiscale methods because the core idea is to learn efficiently about conditions that would be impractically expensive to compute head-on.

Practical Caution

Learning long-time distributions at moderate costs does not guarantee you get the right answer when you push to extreme costs and try to predict something far from where you trained. If you extrapolate to much higher Peclet numbers, you must double-check the answers against the physics or against running bigger simulations to make sure the program didn't just get lucky.

Takeaway

Some of the hardest physics prediction problems become solvable if you focus on learning the single long-time probability pattern that actually controls what you care about — instead of learning the entire motion at every instant.
