Talk 2 · Courant Institute / NYU

Donsub Rim: Distilling Nonlinear Shock Waves

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples. One common trick is to use a learning program (a huge network of dials that tunes itself by trial and error until it predicts correctly) to build a small, fast version of a big simulator. This talk focuses on why that fails for one specific type of problem: when something moves or a shock wave travels across the space — linear tricks don't work because motion makes the snapshots messy and high-dimensional, but the actual motion itself is simple. This lecture shows how to find the right low-dimensional description by tracking how things move through space instead of memorizing field values.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Imagine a camera recording a wave or pulse moving across a domain. Each photo looks different from the last, so if you collect many photos and try to fit them into a low-dimensional space (like finding 5 or 10 shared patterns to explain all photos), you need many patterns — the ranking is high. But the underlying motion is simple: the same shape just slid left or right. The problem is that standard tricks assume all solutions cluster around smooth, simple directions. When things move or translate, the snapshots are nearly perpendicular to each other in the math space, so the counting of independent directions explodes even though the physical motion is straightforward.

First-Principles Model

For moving waves and shocks, the real low-dimensional object is not the field values in a photo but the map that describes where information flows. Instead of saying 'the temperature here is 25 degrees in photo 1, 24 in photo 2,' think of it as 'the hot spot is moving forward by one grid cell per time step.' The transport map (how fast and in what direction the wave travels) or the deformation map (how the whole shape stretches and shifts) may be simple and low-dimensional, while the snapshots are complicated. Nonlinear model reduction tries to directly capture that moving geometry — the flow paths and transformations — rather than trying to squash moving snapshots into a fixed basis.

Key Claims

  • When things move or interfaces evolve, traditional small models struggle because motion spreads the snapshots across many independent directions, even though the motion itself is simple.
  • A mathematical measure called Kolmogorov n-width shows why a linear basis can need many modes even for basic moving discontinuities: it quantifies how well the solution set fits into an n-dimensional linear subspace.
  • The set of all solution snapshots can be high-dimensional and complicated, while the map describing how things transport or deform can be low-dimensional.
  • Small nonlinear models can be built by combining a basis for shape with a learned or constructed map for motion.
  • Shock formation adds extra difficulty because the map that tracks motion can develop breaks or lose smoothness at the shock.
  • Neural networks can be understood as a way to compress a large solution representation into a smaller nonlinear model.

Important Terms

Transport-Dominated Problem

A physics rule (PDE) where motion, waves carried by flow, shocks, or sharp interfaces control what happens.

Kolmogorov n-width

A measurement of how well a set of solutions can fit into an n-dimensional linear space — high n-width means you need many independent directions.

Characteristic Map

A description of how points and features move through space under the motion or flow.

Nonlinear Reduced Model

A small model that uses nonlinear coordinates or transformation maps to capture solutions more efficiently than a linear basis alone.

Reduced Deep Network

A compact neural network designed to approximate a family of solutions more cheaply than a large generic network, by learning the essential structure of that solution family.

Connections Across The Series

  • This connects directly to the concept of small models that capture solution structure, and introduces the first major way that linear small models fail.
  • It prepares later talks on curved solution sets, registration-based reduction (aligning solutions so they line up), and simulation in learned spaces.
  • It also explains why data-driven simulation cannot ignore how things are arranged: the same physical motion can look expensive and high-dimensional in one choice of coordinates but cheap and simple in another.

Practical Caution

If your small learned model fails when predicting how a shock or wave moves, don't automatically blame lack of data or say the network needs to be bigger. The real issue might be the coordinate system. The low-dimensional structure may live in how things transport through space, not in linear combinations of snapshots. Choosing the right description of the solution — finding the right coordinates — often matters more than collecting more examples.

Takeaway

The lecture's core message is: separate what the physics does (the actual low-dimensional behavior) from the rank of the snapshots (how many independent directions you need to write them down). A moving wave can be simple when described in terms of its transport and deformation, even if collecting raw photos makes it look high-dimensional and tangled.
