Talk 7 · University of Washington

Steven Brunton: Interpretable And Generalizable Machine Learning For Fluid Mechanics

This whole series asks: can computers learn to predict how physical things behave by studying examples, instead of just relying on hand-written math? This talk focuses on one specific goal: when teaching a computer to predict how fluids move and change, prefer models that you can actually read and understand. A model that is easy to read usually works on new situations the computer has never seen before, while a hidden locked-box model often fails when conditions shift—new speeds, different shapes, changed temperature.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Measuring fluids produces enormous amounts of numbers, and running exact simulations takes forever. But just teaching a computer to match numbers it has already seen is not good enough. A model can repeat past observations while completely breaking down when the conditions change—when you use a new control, change the shape, or vary a parameter—if it has only memorized patterns instead of truly learning how fluids actually work. The real structure hiding inside fluid motion is often surprisingly simple: just a handful of important directions and perhaps only a few key forces matter, but most models never find that simplicity.

First-Principles Model

Real fluid systems carry tons of measurement data but almost always boil down to just a few essential directions; the hidden rules that govern them also usually rest on only a few key pieces instead of using every possible term. Interpretable machine learning hunts for those few crucial directions, determines which forces truly matter, finds hidden terms that are missing from our approximate rules, or spots where our simple models need small fixes. The alternative is building a secret system that just converts inputs to outputs without you ever knowing why it works.

Key Claims

  • Applying machine learning to engineering is essentially optimization: find the dials and numbers that make your predictions as close to the real measurements as possible.
  • For a model to work on new situations, it must capture the true hidden simplicity, not just memorize old training examples.
  • Simple is better: build models with as few terms as required, but no fewer.
  • One specific method called SINDy (Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics) watches how a system changes and automatically picks out which forces and interactions are actually doing the work.
  • The way you choose to describe and measure a fluid—your coordinate system—can either hide the simple structure or expose it; picking the right way of measuring turns a huge complicated problem into something small and clear.
  • Machine learning can also focus on finding mismatch: what is the difference between our approximate rules and what actually happens in reality.

Worked Example

SINDy discovered the Lorenz 63 model from noisy measurement data. Using only 2-3 trajectories collected in a couple of hours on a laptop, the algorithm recovered the three core equations (x dot, y dot, z dot) with correct coefficients without ever seeing the equations. For the cylinder wake system, researchers measured lift and drag and used sparse identification to discover a spring-mass-damper model—achieving both the highest accuracy (nearly perfect quantitative match) and simplest description ever written for this canonical problem.

One Level Deeper

SINDy builds a library of candidate terms (polynomials, derivatives, products) organized as a matrix Theta. Given time-derivative data X_dot, it solves a sparse regression: find the minimum number of columns of Theta such that Theta @ c = X_dot. The loss enforces skew-symmetric structure for energy conservation; when combined with cubic and higher-order closure terms, it captures how truncated low-energy modes couple back to retained modes. The constrained sparse selection uses iterative least squares with hard zeros: minimize ||X_dot - Theta @ c||^2 subject to ||c||_0 being small.

Failure Boundary

SINDy struggles with discontinuities and shock waves—piecewise constant data requires many more terms or fails entirely. For turbulent flows without coherent structure, it cannot extract the small number of dominant terms needed; full isotropic box turbulence is beyond reach. Systems with weak coupling or slow timescale separation lead to ambiguous term selection and require careful hyperparameter tuning. Measurement noise introduces false terms that must be pruned, and extrapolation beyond the training regime (new parameter ranges) is unreliable because the discovered equations encode only the dynamics observed.

Important Terms

Parsimony

The principle that a good explanation should be as simple as possible to describe what you see, without unnecessary details.

SINDy

A method that takes measurements of a system and automatically discovers which forces and interactions matter. You give it a long list of candidate pieces (linear terms, multiplication terms, etc.), and it systematically removes the ones that contribute almost nothing, leaving only the few that actually drive the behavior.

Sparse Regression

A type of fitting that pushes most candidate contributions toward zero, keeping only the few that truly matter for prediction.

Coordinate Discovery

Finding new ways to measure and describe a system so that complicated high-dimensional motion becomes simple and clear.

Discrepancy Modeling

Learning the gap between an approximate rule (like a simplified physical equation) and what you actually observe in the real world; the correction term that fills the hole in your theory.

Connections Across The Series

  • This is the core thread linking reduced-order models (stripped-down simpler versions that keep the essential behavior), operator inference (learning how operations transform the system), and scientific machine learning: the shared idea is to learn true structure rather than just memorizing outputs.
  • It directly connects to talks about surrogates and closure because closure terms are often exactly discrepancy models for physics happening below the scale you can measure.
  • It also sets a clear test for interpretability: can you actually inspect and understand what you learned—as explicit equations, as a list of important directions, as a balance of forces, or as correction terms?

Practical Caution

A computer model that predicts the next snapshot of a fluid flow may look impressive on graphs but still be scientifically hollow if it never reveals which variables are actually driving the flow, how different forces combine and interact, or what physics pieces we left out. To truly test if a model works in new regimes, you must try situations it has never encountered—not just check whether it predicts numbers in between the training examples.

Takeaway

The deep lesson from this talk is that when a model is easy to read and inspect, that is not extra decoration—it is often the exact thing that makes it work on new situations. In fluids especially, once you can see the structure and write it down clearly, the model begins to transfer to problems it has never trained on.
