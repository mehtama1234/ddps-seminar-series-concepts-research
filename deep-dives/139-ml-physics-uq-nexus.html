Talk 139 · Johns Hopkins University

Michael Shields: The Nexus of Machine Learning, Physics-Based Modeling, and Uncertainty Quantification

This whole series is about teaching computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math rules. One popular approach mixes learning programs with the laws of physics. This talk focuses on something those approaches often skip: measuring and reporting the uncertainty in those predictions so other scientists know when to trust them and when not to.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Physics-based models always have uncertainty baked in: the numbers we start with are uncertain, the constants we plug in are approximate, and our mathematical description of the world is incomplete. When we add machine learning on top, we pile on more uncertainty from the data we trained on, the trial-and-error choices we made while tuning, and the chance that the learned model breaks on new situations. For predictions that matter—in medicine, engineering, climate—you need both the speed and power of learning programs and a honest report of how much uncertainty sits in your answer.

First-Principles Model

Uncertainty comes in two flavors. Epistemic uncertainty is ignorance we could cure: get more data, run better experiments, gather more information and that uncertainty shrinks. Aleatory uncertainty is randomness woven into nature itself: flip a coin enough times and you still can't predict one flip perfectly. In scientific machine learning, there are two separate jobs. First: measure how sure a learning program can be about its own predictions—that's uncertainty quantification for machine learning. Second: use learning programs to speed up or improve the hard work of measuring uncertainty—that's machine learning for uncertainty quantification. It includes building simpler learned models that run in milliseconds instead of hours, finding which inputs have the most impact on the answer, and solving inverse problems (working backward: you have measurements, what hidden causes produced them?). The talk shows both roles and why they matter for models other scientists will actually use.

Key Claims

  • Uncertainty measurement and learning programs are now essential tools in physics-based models run on computers.
  • Physics-based models harbor uncertainties at every stage: inputs, constants, the model structure itself.
  • Layering machine learning onto a physics model introduces new uncertainties on top of the physics ones.
  • Measuring and accounting for uncertainty has become as important as making the prediction in the first place.
  • The talk distinguishes two separate tasks: measuring uncertainty in machine-learning models, and using machine learning to measure uncertainty in other systems.
  • Scientific models used across research communities must include methods to measure and communicate uncertainty.

Important Terms

Uncertainty Quantification

The practice of measuring and calculating how much we don't know in model inputs, the numbers we plug in, how well our model describes the real world, and what the model predicts.

Epistemic Uncertainty

Uncertainty that comes from not knowing something you could find out with more measurements, more data, or better experiments; gathering information shrinks this kind.

Aleatory Uncertainty

Uncertainty from randomness that exists in nature itself; no amount of new data will shrink it because randomness is real.

UQ for ML

Measuring how confident a machine-learning model should be in its own predictions—knowing where the model is likely to be right or wrong.

ML for UQ

Using machine-learning programs to make uncertainty measurement faster or better: building learned models that substitute for slow calculations, spotting which inputs matter most, or solving inverse problems.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk builds on ideas about trustworthy AI, Bayesian methods for tuning neural networks, multi-fidelity learning (mixing simulations at different levels of detail), and scientific foundation models.
  • It strengthens every other DDPS lecture by making uncertainty measurement a first-class output alongside predictions.
  • It also connects to how we judge whether a model is good: reliability means knowing when the model might be wrong and by approximately how much.

Practical Caution

Uncertainty measurements can look trustworthy but be wrong. Always check them against cases where the true answer is known, test them on data the model never saw during training, and explore how sensitive they are to assumptions baked into how you set up the model.

Takeaway

Scientific machine learning is incomplete without measuring how uncertain its predictions are and explaining why the uncertainty exists.
