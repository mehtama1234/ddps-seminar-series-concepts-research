Talk 58 · RocketML

Santi Adavani: Industrial Grade Scientific Machine Learning

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples, instead of just hand-written math. One approach is to teach a neural network (a program with millions of dials the computer tunes by trial and error until its answers look right) using both data and the laws of physics. This talk is about what stops that approach from working in real factories and power plants: not whether the idea is good in theory, but whether it can handle real-world messy problems, scale up to huge data and slow computers, stay running without breaking, and actually be trusted more than the old trusted solvers we've used for decades.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Researchers have published many papers and released free software tools for scientific machine learning, but when engineers in real factories and power plants try to use them, they hit hard walls: nobody's problem statement is clear, the new method has to talk to the old software that's already running, it needs to work with massive grids of numbers that the computer can barely hold in memory, it has to run inside a real system 24/7 without crashing, and nobody knows when a learning program beats the old trusted step-by-step method (one that computes what happens next given what we know now) or the method that guesses backward to find the hidden starting state we can't measure.

First-Principles Model

A real production solver is not just clever code; it's a piece of software glued into workflows, hardware, data coming in from sensors, what the domain expert says is allowed or not allowed, and how to check that the answer is actually right. Scientific machine learning must be evaluated on three things that matter in a factory: Is the problem actually described clearly enough that two engineers agree? Does the method still work when you scale up to a million or a billion numbers? Can it be installed, run every day, watched for problems, and fixed when something breaks?

Key Claims

  • Scientific machine learning takes modern learning programs and applies them to systems we describe with rules that say how things change through space and time.
  • Lots of researchers and engineers are excited about it, but actually getting it working in real factories and power plants hits huge roadblocks.
  • People running real systems need to know how to move from the old trusted solvers to a new learning-based approach, step by step, without breaking anything.
  • The three biggest problems in real use are: unclear problem descriptions, the method refusing to scale up, and trouble installing and keeping it running.
  • Free software and published papers alone do not make something that a specific industry can actually use every day.
  • The research community can help most by thinking about what actually needs to happen when you move a new idea from a lab into a factory: not just inventing something new, but making it work inside real constraints.

Worked Example

A steady-state heat transfer problem on a 512×512×512 cube (millions of grid points) takes ~400 seconds with traditional finite element solvers; neural operator inference runs in ~20 seconds. A 3D elliptic diffusivity problem (Poisson-like) maps input diffusivity field μ to solution u. Training on 3D U-Net with 256³ resolution takes 2000 minutes (34 hours) on 8 NVIDIA GPUs using baseline approach. Multi-grid acceleration (starting coarse 32³, then 64³, 128³, 256³ with transfer learning) reduces total training to ~150 minutes—13x faster. Burgers equation with initial conditions as input, full field as output, also benefits from multi-grid approach with documented 5-10x speedups.

Illustrative: fluid flow past obstacle from 400s (traditional) to 10s (ML inference). Neural material model in finite element code (ABACUS): Johnson-Cook constitutive model costs 50 seconds per Taylor impact simulation; learned model costs 450 seconds (~9x overhead), but amortizes offline cost (10⁶ data generation + 10⁴ training steps on GPU) vs. full concurrent multiscale (10⁸ CPU units).

One Level Deeper

Ambiguity challenge: traditional PDE solvers have clear i/o contracts (coefficients→solution), but neural approaches mix point-wise vs. full-field architectures, data-driven (comparing to FEM solutions) vs. physics-informed (PDE residual), different basis representations (points, voxels, graphs). Multi-grid training: coarse network trained first on low-resolution data, then weight-initialized into finer network via transfer learning, continuing hierarchically. This borrows from numerical multigrid but applies to neural network training: train Level-0 (coarse), stop early, transfer to Level-1, train Level-1 briefly, transfer to Level-2, etc. Reduces memory overhead and computational cost by exploiting smoothness properties learned at coarser scales.

Deployability issues: models must integrate with legacy simulation software (ABACUS, ANSYS, etc.). ML introduces reproducibility challenges (does new input lie in training distribution?), monitoring drift, retraining triggers—unlike traditional solvers with proven error analysis. Community needs: ontology (define problem classes: ODE/PDE, steady/transient, 2D/3D), benchmarks (reference geometries, boundary/initial conditions at industrial scale), error bars relative to reference solvers.

Failure Boundary

GPU memory limits: 128³ exceeds Tesla memory, 256³ requires multi-GPU setup. Problem complexity: separable solutions (one PCA mode fits all) work well; highly nonlinear bifurcating systems fail—transfer learned weights may not generalize. Out-of-distribution inputs: model trained on diffusivity μ ∈ [0.1, 10] fails if μ = 50. Scalability ceiling: purely data-parallel training (throwing more GPUs at 512³ problem) is too slow; algorithmic innovation needed. Architecture transfer tricky: U-Net multi-grid to mixed MLP+CNN architectures requires manual weight reshaping, not automatic. Deployment reproducibility: given trained model, can you guarantee output for new input? ML lacks rigorous error bounds like FEM. Reproducibility across frameworks: PyTorch to C++ integration only trivial for inference, not training. Legacy ecosystem coupling: must work alongside existing ANSYS/ABACUS pipelines, not replace—integration cost high.

Important Terms

Industrial Grade Scientific Machine Learning

A learning program designed to work reliably in a factory or power plant: it has to scale to real problem sizes, plug into existing systems without breaking them, run all day without crashing, and let you check that the answers are actually trustworthy.

Forward Solver

A program that predicts what happens next if you know how things start, what the shape is, and what the material is made of — like predicting how hot a metal bar gets if you know the starting temperature.

Inverse Solver

A program that works backward: you tell it what you saw happen, and it figures out the hidden facts that caused it — like guessing the starting temperature by seeing how fast the bar cooled.

Deployability

The ability to take a working program, put it into a real factory system, have it run 24/7 without crashing, watch it for problems, and fix it when something breaks — all without shutting everything else down.

Scalability

Whether a program still works when the problem gets enormous: millions or billions of numbers to track, huge piles of data coming from sensors, or compute power so limited that you have to wait hours for an answer.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture builds on earlier talks about letting machines learn to copy what real systems do: adding the constraint that the learned program also has to earn the trust of engineers who have decades of experience with older methods.
  • It frames why replacing old solvers is so much harder than just proving a new method works on a computer: people will not switch until the new thing fits into their workflow, costs less time and money, and they can still check that it's right.
  • It sharpens the lens for understanding why approaches like learning programs trained on physics (neural networks that know the laws), neural operators (programs shaped like PDEs), and solvers where the computer can nudge its own guesses all matter, but only if they can survive contact with a real factory.

Practical Caution

Do not treat a research demo as a tool ready for real use. People who run factories need exact definitions of what the problem is, proof that it works at large scale, a concrete plan to install it, and ways to double-check that the answers are right before they stop trusting the old solvers they know and depend on.

Takeaway

Industrial scientific machine learning gets judged by one thing: does it fit into what we already have? A new method only matters when you can write it down clearly, make it handle the real size of the problem, install it without breaking everything else, and prove you can trust it.
