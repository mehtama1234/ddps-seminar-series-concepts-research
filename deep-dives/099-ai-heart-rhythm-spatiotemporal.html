Talk 99 · University of California, San Francisco

Jan Christoph: Predicting Heart Rhythm Disorders from Spatio-Temporal Imaging Data Using AI

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical systems behave by learning from data instead of only hand-written rules. This talk focuses on a hidden-state problem: electrical waves moving through heart tissue cause the heart to squeeze and change shape, and those shape changes show up in images. The question is whether a learning program can run this backward—watch the motion video and predict what the hidden electrical waves were doing.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Heart rhythm disorders happen when electrical waves inside cardiac tissue go wrong and become chaotic or blocked. Current medical imaging (ultrasound, CT, MRI) captures images of how the heart tissue moves and deforms, but cannot directly see the electrical activity deep inside the heart wall. Doctors need a way to infer those dangerous hidden electrical patterns from the motion pictures they can easily record.

First-Principles Model

Heart cells are electrically active (each cell can fire and rest like a tiny switch). When cells fire in sequence, the electrical pulse spreads from cell to cell through diffusion—local reactions triggering neighbors—creating waves that sweep through tissue (the same kind of pattern spread you see when ink drops into water and the color spreads out). Those electrical waves make heart cells contract, and contraction causes the tissue to deform and move. Here is the main idea: if electrical activity leaves a fingerprint in the mechanical motion, then a learning program can be trained to read backward from motion to electrical state. In the lab, scientists remove a heart and keep it alive in controlled fluid outside the body (called an ex vivo heart). In this setup they can measure both the electrical activity (using fluorescent dyes that glow when cells fire) and the tissue motion (using high-speed video cameras) at the same time. This paired data—electrical state plus visible motion captured together—becomes the training examples a learning program needs.

Key Claims

  • Heart rhythm disorders are abnormal, chaotic electrical waves spreading through tissue.
  • Electrical activity and mechanical motion in the heart are tightly connected: electrical waves cause muscle contraction, which creates visible deformation.
  • A learning program can be trained to predict hidden electrical patterns from motion videos alone.
  • Different imaging methods—fluorescence (glowing dyes), ultrasound (sound waves)—can each reveal motion from different angles.
  • Ex vivo hearts (removed and kept functioning outside the body) let scientists collect training data where they measure electrical and mechanical signals at the same time.
  • The goal is to predict arrhythmias and understand how rhythm disorders form.

Worked Example

The lab images isolated rabbit and pig hearts kept alive by retrograde (Tyrode-solution) perfusion, using 12 cameras that capture 500 frames/second and reconstruct the whole ventricular surface, cross-registered with a 2D-matrix ultrasound probe giving 25-100 volumes/second. Fluorescent voltage dye makes action-potential waves visible; the raw intensity change per beat is tiny, so a routine step amplifies the signal over time to expose the wavefront, and blebbistatin is left out so mechanics and electrics are recorded at the same time (rare in the field, where ~99% of prior work froze contraction).

For tracking rotor cores (phase singularities), a U-net/autoencoder reads a short voltage sequence and predicts phase maps and singularity points from as few as 5 snapshots and less than 10% of the spatial information, where the classical Hilbert-transform-plus-circular-integral method needs about 1 second of clean data and breaks on sparse, noisy catheter measurements. Trained purely on 2D simulation data and applied to real 3D-surface pig/rabbit recordings, it still recovers rotor locations with decent angular accuracy -- transfer from simulation to experiment that the authors highlight as surprising given the training was only 2D.

The headline task is computing electrical waves from mechanical motion. In idealized biventricular electromechanics, a plane wave, two rotors, and five-plus rotors each stamp a distinct contraction pattern, so the inverse should be feasible. An autoencoder/U-net trained on 20,000 deformation-to-excitation pairs reconstructs even fully turbulent 3D wave patterns nearly indistinguishably (vortex filaments of prediction and truth overlap), versus only ~80% accuracy for their earlier physics-based data-assimilation approach. Scaling to patients, they generate 10,000-50,000 SPH-based electromechanical simulations (each episode under 10 minutes) over 1,000 geometries and 4 electrical models, and a sparse residual U-net (Minkowski engine on the point cloud) predicts activation maps from 5 mechanical frames, still working when scar tissue is present.

One Level Deeper

Cardiac excitation is modeled as a reaction-diffusion system: du/dt = D * laplacian(u) + f(u, v), where the diffusive term spreads excitation to neighbors and the local reaction terms f, g (e.g. FitzHugh-Nagumo or Fenton-Karma) give an excitable cell a rest state, a threshold, an action-potential excursion through phase space, and a refractory tail that prevents immediate re-excitation -- the fire-front analogy where burned fuel keeps the wave from turning back. Rotors are spiral/scroll waves rotating around a phase singularity (in 3D, a filament); fibrillation is these filaments interacting and breaking up into spatiotemporal chaos.

The classical rotor detector embeds the scalar voltage time-series via a Hilbert transform into a complex phase, then a circular line integral of the phase gradient around a point returns a topological charge (a 2-pi discontinuity marks a singularity). It is fragile, so the deep-learning version instead learns the map from a short image sequence directly to phase/singularity fields through an encoder-decoder, robust to sparsity and noise.

The electromechanical inversion learns f: (sequence of mechanical deformation / displacement fields) -> (electrical excitation field). The physical justification is direct experimental evidence: the boundary between dilating and contracting strain follows the action-potential wavefront with a small, characteristic electromechanical delay and in the same direction, so the mechanics carries a shifted imprint of the electrics. The physics-based precursor coupled two excitable systems (real system 1 and a virtual replica system 2) with a term proportional to the state difference, driving system 2 by mechanical-state comparison until it synchronized (~80% match); the learned encoder-decoder replaced this and jumped to near-perfect reconstruction.

Failure Boundary

Reconstruction of the 3D wave from surface data is fundamentally depth-limited: superficial layers come back sharp, but accuracy falls with depth and the interior fuzzes out, because the surface simply does not contain enough information about deep chaotic dynamics -- diffusion models help only up to a point and only if fed more than one rotation period of data. So extrapolating turbulent 3D activity from surface observation alone is flagged as maybe not the right approach.

The larger boundary is simulation-to-patient transfer: a network trained only on simulation data failed on real patient data, which is why they are now generating experimental training data and fine-tuning. Patient-specific anatomy (heart shape, scar location, fiber architecture) is a known sensitivity they have not fully resolved, though scar-containing cases still worked in simulation. Phase singularities themselves are called unreliable to track in real tissue -- they wobble, vanish, and reappear, and linear-core rotors and scar-anchored reentry can lack a clean point singularity -- so the speaker cautions against over-relying on the phase-singularity concept for describing fibrillation.

Important Terms

Electrophysiology

The study of how electricity works in living tissue—in this case, how heart cells fire and how those electrical pulses spread.

Reaction-Diffusion System

A process where local reactions trigger neighbors to react, and that spread continues outward, creating traveling waves. Think of lighting a candle—the flame spreads because local heat ignites the next bit of wax.

Spatiotemporal Imaging

Video or pictures that show both where things are (spatial—in 3D space) and how they change moment by moment (temporal—over time).

Ultrasound Imaging

A medical technique that bounces sound waves through tissue and listens to the echoes to create pictures of motion and structure inside the body.

Ex Vivo Training

Collecting examples by working with an organ or tissue outside a living body in a controlled lab setup—here, keeping a heart alive in fluid so you can measure its signals precisely.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk combines three ideas from this seminar: using physics knowledge to guide learning programs, solving inverse problems (running a process backward from observed result to hidden cause), and using cameras and sensors to infer states inside tissues.
  • It shows the broader DDPS theme: when two processes are linked, watch the visible one to infer what the hidden one is doing.
  • It takes nonlinear waves and spreading patterns (reaction-diffusion dynamics) from abstract theory and applies them to a real clinical problem that could save lives.

Practical Caution

A model trained on ex vivo hearts may not work the same way on living patients in the clinic. Real hearts are different: tissue conditions vary from person to person, medical equipment and imaging quality differ, temperature and chemistry fluctuate, drugs and pacemakers interfere. These differences must be tested and validated carefully before using the model on patients.

Takeaway

When two physical processes are linked (like electrical waves creating mechanical motion), you can sometimes infer the hidden process from watching the visible one—if you have enough paired examples to learn the connection.
