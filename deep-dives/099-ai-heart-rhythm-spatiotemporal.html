Talk 99 · University of California, San Francisco

Jan Christoph: Predicting Heart Rhythm Disorders from Spatio-Temporal Imaging Data Using AI

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical systems behave by learning from data instead of only hand-written rules. This talk focuses on a hidden-state problem: electrical waves moving through heart tissue cause the heart to squeeze and change shape, and those shape changes show up in images. The question is whether a learning program can run this backward—watch the motion video and predict what the hidden electrical waves were doing.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Heart rhythm disorders happen when electrical waves inside cardiac tissue go wrong and become chaotic or blocked. Current medical imaging (ultrasound, CT, MRI) captures images of how the heart tissue moves and deforms, but cannot directly see the electrical activity deep inside the heart wall. Doctors need a way to infer those dangerous hidden electrical patterns from the motion pictures they can easily record.

First-Principles Model

Heart cells are electrically active (each cell can fire and rest like a tiny switch). When cells fire in sequence, the electrical pulse spreads from cell to cell through diffusion—local reactions triggering neighbors—creating waves that sweep through tissue (the same kind of pattern spread you see when ink drops into water and the color spreads out). Those electrical waves make heart cells contract, and contraction causes the tissue to deform and move. Here is the main idea: if electrical activity leaves a fingerprint in the mechanical motion, then a learning program can be trained to read backward from motion to electrical state. In the lab, scientists remove a heart and keep it alive in controlled fluid outside the body (called an ex vivo heart). In this setup they can measure both the electrical activity (using fluorescent dyes that glow when cells fire) and the tissue motion (using high-speed video cameras) at the same time. This paired data—electrical state plus visible motion captured together—becomes the training examples a learning program needs.

Key Claims

  • Heart rhythm disorders are abnormal, chaotic electrical waves spreading through tissue.
  • Electrical activity and mechanical motion in the heart are tightly connected: electrical waves cause muscle contraction, which creates visible deformation.
  • A learning program can be trained to predict hidden electrical patterns from motion videos alone.
  • Different imaging methods—fluorescence (glowing dyes), ultrasound (sound waves)—can each reveal motion from different angles.
  • Ex vivo hearts (removed and kept functioning outside the body) let scientists collect training data where they measure electrical and mechanical signals at the same time.
  • The goal is to predict arrhythmias and understand how rhythm disorders form.

Important Terms

Electrophysiology

The study of how electricity works in living tissue—in this case, how heart cells fire and how those electrical pulses spread.

Reaction-Diffusion System

A process where local reactions trigger neighbors to react, and that spread continues outward, creating traveling waves. Think of lighting a candle—the flame spreads because local heat ignites the next bit of wax.

Spatiotemporal Imaging

Video or pictures that show both where things are (spatial—in 3D space) and how they change moment by moment (temporal—over time).

Ultrasound Imaging

A medical technique that bounces sound waves through tissue and listens to the echoes to create pictures of motion and structure inside the body.

Ex Vivo Training

Collecting examples by working with an organ or tissue outside a living body in a controlled lab setup—here, keeping a heart alive in fluid so you can measure its signals precisely.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk combines three ideas from this seminar: using physics knowledge to guide learning programs, solving inverse problems (running a process backward from observed result to hidden cause), and using cameras and sensors to infer states inside tissues.
  • It shows the broader DDPS theme: when two processes are linked, watch the visible one to infer what the hidden one is doing.
  • It takes nonlinear waves and spreading patterns (reaction-diffusion dynamics) from abstract theory and applies them to a real clinical problem that could save lives.

Practical Caution

A model trained on ex vivo hearts may not work the same way on living patients in the clinic. Real hearts are different: tissue conditions vary from person to person, medical equipment and imaging quality differ, temperature and chemistry fluctuate, drugs and pacemakers interfere. These differences must be tested and validated carefully before using the model on patients.

Takeaway

When two physical processes are linked (like electrical waves creating mechanical motion), you can sometimes infer the hidden process from watching the visible one—if you have enough paired examples to learn the connection.
