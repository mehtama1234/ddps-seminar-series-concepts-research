Talk 72 · Northwestern University

Jian Cao: Physics-Based AI-Assisted Design And Control In Flexible Manufacturing

This lecture shows how physics-based and machine-learning methods together support design, sensing, prediction, and real-time control in flexible manufacturing processes such as metal additive manufacturing and rapid dieless forming.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Flexible manufacturing aims to produce three-dimensional parts without dedicated tooling for each shape, but the process physics is hard: material properties depend on long production histories, geometric features are complex, and the design and control space has many dimensions. Pure physics models can be too slow or incomplete, while purely machine-learning models may miss the actual mechanics and control constraints.

First-Principles Model

A manufacturing process connects design choices and control inputs through the physics of how material moves, solidifies, and hardens into microstructure, material state, shape, and mechanical behavior. Physics supplies conservation laws (energy and mass balance), mechanical principles, and what the process allows; machine learning supplies fast prediction, active sensing (choosing what to measure during production), and adaptive control. The control target is local and during production: measure what is happening right now as the part is being made and adjust inputs before flaws lock into the final part.

Key Claims

  • Flexible manufacturing improves when physics-based and machine-learning approaches work together.
  • Metal powder-based additive manufacturing and rapid dieless forming show production without dedicated tooling for each shape.
  • Basic process physics, process control, and machine learning can be combined to predict mechanical behavior during manufacturing.
  • Machine learning can guide active sensing—deciding what to measure—to enable effective real-time local process control.
  • Long history-dependent properties are a central difficulty in manufacturing prediction.
  • Complex geometries and high-dimensional design spaces make purely manual or exhaustive search approaches impractical.

Important Terms

Flexible Manufacturing

Manufacturing methods that can produce many different shapes or products without needing dedicated tooling for each design.

Additive Manufacturing

Building parts layer by layer, often using metal powder and laser heat in this talk's examples.

Rapid Dieless Forming

A forming process for three-dimensional parts that does not require a dedicated die or mold for each geometry.

Active Sensing

Using trained models and measurements to decide what process information to collect during production.

In-Situ Process Control

Adjustments made during manufacturing based on real-time measurements of the process as it happens.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk connects industrial digital twins to actual manufacturing control loops that act during production.
  • It also links to active learning and sensing because the model must choose what to measure during the process.
  • It complements materials microstructure talks by showing how production history shapes material behavior.

Practical Caution

A manufacturing machine-learning model that ignores production history can miss the real source of material properties. Control needs history-aware state estimation—tracking how material changed from its start—not just static prediction from geometry to property.

Takeaway

The first-principles lesson is that manufacturing with scientific machine learning is closed-loop process modeling. Prediction, sensing, and control must be learned by understanding the physics of how parts are made.
