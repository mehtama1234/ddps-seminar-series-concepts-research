Talk 45 · Stanford University

Lexing Ying: Prony's Method, Analytic Continuation, And Quantum Signal Processing

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of only hand-written rules. One hard version of this is: given only a few snapshots of a measurement over time, recover the hidden ripples and decay rates that created those snapshots, even when noise corrupts the data. Prony's method is an old shortcut that does exactly this by spotting patterns in equally spaced samples, and this talk shows how the same idea also helps in quantum computing and in extending functions beyond the region where we know them.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Many science and engineering questions boil down to: given a limited set of measurements, find the hidden frequencies and decay rates underneath. Why is this hard? Noise always creeps in, we can only sample so many times, and small mistakes in the data can explode into huge mistakes in the answer (a property called ill-conditioning). On top of this, physicists sometimes want to extend a function beyond the region where they measured it—another version of the same challenge. Quantum computing adds one more twist: to make quantum algorithms work, you need to factor certain mathematical expressions in a stable way, and that's actually the same problem in disguise.

First-Principles Model

Prony's method starts with a simple assumption: what you measured is just a sum of exponentials (signals that grow or decay at steady rates). If that assumption is correct, the frequencies and decay rates are locked into algebra that shows up in your equally spaced samples. The core insight is: instead of trying to guess the frequencies directly, convert the hard problem into spotting patterns, then solve for the frequencies from those patterns. This works because structure (exponentials have a very specific form) is easier to find than trying to fix something without knowing its shape.

Key Claims

  • Prony's method recovers the decay rates and ripple frequencies that are hidden in equally spaced time samples.
  • This method is old but too often forgotten, even though it solves hard problems simply.
  • Noisy measurements can be cleaned up and extended to regions you didn't measure using these same structure-spotting ideas.
  • Quantum computing needs the underlying algebra of Prony's method to reliably factor certain expressions.
  • The same mathematical thinking connects three different fields: old signal processing, extending functions into the complex plane, and quantum circuits.
  • Noise and measurement error are the real practical enemy, because the problem is sensitive—small changes to the data change the answer a lot.

Important Terms

Prony's Method

A recipe for identifying the decay rates and ripple frequencies in a signal that is a sum of exponentials, by spotting patterns in equally spaced samples.

Analytic Continuation

Extending a function beyond the region where you directly measured it, by using the relationships between the values you did measure. This is easy if data are clean but fragile if noise is present.

Quantum Signal Processing

A method for steering quantum computers by sending them carefully chosen phase shifts (twists in the quantum state) in sequence, similar to how you might amplify or suppress certain frequencies in a radio signal.

Frequency Recovery

Figuring out what ripple rates or decay rates are present in a measured signal—the inverse of building the signal from known ripples.

Ill-Conditioning

A situation where tiny mistakes in measurement lead to huge mistakes in the answer. Like trying to draw a line through two dots that are very close—small shifts in where the dots are move the line a lot.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk builds on the series' theme of recovering hidden information from incomplete measurements—that problem runs through inverse problems and imaging talks.
  • It connects to other talks about finding special frequencies and rates of change because all of them hunt for the 'skeleton' inside messy data.
  • It reminds us that not every solution to learning from data comes from neural networks; sometimes the oldest, simplest ideas from algebra and signal theory work best.

Practical Caution

Prony's method can give great answers if the real data match the exponential model and the noise is small. But if your actual signal is not a sum of exponentials, or if noise is too big, the method can make errors much larger instead of smaller—it amplifies the noise rather than filtering it out.

Takeaway

Good algorithms spot the structure hidden in your data before they start tweaking numbers. Prony's method works because it trades a hard, formless problem (find frequencies from samples) into a structured one (find parameters that fit a known pattern). The same pattern-spotting idea shows up in how physicists extend functions in the complex plane and how quantum computers process information.
