Talk 78 · University of California, Santa Barbara

Paul Atzberger: Generative Machine Learning For Data-Driven Modeling And Reductions

This whole series is about getting computers to predict physical behavior by learning from examples instead of only using hand-written math rules. This talk covers a special kind of learning program that builds simpler versions of complex systems (like soft materials or biological cells) that still capture how they actually move and change over time, including their random jiggling. It does this using two techniques: one that learns simpler versions while keeping the shape and smoothness of the real system intact, and another that learns systems with random parts by watching lots of examples and tuning two competing networks until they agree on how randomness works.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Simulating how soft materials, fluids, or living systems behave involves tracking millions of tiny pieces at many different sizes and speeds, plus random wiggling from heat and other noise. When we want a simpler version that runs faster, we face a hard choice: cut out details and lose important behavior, or keep the details and stay slow. The simpler version needs to remember which pieces stick together or follow conservation laws (like energy stays constant), and it needs to stay accurate when we step it forward many times — but most standard learning programs just compress data into hidden layers without guaranteeing these safety properties.

First-Principles Model

A generative reduction learns a simulator by watching real observations or detailed computer simulations and extracting the patterns that repeat. One approach uses a special type of learning program called a geometric variational autoencoder — which is like a compressor that learns to crunch the system down to its essentials while respecting the shape and curvature of the original (like learning a map that folds a crumpled fabric without tearing or distorting it). Another approach, called SDYN-GAN, learns systems with randomness by using two competing learning programs: one tries to generate realistic random sequences, the other tries to catch it in a mistake — they push each other until the generator learns how the randomness actually behaves. Both methods produce not just compressed snapshots but actual running simulators you can step forward through time.

Key Claims

  • Learning programs can build simpler versions and multiscale shortcuts of nonlinear scientific simulations by finding patterns in data or detailed runs.
  • Building the laws of physics and scientific knowledge directly into the learning process as hard rules changes what the program can learn.
  • Geometric variational autoencoders learn simpler versions that keep shape, connectivity, smoothness, and physical laws intact.
  • Geometric variational autoencoders can build simpler versions of high-dimensional time-changing systems and complex physics equations.
  • Two-network systems competing to model randomness can learn random dynamics from watching real stochastic systems run.
  • These two-network systems learn random dynamics as mathematical rules for change and use numerical methods that stay accurate over many steps forward.

Worked Example

Stochastic dynamical system: learning simultaneous stiffness (spring constant k), damping coefficient, and temperature from inertial Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process trajectories. Three-dimensional system achieving accurate parameter estimation; stiffness estimated within narrow range across varied temporal sampling (tau); damping and temperature parameters learned concurrently. Comparison: conditional training protocols with marginal distributions yield better accuracy than full trajectory approaches for parameter estimation.

One Level Deeper

M-step stochastic integrators with probabilistic graphical model structure where state at step j depends on previous M states. Maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) as discriminator loss: forms integral probability metric comparing empirical distribution of generator to training data distribution. Kernel-based critic with reproducing kernel Hilbert space; unbiased MMD estimator substitutes for discriminator optimization. Adjoint methods applied to stochastic trajectories to efficiently compute gradient contributions without explicit backpropagation through all trajectory steps. Integration by parts transfers derivatives from data to test functions in weak form.

Failure Boundary

Noise vs. learning signal tradeoff: temperature parameter very difficult to pin down (overfits by increasing noise amplitude to explain training data discrepancy); damping learning degrades at very small time scales where thermal fluctuations wash out stiffness sensitivity. High-dimensionality in probability spaces causes poor sample coverage in standard GAN formulation; mitigated by restricting to m-step graphical structure. Oscillations in training if using naive Jensen-Shannon divergence; MMD approach provides stabilization. Limited ability to learn truly novel Force laws without sufficient trajectory diversity to probe all parameter interactions.

Important Terms

Generative Model

A learning program that watches examples and learns to create new samples or time sequences that match what it saw, capturing the patterns that repeat.

Geometric Variational Autoencoder

A learning program with two parts: one crunches high-dimensional data into a simpler form, the other reconstructs it — but with built-in rules that preserve shape, connectivity, smoothness, and structure without tearing or skewing things like a careful map would.

Stochastic Dynamic GAN

Two learning programs that compete: one generates time sequences with randomness, the other judges whether they look real. They push each other until the generator learns the true randomness patterns.

Stochastic Differential Equation

A mathematical rule that says how a system changes at each instant when random noise is pushing it around; it has two parts: a smooth drift (like a current) and a random jiggle (like turbulence).

Multi-Step Stochastic Integrator

A numerical recipe for stepping a random system forward multiple time ticks while keeping the numbers from blowing up and losing accuracy, like steering a boat through waves without capsizing.

Connections Across The Series

  • This lecture brings together learning programs, methods for building simpler versions of systems, shrinking complexity across different scales, and learning the curved geometry of hidden data patterns.
  • It builds on earlier talks about information geometry and learning dynamics on curved spaces by asking: what structure should live in the simpler hidden version to make it work like a real simulator?
  • It connects to uncertainty and how wrong we might be, because random dynamics require probability-based simpler models, not just single-path predictions.

Practical Caution

A learned model can generate sequences that look right to the eye or pass statistical tests while secretly breaking the laws of physics (like energy vanishing, or heat flowing uphill). For science, the compressed version must have real physics built into its structure from the start, not just similarity on the surface.

Takeaway

The core lesson: generative learning programs for science should learn systems that actually run correctly, not just produce samples that look alike. The compressed version must be a real simulator.
