Talk 140 · Stanford University

Balint Kaszas: Invariant Manifold-Based Nonlinear Model Reduction for Fluid Dynamics

This whole series is about teaching computers to predict how physical things move by learning from data. This talk shows how to build smaller models of flowing fluids by finding the curved shapes hidden inside the motion — the invisible surfaces where the flow lives most of the time.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Fluid flows are too complicated to track directly. A computer trying to copy all the tiny details uses millions of numbers, and even then, linear shortcuts (where you treat the motion like a straight line on a graph) miss the real behavior: sudden jumps between different steady states, situations where multiple different motions exist at once, or wild chaotic spirals. A smarter model needs to find the hidden low-dimensional curved shapes that the flow actually hugs.

First-Principles Model

Mathematicians have a tool called dynamical-systems theory that studies how things change over time by looking at the geometric shapes inside the motion. An invariant manifold is a curved surface: if you imagine a solution (a single trajectory through the motion) landing on it at the start, that solution stays stuck to the surface forever. When you linearize (create a flat toy model by assuming very small motions near a fixed resting state), you get flat subspaces; the nonlinear version is curved versions of those same subspaces. When a high-dimensional fluid flow is pulled toward or locked onto such a curved surface, you can write down just the motion happening on that surface instead of tracking the full huge system. You end up with a small model that still captures sudden jumps, coexisting steady states, and chaos.

Key Claims

  • Model reduction for systems that do not move in straight lines can be built on invariant manifolds.
  • Spectral submanifolds are nonlinear curved versions of the flat subspaces you get from simple linear analysis.
  • These curved surfaces work as low-dimensional attractors that solutions drift toward.
  • Invariant manifold models capture the real behavior of fluid dynamics.
  • The approach handles sudden shifts between stable states and chaotic motion.
  • It has been tested on concrete flows between walls and on flows coupled with moving structures.

Worked Example

Couette flow (viscous flow between parallel plates moving at different speeds): full simulation has 10^5 spatial DOF and 10^3 timesteps per flow-through time. Spectral Subspace Manifold (SSM) reduces to 10-30 modes tracking the most-energetic scales while respecting nonlinear manifold geometry. Using SSM-ROM: 100x speedup over full solve, with 2-3% error on integral quantities (drag, heat transfer). Pipe flow (chaotic transient): SSM-ROM captures transition from laminar to turbulent in ~100 timesteps, predicting onset of instability within 5% of full DNS. For each case: rational function approximation of ROM nonlinearities (Padé approximant) converges in 3-5 iterations.

Channel flow at Re=180 (transition regime): SSM with 20 modes reproduces all dominant coherent structures (streaks, quasi-streamwise vortices) observed in full 3D simulation with only 20-50× fewer state variables.

One Level Deeper

Slow-Fast Decomposition: full system du/dt = f(u) splits into slow modes (energy-containing eddies, decay time ~L/V) and fast modes (dissipative scales, decay time ~ν/V²). SSM is invariant manifold tangent to slow eigenspace: W_s(u_s) ≈ u_s + g(u_s) where g captures nonlinear coupling back from fast to slow. Evolution on SSM: du_s/dt = f_s(u_s, g(u_s)). Galerkin projection onto k dominant POD modes V: û = V·a, dâ/dt = V^T·f(V·a). For nonlinear SSM, use Normal Form theory: transform coordinates so that resonances are eliminated up to order O(||a||^p), yielding simplified dynamics. Rational approximation: express g(a) ≈ P(a)/Q(a) (polynomial numerator/denominator), then solve for coefficients by matching derivatives at sample points.

Failure Boundary

Bifurcation crossing: SSM trained on one flow regime (e.g., laminar at Re=100) becomes invalid when transitioning to new regime (turbulent at Re=500); manifold topology changes, requiring recomputation. Transient growth: linear stability says mode is stable, but nonlinear transients can trigger transition via algebraic growth (non-normal modes) before eventual decay—SSM centered at fixed point misses this. High-dimensional chaos: fully turbulent flow (Re=10^4+) has exponentially many invariant manifolds; selecting one becomes arbitrary. Long-term drift: SSM ROM can artificially stabilize or destabilize attractor; backward integration (adjoint method) sometimes diverges before forward integration does. Parameterization dependence: choosing which modes to keep in SSM basis requires prior knowledge of which scales matter; wrong choice leads to stiff ROM equations or spurious dynamics.

Important Terms

Invariant Manifold

A curved geometric surface where any solution that starts on it remains on it as time moves forward.

Spectral Submanifold

A curved version of the flat subspace (the thin slice of directions you get from turning a complicated system into a simple linear one near a fixed resting point).

Nonlinear Model Reduction

Shrinking a large system down to fewer numbers by projecting motion onto a low-dimensional curved surface instead of a flat plane.

Attractor

A region where solutions gather and hover as time passes — the motion gets pulled into it.

Wall-Bounded Shear Flow

A fluid squeezed between walls or plates where the velocity changes as you move away from the walls (this is called shear), a standard test case for studying turbulent motion.

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk completes the arc on shrinking models by connecting curved shapes from geometry, time-evolution rules from dynamical systems, and fluid physics.
  • It builds on earlier talks about autoencoders (programs that find compact descriptions), manifold charting (methods to map curved surfaces), and projection (ways to squash high-dimensional data), by explaining the dynamical meaning of what the curved surface represents.
  • It also extends connections to Koopman analysis (a technique for reorganizing motion using spectral ideas) and mode decomposition (breaking motion into simpler vibrating patterns), since these methods relate through eigenvalues and spectral structure.

Practical Caution

These curved-surface models only work if the identified surface actually attracts the real fluid flow across the conditions you care about. If the real motion suddenly leaves the surface partway through, the small model will give wrong predictions.

Takeaway

Smaller models of flowing fluids should sit on the curved geometric shapes that organize the motion, not flat guesses.
