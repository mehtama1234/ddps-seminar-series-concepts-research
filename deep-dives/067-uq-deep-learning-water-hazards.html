Talk 67 · University of Manchester

Ajay Harish: Uncertainty Quantification And Deep Learning For Water-Hazard Prediction

This series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples. This talk focuses on one high-stakes prediction: when a hurricane or tsunami hits a coast, how much damage does it cause? We'll see why this is so hard—the water height isn't the only thing that matters—and how a learning program that also estimates its own uncertainty can help decision-makers prepare.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

Storm surges, tsunamis, and coastal floods push enormous forces on buildings, roads, and communities. How much damage occurs depends on far more than just the water level: it depends on uncertain starting conditions (how high is the ocean before the storm?), how waves bounce off structures, whether debris gets swept in, how sediment moves, what the building is made of, and how strong the structure actually is. To make real decisions about protection—where to build, what walls to reinforce, who evacuates—we need numbers for all these uncertainties and how they combine to produce damage.

First-Principles Model

A hazard prediction model takes uncertain inputs (initial surge height, building strength, sediment thickness) and produces uncertain damage outputs. We can use three tools together: Bayesian calibration, which means adjusting the model's guesses about unknown numbers by comparing predictions to real measurements; neural networks, which are fast stand-ins for slow, detailed simulations (a program with millions of little knobs it tunes by trial and error until its predictions look right); and uncertainty tracking (following how uncertainty in the inputs spreads through the model to affect the final answer). Together, these produce not just one damage prediction but a full range of possible outcomes—a picture of how likely each damage level is.

Key Claims

  • Storm surge is the rise of the ocean above its normal level, caused by wind and low air pressure from a storm.
  • When surge combines with normal waves and high tides, it can lift the average water level by several feet over a large region, flooding cities built near shore.
  • Predicting damage to buildings and infrastructure is harder than predicting water flow alone because buildings behave differently depending on materials, age, and how they were built.
  • Big sources of uncertainty include the initial water level before the storm, floating objects that get swept into buildings, how the seafloor shifts, and whether concrete or steel fails the way engineers expect.
  • A learning program trained on examples, plus methods for updating guesses with real data, can help us measure and predict extreme-event impacts with better precision.
  • To protect cities, you need damage estimates tied to specific buildings and roads, not just water-flow predictions for the whole region.

Important Terms

Storm Surge

The unusual rise in ocean level caused by storm winds pushing water toward shore and by low air pressure underneath the storm.

Water Hazard

A damaging event driven by water—such as storm surge, tsunami, coastal flooding, or a large wave hitting a structure.

Damage Assessment

An estimate of how much physical harm or money loss a hazard event will cause to buildings, roads, or other assets.

Bayesian Calibration

A method of adjusting the numbers inside a model by comparing what it predicts to real measurements, so the model's guesses about unknown values improve.

Urban Infrastructure Risk

The chance that buildings, roads, utilities, and other structures built near a coast will be harmed by a hazard event.

Connections Across The Series

  • This is an applied case study in uncertainty quantification that builds directly on earlier talks about how to track uncertainty as it flows through a system and how to monitor the health of structures.
  • It connects to digital twins—computer copies of real infrastructure—because good decisions about protecting cities require fast, calibrated models that run in real time to show different possible futures.
  • It also relies on surrogate modeling: simulating extreme events with many different conditions requires a fast replacement for slow, detailed simulations, and a learning program can provide that.

Practical Caution

A single-value flood map tells you 'water rises to 10 feet here' but hides the fact that it might actually be 8 or 12 feet depending on winds, starting conditions, or how you measured the building. Real decisions need the full picture: this damage might be 2 million dollars, but it could realistically be 1 million or 4 million depending on factors we don't know yet. Ignoring that uncertainty leads to under-prepared cities.

Takeaway

Hazard prediction is really uncertainty tracking that flows into decisions. A learning program helps when it can measure and communicate risk faster and more precisely than older methods.
