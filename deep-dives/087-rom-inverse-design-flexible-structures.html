Talk 87 · University of California, Los Angeles

Khalid Jawed: Reduced Order Modeling And Inverse Design Of Flexible Structures

This whole series is about getting computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of only hand-written math. This talk focuses on bendy structures like cables and springs that twist and stretch a lot. Instead of tracking every tiny part of the structure (which takes forever), a learning program can learn a shortcut using far fewer variables—then use that shortcut to design what shape the structure should be or how to actuate it to hit a target configuration.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

When a structure bends and deforms a lot, the math gets complicated because the geometry itself changes, and the material behavior (how it resists bending) changes too. Simulating the full structure precisely, tracking every piece, costs a lot of computing time. And when you want to invent a new shape—deciding what design choices or what forces to apply—you have to try many possibilities. Each try needs a full simulation, and there are too many combinations to test by hand.

First-Principles Model

A structure like a slinky has a shortcut: instead of tracking the whole twisting helix at every point, you can just track the position and twist of the helix's center line. A learning program trained on real simulations (precise 3D simulations of how a chain of rigid segments bends and stretches) can learn what forces show up at each segment in this reduced coordinate system. Then that learned force law lets you simulate the slinky's motion much faster. For a morphing structure (like a flat sheet that needs to fold into a 3D shape), a learning program learns to map a design choice—the pattern cut into the flat sheet—directly to what 3D shape it makes.

Key Claims

  • Structures that bend and stretch a lot need simulators that handle the changing geometry and changing material resistance.
  • Learning programs can cut down the number of variables and make simulation vastly faster.
  • A slinky's deformation often comes down to tracking just its center helix axis, not every piece of the metal.
  • Learning programs can store the effective forces in the reduced coordinate system.
  • Precise simulations of chains of rigid segments (called Discrete Elastic Rods) provide the training data.
  • Designing morphing structures—going from a flat pattern to a target 3D shape—becomes much faster when a learning program learns that mapping directly.

Worked Example

Take a Slinky-like helical rod. The precise way to simulate it is Discrete Elastic Rods (DER): chop the rod into a chain of short cylinders connected at nodes, and track the position of every node plus the twist of every segment. For a long helix that is a lot of degrees of freedom, and every timestep you must recompute the bending, stretching, and twisting forces on all of them.

Jawed's shortcut collapses the 3D rod onto its center line: instead of the full helix, you track only the center line as a 2D beam, described by a small set of constitutive parameters he calls theta (bending stiffness, twisting stiffness, damping, and so on). A neural network is trained on precise 3D DER runs to predict the reduced forces in these coordinates. He reports the reduced beam model runs roughly 50 to 60 times faster than the full 3D discrete elastic rod simulation.

Inverse design then becomes tractable: for a target shape (say a soft robot or a bacteria-inspired flagellum that must coil a certain way), you search over theta or over the actuation, running the fast reduced model thousands of times instead of the 3D model. Because each forward evaluation is ~50-60x cheaper, a design sweep that would take hours of full DER finishes in minutes.

One Level Deeper

The precise model is Discrete Elastic Rods: the rod energy is the sum of stretching, bending, and twisting terms, E = E_stretch + E_bend + E_twist, and forces are f = -∂E/∂x on each node x plus a twist degree of freedom per segment. Jawed reduces a 3D rod to a 2D beam center line, so the state shrinks from (node positions + per-segment twist for the whole helix) to a beam described by constitutive parameters theta.

The learned piece is a force law: a neural network maps the reduced beam configuration to the effective forces that would have appeared in the full 3D DER, so the reduced simulation reproduces the 3D motion without resolving the helix. To use precise time integration he splits the second-order equation of motion into two first-order equations (position rate and velocity rate), then steps them forward. The theta parameters are not always known for a real object, so he back-calculates them: run the fast model, compare to observed shape, and adjust theta to fit — an inverse fit that closes the loop before design search begins.

Inverse design minimizes a mismatch J(theta or actuation) = ||shape_reduced - shape_target||^2 over the design variables, using the cheap reduced model for each trial shape. Because the reduced forces are differentiable, gradients dJ/d(design) can be taken through the model to steer the search rather than sampling blindly.

Failure Boundary

The shortcut assumes the rod's behavior is well described by its center line and a fixed set of theta parameters. That breaks when the real deformation is not center-line-dominated — sharp local kinks, contact and self-collision between coils, or buckling where the helix snaps into a new shape. The reduced beam has no coordinate for those events, so it will predict a smooth shape while the true rod jumps, and the design search will converge to a target the physical structure never reaches.

The learned force law is also only trained over the range of 3D DER runs it saw. Push the design outside that range — much larger curvature, a different aspect ratio than the beam assumption (length no longer much greater than width), or strong material nonlinearity — and the network extrapolates. The practitioner sees the reduced model and a fresh full 3D DER check disagree, and the back-fit theta stops being stable across shapes, which is the signal that the center-line reduction no longer holds.

Important Terms

Highly Deformable Structure

A structure that bends and stretches so much that the shape changes dramatically, and the forces resisting that motion change as the shape changes.

Reduced-Order Representation

A compact description using far fewer numbers than tracking every piece; for a slinky, it might be just the center line instead of the full helix.

Neural ODE

A learning program that copies how something changes continuously over time; instead of breaking time into steps, it learns a smooth rule for how things flow.

Discrete Elastic Rods

A precise method that simulates thin elastic structures (cables, springs, rope, or helices) by treating them as chains of stiff segments connected by joints that resist bending.

Inverse Design

Working backward from a desired outcome to find what design choices or forces make it happen; instead of 'what shape does this design make,' it asks 'what design makes this shape.'

Connections Across The Series

  • This talk brings together reduced models (shortcuts for complex systems), learning programs that mimic differential equations (continuous-time rules), and inverse design (working backward from a goal) all in the real-world setting of bending and flexing structures.
  • It builds on manufacturing and materials research because how you cut and build a structure, what material you pick, and how it actually bends are all tangled together.
  • The best shortcuts come from understanding the physics first, not just trying to compress data—so good judgment about what matters saves a lot of work.

Practical Caution

A shortcut coordinate only works when the structure moves the way it did during training. If the structure bends, twists, folds, or collapses in a new way not seen in training, the learned force law will give wrong answers. You need to check that your training covered all the deformations that will actually happen.

Takeaway

Getting inverse design right for bendy structures depends on picking the right reduced coordinates—ones that capture how the structure actually deforms. Once you have the right coordinates, learning programs can speed up the simulation by thousands of times and search through designs quickly.
