Talk 8 · EPFL / Politecnico di Milano

Alfio Quarteroni: The Mathematical Heart

This whole series is about teaching computers to predict how physical things behave by learning from examples instead of only using hand-written rules. This talk focuses on the heart: an organ where electricity triggers muscle contraction, which squeezes and deforms tissue, which pushes blood around, with valves opening and closing, all while blood vessels feed the muscle itself. The challenge is that these five processes (electricity, mechanics, blood flow, valves, vessel perfusion) all happen at different speeds and scales, and they all affect each other—so a real heart model is not one simple rule, but five interconnected simulators (programs that step a physical situation forward in tiny increments) that must stay in sync.

YouTube source · Talk page

Core Problem

The heart is not one physics rule or one simulator. An electrical wave spreads through heart muscle and tells it to contract. That contraction deforms tissue, bending and stretching it. The bent tissue moves blood sitting in the heart chambers. Valves snap open and shut to guide that blood flow. At the same time, tiny blood vessels feed oxygen and nutrients back into the contracting muscle. Each of these five processes runs at a different speed (some fast, some slow), uses a different spatial scale (some tiny vessels, some whole chambers), and needs its own equations and numerical tricks to solve. Putting them together in one model means that electricity, mechanics, flow, valves, and perfusion all touch each other—change one, and all the others shift.

First-Principles Model

To build a heart simulator on a computer, start with the organ's actual shape from a CT scan or MRI. Chop that shape into millions of tiny boxes (a mesh—like graph paper wrapped around the heart). Then add five separate physics modules: (1) An electrical rule that spreads an activation wave through muscle, powered by ions flowing in and out of cells. (2) A mechanics rule that says muscle fibers contract when activated, pulling the tissue; the tissue also has stiffness and resists being bent (elasticity). (3) A fluid-dynamics rule that governs how blood flows inside the heart chambers, following the same principles that govern water rushing through pipes. (4) A valve rule that opens and closes at the right times to keep blood moving the right way. (5) A perfusion rule that tracks blood supply through large coronary arteries splitting into millions of capillaries feeding the muscle. Link all five together so that electrical activation triggers contraction, contraction deforms the chambers, deformation guides blood flow, flow pulls on the walls, and vessel pressure feeds back into the muscle.

Key Claims

  • Hand-written math rules can describe how electricity spreads through heart muscle, how muscle contracts and deforms tissue, how blood moves inside the heart and vessels, how valves open and close, and how blood feeds the muscle.
  • The heart is a coupled multiphysics problem (several distinct physical processes affecting each other) with multiscale behavior (processes happen at vastly different speeds and sizes).
  • The exact shape of a patient's heart can be traced from medical scans (CT or MRI), or approximated using statistical average shapes built from many healthy hearts, or hand-designed as a simplified CAD model.
  • Electricity and contraction may need different spatial grids (mesh divisions) than blood flow, because the detail needed is different in muscle tissue versus flowing liquid.
  • A complete heart model needs six components: electrical activation, active muscle contraction (the muscle's own pulling force), passive tissue stiffness (resistance to bending), blood flow in chambers and vessels, valve mechanics, and blood supply through coronary arteries.
  • Solving the full model on a computer is expensive, so scientists build simpler surrogate simulators (reduced-order models) for clinical studies and when uncertainty matters—these trade some accuracy for speed.

Worked Example

The team reconstructed the geometry of an actual human heart using CT/MRI scans and created a computational mesh with 60 million tetrahedral elements in the myocardium alone. This extremely fine discretization was necessary to accurately simulate electrical propagation. When solving the electrophysiology model, they used time steps of 0.01 milliseconds to capture the fast ionic dynamics. The complete heart model involves 4 different state variables and 16 parameters describing ion channel behavior.

For clinical arrhythmia detection, they simulated a heart with ventricular arrhythmia—an irregular electrical pattern—and the numerical model identified late activation zones and conduction block lines that corresponded to biomarkers surgeons needed to find. The prediction required solving 10 to the power 7 degrees of freedom, which took approximately 6 hours on a modest cluster with standard cores. After identifying these biomarkers, doctors could then perform ablation (burning specific points) to restore normal rhythm.

One Level Deeper

The electrical propagation in cardiac tissue is governed by the monodomain equation: a reaction-diffusion PDE where the transmembrane potential u(x,t) evolves under an elliptic component (divergence of conductivity tensor times gradient of u) coupled to a reaction term describing ionic activity. The conductivity tensor is anisotropic—it depends on the local fiber orientation—which means heart muscle conducts electricity much faster along fibers than perpendicular to them.

The mechanical deformation follows Lagrangian elastodynamics: the second time derivative of displacement equals the divergence of the Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor (which is the derivative of a strain energy function). The constitutive models used were hyperelastic (Guccione and Orthotropic energy functions), capturing both passive and active mechanics. The active force is generated when calcium binds to contractile proteins, and this calcium concentration comes from solving the ionic model. The problem is fully coupled: electrical activation drives mechanical contraction, deformation changes geometry (moving the domain), and the altered geometry feeds back into the electrical propagation.

Failure Boundary

The integrated heart model becomes computationally intractable for clinical use without severe simplifications. Even solving the full coupled system for a single heartbeat (about 1 second) takes on the order of 100 hours. Forward simulations are possible but clinical decision-making needs real-time or near-real-time answers. The model also requires extensive patient-specific data (fiber orientation, material properties, initial conditions) that are often unavailable or noisy from imaging. When fiber orientations are estimated from atlas or rule-based methods rather than measured, the electrical predictions become less accurate. Additionally, the model's high dimensional nonlinearity makes sensitivity analysis and uncertainty propagation extremely expensive—addressing any of these requires solving the model many times over.

Important Terms

Electrophysiology

The study of how electrical signals spread through heart tissue, triggering muscle cells to contract. Think of it like a wave of activation rippling across the heart, powered by charged atoms (ions) moving in and out of cells.

Active Mechanics

The actual pulling and pushing force that a muscle fiber generates in response to electrical activation. When a nerve signal arrives, the muscle fiber burns chemical energy and contracts—this is the active part.

Passive Mechanics

The stiffness and resistance of tissue when it is stretched or compressed by external forces (like blood pressure pushing against the heart wall). Every material resists being bent; this rule describes that resistance.

Fluid-Structure Interaction

The two-way coupling between a flowing liquid (blood) and the solid walls and valves it touches. The blood pushes on the tissue; the tissue moves and shapes the blood flow.

Multiscale Perfusion

The delivery of blood to tissue through vessels of many different sizes—wide coronary arteries branch into progressively smaller vessels down to capillaries the width of a single cell. Modeling this requires describing large vessels with one method and tiny vessels with another.

Connections Across The Series

  • The heart is the clearest example in this series of a real multiphysics simulator (many interacting physics pieces) where machine learning methods must respect all the coupled modules, not ignore them.
  • It ties to reduced-order models (ROM) because doctors need fast surrogates—simplified stand-in simulators that run in seconds instead of hours—to do patient studies and answer 'what if' questions about variations in a treatment.
  • It ties to differentiable simulation (the ability to run a simulator backward to ask 'what patient parameters would have caused these measurements?') and inverse problems because clinical diagnosis often means estimating patient-specific numbers from measured data, not just forward-predicting outcomes.

Practical Caution

Each of these five physics pieces can be solved correctly on its own—the math is sound, the computer code runs—but the model will give wrong answers if the handoff between them is wrong. Electrical activation and mechanical contraction must stay locked to each other with the right timing. The fluid pressure in the heart chambers must balance the pulling and pushing of the deformed tissue. Coronary vessel pressure must feed blood into tissue that is squeezing and releasing. The biggest difficulty is not solving one piece; it is making sure the time steps, boundary edges (fixed values at the borders), and feedback loops between all five pieces stay consistent. Miss this coupling, and a patient's prediction based on the model will be dangerously wrong.

Takeaway

This talk shows what a real first-principles scientific simulator looks like when it is built for a living system: not a single learned guessing program, but multiple interconnected physics solvers that must be tuned together. Any approach—whether hand-built math or machine learning or a hybrid—eventually must respect this coupled reality.
