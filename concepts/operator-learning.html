data-driven physical simulation

Operator Learning

Suppose you want to solve lots of slightly different physical problems — maybe heat spreading through bars of different shapes, or water flowing with different starting conditions. Instead of running a simulator each time, you train a program to learn the pattern: given one function (say, the starting temperature everywhere), spit out another function (the final temperature everywhere). That learned mapping between functions is an operator, and operator learning is the skill of training programs to capture these mappings fast.

Ordinary Problem

In the real world, engineers don't solve one physics problem and move on. They solve families of related problems: the same differential equation (a rule that says how something changes from moment to moment or place to place) but with different starting conditions, different shapes, different material properties, or different boundary conditions (the fixed facts at the edges and at the start). Brute-force simulators have to run all the way through each time.

Naive Picture

A first instinct: once you have enough examples of input-and-output pairs from a simulator, throw them at a neural network (a program with millions of little dials it tunes by trial and error until its answers look right), and it should learn to predict the next one instantly.

Why It Fails

The real world is picky. A simulator balances multiple rules at once — the physics equations, the starting facts, the size of the tiny steps it takes, how errors accumulate, whether the math problem is stable or wiggly. A program that looks correct on the training examples might break when you move even slightly outside them: a new shape it never saw, a longer time window, a new boundary condition, or a scenario that violates physical rules in subtle ways. A bare neural network has no idea these rules exist.

First-Principles Move

The right mental model: when you feed a function into the learned program and get a function out, you are not just predicting numbers — you are approximating an operator. An operator is a machine that takes one complete function (all the data, everywhere) and transforms it into another complete function. The program should learn to do this transformation in a way that respects the underlying physics, not just memorize examples.

What Breaks Without It

If you skip this idea and just throw data at a black box neural network, you'll build something that works on your test set but crumbles in real use. It might predict fine for a familiar shape but fail for a new one. It might track the first few seconds right and then drift wildly. It might learn a pattern that looks like the physics but violates conservation laws or other hard rules that the real world enforces. Years of engineering then go to waste.

Summit Role

This seminar series focuses on training programs to predict how physical things behave by learning from data. Operator learning is one of the central routes for doing this when your problem involves solving physics rules for many different starting points or parameters. Understanding operators helps us ask the right questions: what should a program actually learn, and how do we make sure it still respects the laws of physics?
