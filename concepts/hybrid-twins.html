Hybrid Twins

A hybrid twin is a digital copy of a real physical thing—a machine, a structure, the weather—built by combining two approaches: a hand-written step-by-step math program (the simulator) that predicts what happens next, and a learning program (neural network: millions of little dials the computer tunes by trial and error until its answers match reality) that watches the real system and corrects the simulator's mistakes. Fresh data from the real thing feeds in constantly, so the digital copy stays in step with what is actually happening.

Ordinary Problem

A pure simulator built from equations may miss forces or details that show up in the real world but were never written into the math. A pure data model—a learning program trained only on past examples—may give completely wrong answers once conditions change outside the range it saw during training.

Naive Picture

You might think: once a learning program has seen enough examples, it can just replace the simulator entirely. Throw away the equations, let the neural network handle it.

Why It Fails

Physical systems are tangled. They mix strict rules (conservation of energy), measured data (friction varies with temperature), numerical approximation tricks (how finely to chop space and time), hunts for steady states (does it settle or spiral?), and uncertainty about what we don't know yet. A learning program trained only to match past data will look good on those examples but will often break catastrophically when the real system meets new scenarios, extreme conditions, or corner cases—ways the system can behave that weren't in the training set.

First-Principles Move

A good digital twin starts by using the equations for structure (they encode what never changes), adds a learning program for correction (to catch what we got wrong or left out), and pulls in new measurements constantly so it stays tuned to reality. The program learns not from scratch but by learning only the corrections, the things the pure equations miss.

What Breaks Without It

Without combining these pieces, you build either a simulator that drifts away from reality, or a learner that works great on a test set but crashes when it meets something new. You lose the ability to trust your predictions, to know when something might go wrong, and to adapt when the real system changes.

Summit Role

This seminar series explores how to build and run systems that predict physical behavior. Hybrid twins are one important answer to the problem: how do you stay faithful to real physics while learning from data? This theme ties together talks about when to trust equations, when to trust data, and how to combine them.
