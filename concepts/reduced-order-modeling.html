data-driven physical simulation

Reduced-Order Modeling

Reduced-order modeling is the idea that when you are simulating something physical—how air flows, how heat spreads, how a bridge bends—most of the interesting behavior happens in a small set of 'directions' or 'patterns'. Instead of tracking every single detail (which takes forever), you track only those key patterns. A neural network (a program with millions of dials that learns by trial and error) can learn to predict what those patterns will do next, far faster than running the full simulation.

Ordinary Problem

Real simulations that predict physical behavior often need to solve thousands of equations at millions of points. Running these over and over—to test different designs, to find the best parameters, or to make decisions in real time—takes hours or days per run. In design, manufacturing, medicine, or robotics, waiting that long is not practical.

Naive Picture

It looks simple: collect a bunch of simulation results as examples, throw them at a learning program, and then use that program instead of running the slow simulator. The learning program can answer instantly, and the problem is solved.

Why It Fails

Simulation has hidden traps. The equations that describe physics have rules about conservation (energy, mass, momentum). The way you chop space into grid points affects what you see. Different starting conditions can lead to wildly different outcomes. A learning program trained only on surface agreement with examples will happily violate these hidden rules, and confidently give wrong answers when it sees conditions it has never encountered—different parameters, longer time, edge cases.

First-Principles Move

the main idea is that most physical systems have natural structure. Their motion does not spread evenly across all possible states; instead, it travels mostly along a thin set of 'paths' or 'modes'. You can think of it like a video: it contains millions of pixels, but most of the action is carried by a handful of objects moving across the frame. If you recognize those objects and their trajectories, you can skip the pixel-by-pixel details. For physics, if you identify the dominant modes, you can build a much smaller model that predicts how those modes evolve—and that smaller model trains faster and behaves more like the real physics.

What Breaks Without It

If you ignore this structure and just build a generic learning model, you end up with something that memorizes the training examples well but breaks as soon as the input drifts away from what it has seen: different material properties, longer simulations, rarer but important events. The model looks accurate in plots but fails in the real system, or in different scenarios, or when someone changes the problem slightly.

Summit Role

This seminar series asks: how can learning from data speed up or replace slow physics simulations? Reduced-order modeling is one answer: it finds and exploits the hidden structure in physical behavior so that a learning program can work on a small, fast model instead of a huge, slow one. This talk gives the ideas you need to recognize when and how this approach works.

Worked Example

Suppose you want to simulate a rocket injector combustor: full 3D incompressible flow with combustion over 1 second. The spatially discretized Navier-Stokes equations yield 100 million unknowns. Running a single simulation costs 1 million CPU-hours; design optimization requires hundreds of runs. A reduced-order model (ROM) first collects snapshots u₁, u₂, ..., u_m by solving the full model at key time points. Proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) finds the dominant modes: a matrix V ∈ ℝ^(100M × 50) whose 50 columns span the 'intrinsic' subspace where the solution lives. The ROM approximates u ≈ V·a(t), where a(t) ∈ ℝ⁵⁰ is the low-dimensional state. A neural network predicts da/dt from the current modes, and running the ROM costs ~0.1 seconds. Trade-off: the ROM is 10,000× faster but only accurate when the new scenario stays within the training manifold.

One Level Deeper

A ROM works by Galerkin projection: multiply the governing equation by V^T to project onto the span of basis vectors. If the full system is du/dt = f(u), then the ROM solves d/dt(V^T u) = V^T f(V a + u_ref), yielding k equations instead of n. POD extracts the basis V via singular value decomposition (SVD) of the snapshot matrix: [u₁ ... u_m] = U Σ V^T, and V retains the left singular vectors with σᵢ ≥ tol·σ₁. The challenge is that nonlinear terms in f(V a + u_ref) don't project cleanly—a term like u·∇u when restricted to the ROM becomes V^T (V a)·∇(V a), which is no longer a low-rank operation and requires additional closure modeling (like hyper-reduction via collateral POD or machine learning). The effective loss is ||du_ROM/dt - V^T f(V a)||² over training snapshots.

Failure Boundary

ROMs become unreliable when the new scenario drifts outside the convex hull of training conditions. Parameter variation (different Reynolds number, boundary conditions, geometry), longer time horizons, or rare but catastrophic events (flow separation, transition to turbulence) can trigger responses that lie far from the POD subspace. Because the modes are passive—they encode history, not causality—a ROM trained on laminar flow may predict negative pressure or non-physical oscillations when subjected to transitional conditions. Instability grows exponentially in time: small projection error at t=0 compounds via the Lyapunov exponent, and the ROM solution diverges within 10–100 time units. Furthermore, nonlinear coupling terms are not accurately captured by the low-rank projection, leading to energy leakage and spurious damping or growth.
