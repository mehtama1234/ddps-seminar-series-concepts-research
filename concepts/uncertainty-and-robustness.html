Uncertainty And Robustness

Uncertainty and robustness ask: does a learned simulation model stay trustworthy when faced with gaps in the data it trained on, new conditions it never saw, tiny rounding errors that pile up, or running it forward for many steps in a row?

Ordinary Problem

A surrogate model (a program trained to mimic a simulator) might get the average answer right but collapse exactly where decisions matter most—where scientists or engineers stake their conclusions.

Naive Picture

The simple idea: once you feed a neural network enough training examples, it can just take over the job of a physics simulator. Collect data, train the network, and you are done.

Why It Fails

Physics simulations involve many moving pieces: the equations themselves, the real measurements fed into training, the way you chop space and time into bits (discretization), whether the math stays stable over time, whether the model admits what it doesn't know, and whether the predictions actually hold up in a real experiment. A generic machine-learning scorecard (comparing guesses to examples) can look great while hiding failures that wreck the real system. The model might match training data perfectly but spiral into nonsense on a slightly different starting condition or after many timesteps.

First-Principles Move

A robust model must do three things: tell you how confident it is (what it knows with certainty and what is a guess), stay stable when you nudge the inputs slightly or run it longer, and be checked in exactly the spots where mistakes would flip a real decision—not just on average, but at the edges where science or engineering judgment lives.

What Breaks Without It

Skip this thinking and you end up with a model that plots beautifully against its training set but breaks the moment someone changes a parameter, runs the forecast much longer, or asks about a scientific quantity that depends on tiny details. The model becomes a false friend—looking reliable until it matters.

Summit Role

This theme ties together talks about knowing when a learned model is trustworthy, spotting where it fails, and building models that admit their own limits. It gives us a roadmap to decide which talks dig deeper into specific ideas.

Worked Example

A climate model surrogate trained on 1000 IPCC climate scenarios predicts temperature anomaly ΔT in 2100 with 2°C mean error. When tested on a new scenario (1.5°C warming target), it predicts ΔT ≈ 2.8°C with high confidence (99%). But ensembles of the full climate model show ΔT ∈ [2.2, 4.1]°C (huge variance). The surrogate is overconfident: it saw similar patterns in training, so it extrapolates smoothly, but the true system's sensitivity to carbon-cycle feedback (tipping point) is new. A robust surrogate quantifies uncertainty: it outputs ΔT = 2.8°C ± 1.2°C (predicted std), flagging the new scenario as out-of-distribution and reducing confidence. During training, it was taught not just to fit (Σ_i (y_i - ŷ_i)²) but also to predict uncertainty variance σ_i² that matches residual magnitude (Σ_i [(y_i - ŷ_i)²/σ_i² + log σ_i²]) and to stay stable under small input perturbations (adversarial robustness).

One Level Deeper

Uncertainty quantification embeds probabilistic reasoning into the surrogate. A Bayesian neural network outputs both mean μ(x) = f_θ(x) and variance σ²(x) via auxiliary network heads. The loss is the negative log-likelihood: L = Σ_i [(y_i - μ_i)²/σ_i² + log σ_i²], where the second term prevents the network from defaulting to high uncertainty to avoid penalty. Robustness to perturbation δx is measured via Lipschitz regularity: ||f(x+δx) - f(x)|| ≤ L·||δx||. This is enforced in the loss: L_robust = L_main + λ·max_{||δ||≤ε} ||f(x+δ) - f(x)||² or approximated via spectral normalization of network weights, which bounds gradient flow. For long rollouts, error accumulation is tracked: if one timestep has std σ_t, after T steps the bounds grow (optimistically) to √T·σ_t via random walk or (pessimistically) to T·σ_t via worst-case accumulation. The model flags when predicted variance exceeds tolerable bounds, signaling that the rollout is unreliable.

Failure Boundary

Uncertainty estimates fail when data is sparse in high dimensions. A model trained on 1000 examples in 100-dimensional input space is severely underdetermined; its predicted uncertainties are likely miscalibrated (underestimates variance in the tail), leading to false confidence on truly novel inputs. Spectral bias in neural networks means the model learns low-frequency patterns first and high-frequency details late; uncertainty estimates may not account for residual high-frequency error that grows with time. In sequential prediction, uncertainty must be updated after each step, but neural networks are not truly Bayesian and don't properly account for prior information; the estimated posterior can drift from the true posterior, causing underestimated uncertainty that compounds over long rollouts. For systems with multiple stable states or bifurcations, the model's point estimate (mean) may predict state A with confidence, but the true system is in state B; the uncertainty estimate doesn't capture mode misidentification. If the training data contains systematic bias (e.g., all measurements from one instrument with a 5% calibration error), the model learns that bias and has artificially low residuals, so predicted uncertainty underestimates total error by a factor of 2–5. In adversarially robust training, the learned model becomes overly conservative and loses predictive accuracy on the clean distribution.
