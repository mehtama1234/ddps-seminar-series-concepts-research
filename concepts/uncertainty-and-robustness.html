Uncertainty And Robustness

Uncertainty and robustness ask: does a learned simulation model stay trustworthy when faced with gaps in the data it trained on, new conditions it never saw, tiny rounding errors that pile up, or running it forward for many steps in a row?

Ordinary Problem

A surrogate model (a program trained to mimic a simulator) might get the average answer right but collapse exactly where decisions matter most—where scientists or engineers stake their conclusions.

Naive Picture

The simple idea: once you feed a neural network enough training examples, it can just take over the job of a physics simulator. Collect data, train the network, and you are done.

Why It Fails

Physics simulations involve many moving pieces: the equations themselves, the real measurements fed into training, the way you chop space and time into bits (discretization), whether the math stays stable over time, whether the model admits what it doesn't know, and whether the predictions actually hold up in a real experiment. A generic machine-learning scorecard (comparing guesses to examples) can look great while hiding failures that wreck the real system. The model might match training data perfectly but spiral into nonsense on a slightly different starting condition or after many timesteps.

First-Principles Move

A robust model must do three things: tell you how confident it is (what it knows with certainty and what is a guess), stay stable when you nudge the inputs slightly or run it longer, and be checked in exactly the spots where mistakes would flip a real decision—not just on average, but at the edges where science or engineering judgment lives.

What Breaks Without It

Skip this thinking and you end up with a model that plots beautifully against its training set but breaks the moment someone changes a parameter, runs the forecast much longer, or asks about a scientific quantity that depends on tiny details. The model becomes a false friend—looking reliable until it matters.

Summit Role

This theme ties together talks about knowing when a learned model is trustworthy, spotting where it fails, and building models that admit their own limits. It gives us a roadmap to decide which talks dig deeper into specific ideas.
